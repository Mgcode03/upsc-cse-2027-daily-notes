# BLOCK A — Why Fundamental Rights exist (25 minutes)

A written constitution without enforceable rights is a sermon. Part III (Articles 12–35) is the enforceable core. Article 32 is the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement. Ambedkar called Article 32 the heart and soul of the Constitution. Without a remedy, a right is literature.

Three ideas to keep separate:

1. **Fundamental Rights** bind the State (and in a few cases private persons). They are justiciable.
2. **Directive Principles** (Part IV) guide the State. They are not courts’ first weapon, but they colour how rights are read.
3. **Fundamental Duties** (Part IVA) bind citizens. They are not generally enforceable by writ in the same way.

Rights are not unlimited. Most of Articles 19–22 carry explicit restrictions. Articles 14–18 look absolute on the page; the courts built the doctrine of reasonable classification so that welfare and reservation do not collapse equality.

Emergency footnote for later days: Article 359 lets the President suspend the right to move court for enforcement of specified rights during a Proclamation of Emergency, except Articles 20 and 21 after the 44th Amendment. Do not mix this with Article 358, which deals with Article 19 automatically during a war or external aggression emergency.

Today’s slice is Articles 12 to 18: definition of State, laws inconsistent with rights, and the equality cluster.

# BLOCK B — Articles 12 and 13: who is bound, what dies (50 minutes)

## Article 12 — “the State”

Unless the context otherwise requires, “the State” includes:

- the Government and Parliament of India;
- the Government and the Legislature of each State;
- all local or other authorities within the territory of India;
- all local or other authorities under the control of the Government of India.

Why this list matters: a writ under Article 32 or 226 lies against the State. If a body is not “State”, Part III generally does not bind it in the same way (private discrimination has other statutes: the Protection of Civil Rights Act, the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, labour codes, and so on).

**Local authorities.** Municipalities, panchayats, improvement trusts, port trusts — bodies of local self-government.

**Other authorities.** This is the litigated phrase. The courts ask functional questions, not the name on the letterhead:

- Is there deep and pervasive financial, administrative or functional control by government?
- Is the body performing a public function of a governmental character?
- Was it created by statute? (Helpful, not conclusive.)
- Does it enjoy monopoly or State-conferred power?

Illustrative outcomes you must own as principles, not as a shopping list of case names only:

- Statutory corporations and many public sector undertakings have been treated as State when control is real.
- A government company can be State if the tests are met.
- A purely private college or private company, without public function and without pervasive control, is usually not Article 12 State. Affiliation to a university or receipt of some grant does not automatically convert it.
- The Board of Control for Cricket in India has been a long argument about public function versus private association. Remember the *test*, not a slogan.
- Judiciary acting in its judicial capacity is not “State” for the purpose of a fundamental-rights complaint against a judgment the way an executive order is; administrative acts of courts can attract a different analysis.

Article 12 is the gate. If the gate is shut, Part III does not open.

## Article 13 — inconsistent laws

Article 13 does four jobs.

**13(1).** Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with Part III are void to the extent of inconsistency. They do not vanish from the statute book by magic; they become unenforceable against fundamental rights. The doctrine of eclipse: a pre-constitutional law inconsistent with a fundamental right is overshadowed. If the right is later amended or the inconsistency is removed, the law can revive without re-enactment. This doctrine was developed for pre-constitutional law.

**13(2).** The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges rights conferred by Part III. A post-constitutional law that does so is void to the extent of the contravention. Eclipse does not rescue a law born void against Part III in the same simple way.

**13(3).** “Law” includes ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law. This is why an executive notification can be struck down as easily as an Act.

**13(4).** Nothing in Article 13 applies to a constitutional amendment made under Article 368. This clause was inserted by the 24th Amendment (1971) after *Golaknath* (1967) had said Parliament could not amend Part III. *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) accepted that Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights, but not destroy basic structure. So: Article 13(4) means an amendment is not “law” for Article 13; basic structure is a different, judicially declared limit.

**Waiver.** A person cannot waive a Fundamental Right in the sense of bargaining it away against the State as if it were a private contract. Rights are a matter of public policy.

**Territorial and personal reach, first cut.** Part III binds the Indian State. Some rights are for “citizens” only (Article 15, 16, 19, 29). Some are for “persons” (Article 14, 20, 21, 22, 25). Foreigners therefore get equality before law and life-liberty protection, not the six freedoms of Article 19. File that distinction; you will meet it again tomorrow.

# BLOCK C — Articles 14 to 18: the equality code (80 minutes)

## Article 14 — equality before law and equal protection of the laws

The text has two limbs, historically from two traditions:

- **Equality before the law** — English rule-of-law idea: no person is above the law; the State has no inherent privilege to be arbitrary.
- **Equal protection of the laws** — American idea: likes must be treated alike; the law must give equal protection in equal circumstances.

In Indian practice the two limbs have fused into one command: **the State shall not act arbitrarily.** Classification is allowed. Arbitrariness is not.

### Reasonable classification

The State may treat different groups differently if two conditions both hold:

1. **Intelligible differentia.** The grouping must be based on a real and understandable distinction (income slab, nature of offence, educational backwardness, source of recruitment).
2. **Rational nexus.** That distinction must have a reasonable connection with the object of the law.

Both are required. A neat group with no link to the object fails. A noble object with a whimsical group fails.

What fails the test: class legislation that singles out a person or a closed group with no public reason; a provision that uses a differentia unrelated to the goal (punishing left-handed people to improve traffic safety); a procedure that is a pretence.

What survives: progressive tax slabs; special courts for specified crimes if the specification is reasoned; different retirement ages if the nature of service differs; separate regulation of hazardous industry.

### From classification to arbitrariness

*E. P. Royappa* and then *Maneka Gandhi* taught that equality is antithetic to arbitrariness. Even a law that can be dressed as a classification can fall if the action is capricious. Article 14 thus polices not only the statute but the manner of State action: tenders, natural justice in many civil consequences, reasoned orders, non-discriminatory allocation of scarce State largesse.

### Equals and unequals

Equals must be treated equally. Unequals may — and sometimes must — be treated unequally if the object is to produce real equality. This is the constitutional hook for reservations, special maternity benefits, and progressive taxation. Article 14 is not a demand that a landless labourer and a billionaire pay the same tax.

### Exceptions you will meet in Prelims language

- Special provisions for particular classes appear in Articles 15 and 16 themselves.
- The President, Governors, foreign sovereigns and diplomats, and some MPs/MLAs have constitutional immunities of speech and vote. These are status immunities, not a general licence to break ordinary criminal law.
- Article 31C (studied with DPSPs later) gives certain DPSP-implementing laws a shield; after *Kesavananda* the shield is read in a limited way.

Lock one sentence: Article 14 forbids class legislation, not reasonable classification; it forbids arbitrariness, not difference.

## Article 15 — prohibition of discrimination

**15(1).** The State shall not discriminate against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth or any of them.

The word **only** is a classic trap. If the State uses one of these grounds *plus* another legitimate ground, the analysis is more careful; you cannot mechanically save a caste-based exclusion by adding a decorative extra label. The spirit of the clause is that these six markers are suspect classifications for State hostility.

**15(2).** No citizen shall be subject to any disability, liability, restriction or condition with regard to access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment; or the use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained wholly or partly out of State funds or dedicated to the use of the general public, on the same six grounds. This clause reaches **private persons** who keep such places. It is one of the rare horizontal rights in Part III.

**15(3).** The State may make special provision for women and children. This is how maternity benefits, women-only compartments, and child-specific criminal protections survive an Article 15(1) challenge.

**15(4).** Inserted by the 1st Amendment (1951) after *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan*. The State may make special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes. This is the educational-reservation clause.

**15(5).** Inserted by the 93rd Amendment (2005). Special provision, by law, for the advancement of SEBCs, SCs and STs relating to admission to educational institutions, including private institutions, whether aided or unaided, **other than minority educational institutions** under Article 30(1). This is how private unaided non-minority colleges can be brought under reservation by statute.

**15(6).** Inserted by the 103rd Amendment (2019). Special provision for the advancement of economically weaker sections (EWS) other than the classes mentioned in 15(4) and 15(5), including reservation up to 10 percent in educational institutions (again except minority institutions under 30(1)), in addition to existing reservations. *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022) upheld this amendment as not violating basic structure, including the idea that economic criteria can identify a separate beneficiary class and that 50 percent is not an iron wall in every situation.

Place of birth versus place of residence: Article 15 bars discrimination on place of **birth**. Residence requirements for some benefits are a different, narrower debate under Articles 14, 15 and 16 and under domicile rules. Do not collapse birth and residence.

## Article 16 — equality of opportunity in public employment

**16(1).** Equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment or appointment to any office under the State.

**16(2).** No citizen shall, on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence or any of them, be ineligible for or discriminated against in respect of any such employment. Note the extra grounds compared with Article 15: **descent** and **residence**.

**16(3).** Parliament may make a law prescribing residence inside a State or Union territory as a requirement for specified classes of employment under that State or UT. Only Parliament, not a State legislature. This is why a State cannot casually lock all its jobs to “sons of the soil” by its own Act unless a parliamentary law authorises a residence filter for specified posts.

**16(4).** The State may reserve appointments or posts for any **backward class of citizens** which, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in the services under the State. This is the employment-reservation clause. “Backward class” here is the constitutional phrase; Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes are included in practice through this and allied provisions; OBC lists are maintained separately for Centre and States.

**16(4A).** Inserted by the 77th Amendment (1995). Reservation in **promotion** for SCs and STs if they are not adequately represented. Later, the 85th Amendment (2001) allowed **consequential seniority** with such promotion. The Supreme Court in *M. Nagaraj* (2006) said the State must show: compelling reasons (backwardness, inadequacy of representation), and that efficiency under Article 335 is not wiped out. *Jarnail Singh* (2018) dropped the requirement to prove backwardness again for SCs/STs as a class, but kept inadequacy and efficiency. *Quantifiable data* remains the administrative homework.

**16(4B).** 81st Amendment (2000). Carry-forward of unfilled reserved vacancies as a separate class, so that the 50 percent ceiling in a single year is not broken by adding backlog in a clumsy way. Backlog is treated as a distinct bucket.

**16(5).** A law may require that the incumbent of an office connected with the affairs of any religious or denominational institution, or a member of its governing body, shall belong to that religion or denomination. Temple administration posts of a religious character can be reserved to the denomination.

**16(6).** 103rd Amendment. Up to 10 percent reservation for EWS in public employment, in addition to existing reservations.

### The 50 percent idea and the creamy layer

*Indra Sawhney* (1992), nine judges:

- Reservation under 16(4) is confined to **backward classes**, identified by social and educational backwardness; caste can be a starting point but is not the only point.
- Total reservation should, as a rule, not exceed **50 percent**, save extraordinary situations.
- Reservation in **promotion** was not available under 16(4) as then read — which is why 16(4A) was inserted later.
- The **creamy layer** among OBCs must be excluded. Income plus status tests apply to OBCs. For SCs and STs, creamy-layer exclusion has been a later, contested judicial conversation; do not treat OBC creamy layer and SC/ST creamy layer as the same settled rule.
- A vacancy-based roster and the distinction between cadre strength and year-wise appointments matter in implementation.

Article 335: claims of SCs and STs shall be taken into consideration, consistently with the maintenance of efficiency of administration. Efficiency is a constitutional word, not a political slogan. It cannot erase reservation; reservation cannot erase it.

## Article 17 — abolition of untouchability

“Untouchability” is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden. The enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law.

This right is available against **private persons** as well as the State. It is another horizontal right.

Parliament enacted the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (originally the Untouchability (Offences) Act). The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 addresses a wider set of atrocities, not only the old civil-disabilities list. Article 17 is the constitutional parent; the statutes are the teeth.

The Constitution does not define “untouchability”. Courts read it as the historical caste-based practice of exclusion, not every social snub or every form of social boycott. A private refusal that has no caste-untouchability character may still be illegal under other laws, but it is not automatically Article 17.

## Article 18 — abolition of titles

**18(1).** No title, not being a military or academic distinction, shall be conferred by the State.

**18(2).** No citizen of India shall accept any title from any foreign State.

**18(3).** No person who is not a citizen, while he holds any office of profit or trust under the State, shall accept a title from a foreign State without the consent of the President.

**18(4).** No person holding any office of profit or trust under the State shall, without the consent of the President, accept any present, emolument or office of any kind from or under any foreign State.

Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan and Padma Shri are **not titles** in the Article 18 sense. They are national awards. They cannot be used as prefixes or suffixes to a name as a title (the official instruction is that they are not to be prefixed). *Balaji Raghavan* accepted the awards so long as they do not become a new nobility.

Hereditary titles of the princely order were a target of this article. Knighthoods and similar foreign honours need presidential consent if the person is a citizen or an office-holder as the clauses require.

Military decorations (Param Vir Chakra and others) and academic degrees (Doctor, Professor as earned distinctions) are expressly carved out of 18(1).

# BLOCK D — How to write this in Mains without dumping articles (15 minutes)

A GS-II paragraph on reservation should not list every clause. A usable core:

> Article 14 permits reasonable classification and forbids arbitrariness. Articles 15(4), 15(5), 15(6), 16(4), 16(4A) and 16(6) are the express special-provision clauses. The judicial settlement since *Indra Sawhney* is that reservation is a tool of representation and equality of opportunity, bounded by adequacy of representation, exclusion of the OBC creamy layer, regard for efficiency under Article 335, and a general 50 percent ceiling that the 103rd Amendment treated as not impregnable for a separate EWS class. The live governance problem is data: a Commission, an updated list, and cadre-wise numbers — not a slogan for or against reservation.

An Ethics (GS-IV) hinge from the same material: a District Magistrate who treats a sanitation worker as socially invisible has not merely been rude; she has failed the constitutional value that Article 17 made a public crime and that fraternity in the Preamble made a political duty.

# BLOCK E — Indian monsoon: mechanism, branches, breaks, retreat (70 minutes)

The monsoon is not “it rains in India in summer.” It is a seasonal reversal of wind tied to differential heating, the position of the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), the Himalaya-Tibet heat engine, the moisture of the Indian Ocean, and the wander of a trough over the plains.

## The seasonal reversal

In winter, high pressure sits over the cold Asian landmass. Winds blow out from the north-west towards the Indian Ocean. These are the **northeast monsoon / retreating monsoon winds** — dry over most of India because they come from land, but they pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal and rain on the **Coromandel Coast** (Tamil Nadu) in October–December.

In summer, the land heats. A heat low develops over north-west India and Pakistan. The ITCZ, the equatorial belt of rising air, moves north over the plains. Moisture-laden winds from the southern Indian Ocean cross the equator, turn right under Coriolis in the northern hemisphere, and arrive as the **southwest monsoon**.

Cross-equatorial flow is not a minor detail. The southern-hemisphere southeast trades, after crossing, become the southwest monsoon current.

## Why the burst is sudden

By late May the heat low deepens. The Tibetan Plateau, a high-altitude heat source, builds an upper-air anticyclone. Easterly **tropical easterly jet** winds appear in the upper troposphere south of the Himalaya. When the lower-level moisture current and the upper-level pattern lock, the monsoon **onset** is declared over Kerala. The conventional normal date for onset over Kerala is **1 June**. It reaches the interior and the northern plains over the next weeks; Delhi’s normal onset is around the end of June.

Onset is a definition based on rainfall, wind and outgoing longwave radiation criteria used by the India Meteorological Department, not a single thunderclap.

## Two branches after the Arabian Sea and the Bay

The southwest current splits.

**Arabian Sea branch.** Strikes the Western Ghats. Windward slopes of the Ghats receive very heavy orographic rain (Agumbe, Cherrapunji is not this branch — see Bay branch). The leeward Deccan sits in a **rain shadow**. One arm of the Arabian Sea branch also moves into Saurashtra–Kachchh and towards Rajasthan; the Thar still remains relatively dry because of distance from moisture, subsidence, and the geometry of the trough.

**Bay of Bengal branch.** Moves towards Myanmar and then is steered by the Himalayan barrier and the trough towards the northeast and then west along the Gangetic plain. Mawsynram and Cherrapunji on the southern slope of the Khasi Hills receive extreme orographic rain from Bay moisture forced up a funnel-shaped valley.

The two branches meet over the Gangetic plain. The **monsoon trough** (a low-pressure line) oscillates. When the trough sits over the Ganga plain, the plains get rain and the Himalayan foothills are relatively quieter. When the trough shifts to the foothills, the plains go dry — a **break monsoon** — and the hills and the Brahmaputra valley can flood.

## Breaks, depressions, and the 100-day season

Most of India’s annual rain falls in about 100 days, and not on all 100. A large share arrives in spells associated with **monsoon depressions** that form over the Bay of Bengal and travel west-north-west across Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan.

A **break** is not the end of the monsoon. It is a shift of the trough. Farmers experience it as a dry week in the middle of kharif. Too long a break burns the standing crop. Too many depressions flood it.

Western disturbances in winter are a different system (extratropical, Mediterranean origin). Do not call winter rain in Punjab a monsoon rain.

## Retreat

From September the land cools relative to the sea. The ITCZ slides south. Withdrawal starts from the north-west (Rajasthan) in early September and reaches the southern peninsula later. Retreat is a north-to-south story; onset was a south-to-north and sea-to-land story.

October–November: the **retreating monsoon** plus cyclones in the Bay. Andhra-Tamil Nadu coasts are cyclone-prone in this window. Tamil Nadu’s main rainy season is this retreating / northeast monsoon, which is why a weak southwest monsoon year can still leave the far south less damaged than Maharashtra.

## El Niño, La Niña, IOD — the teleconnections

**El Niño:** warming of the central and eastern equatorial Pacific. Often associated with weaker Indian summer monsoon, but not as a one-to-one law. Several El Niño years have had near-normal rain when other drivers compensated.

**La Niña:** cooler eastern Pacific. Often associated with a stronger monsoon, again not a guarantee.

**Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD):** a temperature difference between the western and eastern tropical Indian Ocean. A positive IOD (west warm, east cool) tends to favour monsoon rainfall over India and can offset a moderate El Niño.

IMD’s operational forecasts combine these with Eurasian snow, the heat low, and statistical-dynamical models. For Prelims, know the direction of the association and the word “tendency,” not a fake certainty.

## What the map must look like in your head

- Western Ghats windward: heavy summer rain.
- Interior Deccan: moderate, rain-shadow pockets.
- Rajasthan west: scanty.
- Northeast and Konkan-Malabar: very heavy.
- Tamil Nadu: relatively dry in southwest season, wet in Oct–Dec.
- Kashmir: not a classic southwest-monsoon province in the same way; western disturbances matter more for winter snow.

Rainfall is orographic, cyclonic-depression, and convection over a heated land — three mechanisms, not one.

# BLOCK F — Paired block: farm calendar, heat, and an ethics cut (25 minutes)

## Economy: why the monsoon is a GDP sentence

Kharif sowing (rice, cotton, soybean, groundnut, maize, pulses in many belts) waits on the onset. A two-week delay in Maharashtra or Telangana changes area sown. Reservoirs behind the Western Ghats and in the peninsula fill in July–September; a weak monsoon is next summer’s urban water crisis and next year’s hydro generation shortfall.

Food inflation in India still has a monsoon coefficient. Even with buffer stocks and imports, vegetable and pulse prices move with local rain. Rural demand for two-wheelers and FMCG follows agricultural cashflow with a lag. This is why the Monetary Policy Committee still reads the IMD outlook.

Rabi (wheat, mustard, gram) lives on residual moisture, irrigation and winter western disturbances, not on July rain directly — but reservoirs and groundwater recharge are the hidden monsoon inheritance of rabi.

## Current affairs explained, tied to today

When a June headline says “monsoon onset over Kerala delayed by a week,” translate it:

- onset line is an IMD operational definition;
- one week at Kerala does not automatically mean a season-long drought;
- the relevant questions are: progress along the west coast, Bay depression frequency, position of the trough, and El Niño / IOD state.

When a court or a government order speaks of “EWS 10 percent” or a State OBC list, translate it to Articles 15(6) and 16(6) plus the State’s own commission data. The static hook is always the clause and the classification test, not the party that issued the order.

When a heatwave kills outdoor workers in May before onset, the geography hook is the pre-monsoon heat low itself; the polity hook is Article 21 (tomorrow’s family of rights) and labour codes; the ethics hook is whether the administration treats heat as a predictable annual disaster or as weather gossip.

## Ethics cut

Equality in Article 14 is a standard for a civil servant writing a tender or a transfer list: give reasons, use a rule you would be willing to publish, do not invent a class to reward a friend. Article 17 is a standard for how the office peon is spoken to. These are not decoration. They are the job description.

# BLOCK G — CSAT: percentages, ratios, and a short passage (40 minutes)

Work every question on paper. Then read the solution.

### Q1. Mixture

A vessel has 40 litres of milk. 8 litres are removed and replaced with water. The same operation is done once more. Milk now left?

**Solution.** After each operation the fraction of milk remaining is (1 − 8/40) = 4/5 of what was present just before.
Two operations: 40 × (4/5) × (4/5) = 40 × 16/25 = 25.6 litres.

Formula: amount of original left = x (1 − y/x)^n.

### Q2. Election

In an election, 8% of voters did not vote. Winner got 48% of the **electoral roll** and won by 1100 votes against the only opponent. Electors on the roll?

**Solution.** Votes polled = 92% of roll.
Winner = 48% of roll.
Loser = 92% − 48% = 44% of roll.
Margin = 4% of roll = 1100.
Roll = 1100 × 100/4 = 27,500.

Trap: 48% is of the roll, not of votes polled. Always name the base.

### Q3. Ratio chain

A : B = 3 : 4, B : C = 8 : 9, C : D = 2 : 3. A : D ?

**Solution.** Make B common between first two: A:B = 6:8, B:C = 8:9 → A:B:C = 6:8:9.
C:D = 2:3 = 9:13.5. Avoid halves: C:D = 2:3, scale A:B:C by 2 → 12:16:18, then D = 18 × 3/2 = 27.
A:D = 12:27 = 4:9.

### Q4. Successive change

A price rises 25% and then 20%. To return to the old price, the new price must fall by what percent?

**Solution.** Multiplier = 1.25 × 1.20 = 1.50. New price is 150% of old.
Required multiplier back = 1/1.5 = 2/3, i.e. a fall of 1/3 = 33⅓%.

Not 45%. Successive percentages never add when the base changes.

### Q5. Time and work

A does a job in 10 days, B in 15. They work together 2 days. Then A leaves. Days B needs to finish?

**Solution.** A = 1/10 per day, B = 1/15. Together = 1/6 per day.
Two days: 1/3 done. Left = 2/3.
B alone: (2/3) ÷ (1/15) = 10 days.

### Q6. Comprehension

Read: “Article 14 does not require the State to treat a professor and a mine worker as if they were the same person. It requires that the reason for difference be public, relevant and consistently applied. A rule that looks even on paper but is used only against one community is not equality; it is costume.”

Q6a. The author’s main claim is that equality is (A) sameness of treatment in all cases (B) a ban on all classification (C) non-arbitrary and consistently applied difference (D) preference for workers over professors.

Answer: **C**.

Q6b. Assumption: a law can be textually neutral and still violate equality in application. True; that is the costume sentence.

Q6c. Inference not supported: that professors should pay less tax. The passage gives no tax rule.

# BLOCK H — Active recall with model cores (25 minutes)

1. What four kinds of body sit inside Article 12?
   **Core:** Union government and Parliament; State government and legislature; local authorities; other authorities in India or under Union control.

2. What did the 24th Amendment do to Article 13?
   **Core:** Inserted 13(4): Article 13 does not apply to Article 368 amendments.

3. Two tests of reasonable classification?
   **Core:** Intelligible differentia, and rational nexus with the object of the law.

4. Which Article 15 clause is horizontal (binds private keepers of public places)?
   **Core:** 15(2).

5. Who alone may prescribe a residence requirement for State employment under 16(3)?
   **Core:** Parliament.

6. Which amendment inserted promotion reservation for SCs/STs?
   **Core:** 77th Amendment, Article 16(4A).

7. Is Bharat Ratna a “title” under Article 18?
   **Core:** No. It is a national award, not a title of nobility.

8. Normal IMD onset date over Kerala?
   **Core:** 1 June (normal date).

9. Why is Tamil Nadu wet in October–December and relatively dry in the southwest season?
   **Core:** Southwest current gives it a rain-shadow / rain-deficit position; the northeast / retreating monsoon and Bay moisture give the Coromandel its main rain.

10. What is a break monsoon?
    **Core:** The monsoon trough shifts to the Himalayan foothills; the plains go dry while the hills may flood. The season has not ended.

11. Positive IOD tends to do what to Indian summer rain?
    **Core:** Favour it; can offset a moderate El Niño.

12. +25% then +20% needs what percent cut to reverse?
    **Core:** 33⅓%.
