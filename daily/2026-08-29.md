# BLOCK A — How to read Articles 19 to 22 as one family (20 minutes)

Articles 14–18 were about equal standing. Articles 19–22 are about living as a free person who can still be governed.

Keep four gates in your head:

1. **Article 19** is for **citizens** only. A foreigner does not get the six freedoms. She still gets Articles 14, 20, 21 and 22.
2. **Article 19** lists freedoms in clause (1) and lists **reasonable restrictions** in clauses (2) to (6). The restriction must be by **law**, not by a circular that pretends to be law, and it must be **reasonable**. Reasonableness is both substantive (is the limit needed?) and procedural (is the manner fair?).
3. **Article 20** is the criminal-process shield. It does not create a freedom to speak or to move. It stops the State from inventing a crime after the act, from punishing twice for the same offence, and from forcing a person to confess.
4. **Article 21** is the residual ocean: no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. After *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) that procedure must be fair, just and reasonable. Almost every modern socio-economic right you meet in newspapers is an expansion of 21, or of 21 read with 14.
5. **Article 21A** (86th Amendment, 2002) is free and compulsory education for children from six to fourteen, as a fundamental right. The statute that gives it teeth is the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009.
6. **Article 22** is the arrest-and-detention code. Ordinary arrest has three gifts (grounds, lawyer, magistrate in 24 hours). Preventive detention has a harsher, separate track.

Property: the old Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31 were removed from Part III by the **44th Amendment (1978)**. Right to property now lives as a constitutional legal right in **Article 300A**: no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law. It is not a fundamental right. Do not write 19(1)(f) as if it still exists.

Emergency reminder from yesterday, sharpened: during a Proclamation of Emergency, Article 358 can suspend Article 19 when the Emergency is on the ground of war or external aggression (not on armed rebellion after the 44th Amendment). Article 359 can suspend the right to move court for other specified rights, except that Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended. File this; the Emergency day will reuse it.

# BLOCK B — Article 19: six freedoms and their brakes (70 minutes)

## The text of 19(1)

All citizens shall have the right

- **(a)** to freedom of speech and expression;
- **(b)** to assemble peaceably and without arms;
- **(c)** to form associations or unions or cooperative societies;
- **(d)** to move freely throughout the territory of India;
- **(e)** to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India;
- **(g)** to practise any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade or business.

Clause (f) is gone. Clause (c) gained “cooperative societies” by the **97th Amendment (2011)**. Part IXB on cooperatives was later read down for States because ratification was incomplete; the fundamental-right words in 19(1)(c) still mention cooperatives. For Prelims, know the 97th inserted cooperatives into 19(1)(c).

These rights are against the **State** (Article 12). A private employer who sacks you for a Facebook post is not, by that fact alone, violating Article 19. Labour law, contract, and platform regulation may apply. Horizontal application of 19 is a live academic debate; the safe exam position is: 19 binds the State.

## 19(2) — brakes on speech

The State may restrict 19(1)(a) by law in the interests of

- sovereignty and integrity of India,
- security of the State,
- friendly relations with foreign States,
- public order,
- decency or morality,
- contempt of court,
- defamation,
- incitement to an offence.

This list is **exhaustive**. A ground not on the list cannot justify a speech curb under 19(2). “Sovereignty and integrity” was added by the 16th Amendment (1963).

Distinctions you must not blur:

- **Security of the State** is rebellion, war, overthrow. It is not a noisy rally.
- **Public order** is a public peace of a community, wider than security of the State, narrower than ordinary law and order. A street fight is law and order. A communal riot is public order. An armed insurrection is security of the State.
- **Decency or morality** is not the personal taste of a policeman.
- **Contempt of court** protects the administration of justice, not the thin skin of a judge as a private person.
- **Defamation** is both civil and criminal in Indian statute law; constitutionally it is an allowed ground of restriction.

**Prior restraint** (ban the speech before it is spoken) is more dangerous than punishment after. Courts tolerate prior restraint only in narrow windows (troop movements, incitement that is imminent). A general pre-censorship of the press is hostile to 19(1)(a).

Speech includes: spoken word, writing, art, film, advertisement that is not purely commercial deceit, the right to receive information, the right to remain silent as a facet in some settings, and — by judicial reading — the right to know as a condition of democracy. Commercial speech gets a weaker shield than political speech.

**Reasonable** is the judicial word that saves 19 from becoming a blank cheque to the Home Department. A ban must have a proximate link to the listed ground. A remote, speculative fear is not enough. The restriction should be the least that will do the job, though Indian courts do not always use American “strict scrutiny” language.

## 19(3) — assembly

Peaceable, unarmed assembly may be restricted in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India or public order. A district magistrate can impose Section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (the successor of old CrPC 144) when there is an imminent threat to public order. The order must be specific in time and place. An indefinite city-wide silence is a red flag.

Assembly is not a right to occupy a public road forever. It is a right to meet. Regulation of traffic and of the use of a maidan is not automatically a ban on assembly.

## 19(4) — association

Restrictions: sovereignty and integrity of India, public order, morality. The State may refuse to register, or may cancel, an association that is a front for violence. It may not dissolve a trade union merely because the union is inconvenient. Compulsory membership of a State-run body is sometimes upheld for a closed profession (a bar council, a medical council) because the body is a regulator, not a political club.

## 19(5) — movement and residence

Restrictions: the interests of the general public, or the protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe. This is why inner-line and tribal-area restrictions on outsiders buying land or settling can survive. A pandemic lockdown is a movement restriction justified as general-public interest, provided it is by law or by a lawfully authorised order and is not endless and reasonless.

Movement (19(1)(d)) and residence (19(1)(e)) are twin rights. A citizen may walk through a State and may also make a home there, subject to the tribal and general-public brakes.

## 19(6) — profession, occupation, trade, business

Restrictions in the interests of the general public, plus two express permissions:

- the State may prescribe **professional or technical qualifications**;
- the State may run a trade or industry itself, as a monopoly, partly or wholly, to the exclusion of citizens.

So: a licence for doctors is valid. A State monopoly in a public utility can be valid. A whim that says “only my cousin may sell grain in this mandi” is not.

Reasonable restrictions here include health, environment, planning, consumer protection, and floor wages. A total prohibition of a trade (for example a dangerous intoxicant) can be a reasonable restriction if the evil is grave. Prohibition of a harmless ordinary trade is harder to defend.

## How the six talk to Articles 21 and 14

If the State silences a citizen without a law, 19 is enough. If the State silences her with a law that is arbitrary, 14 joins. If the State silences her by threatening her body or her passport or her livelihood in a way that is not fair procedure, 21 joins. *Maneka Gandhi* is the case that braided 14, 19 and 21 into one due-process rope. Learn the braid, not only the passport story.

# BLOCK C — Article 20: three criminal-law locks (35 minutes)

Article 20 has three clauses. None of them is suspended in an Emergency.

## 20(1) — no ex post facto criminal law

No person shall be convicted of an offence except for violation of a law in force **at the time of the act**, nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at that time.

Two sub-rules:

- You cannot invent a crime after the day and punish yesterday’s act.
- You cannot raise the punishment after the day and apply the new, heavier penalty to yesterday’s act.

Civil liabilities and taxes can be retrospective. Article 20(1) is about **criminal** offence and **penalty**. A new reporting duty, a new tax slab, or a new civil disqualification is not automatically 20(1).

If a later law **reduces** the penalty, the accused can usually claim the lighter one as a matter of penal policy; 20(1) itself is a shield against the heavier one.

## 20(2) — no double jeopardy

No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the **same offence** more than once.

This is narrower than the American phrase. Both prosecution **and** punishment must have happened once. A departmental enquiry plus a criminal trial can run together because they are not the same offence in the same forum. A fresh trial after an acquittal on the merits is the classic 20(2) problem. “Same offence” means the same ingredients, not a loosely related story.

The Code of Criminal Procedure / BNSS also contains a statutory double-jeopardy rule. Article 20(2) is the constitutional floor.

## 20(3) — no compulsion to be a witness against oneself

No person **accused of an offence** shall be compelled to be a witness against himself.

Triggers:

- the person must be an **accused**, not a mere witness or a suspect in a vague inquiry;
- the thing compelled must be of a **testimonial** character — an oral or written statement that communicates a fact from the person’s mind.

Fingerprints, blood samples, voice samples, DNA swabs, and standing in an identification parade have been treated as physical evidence, not testimony, in the main line of cases. A compelled signature on a confession is testimony. A lie-detector test without consent raises 20(3) and 21 together.

“Compelled” includes physical duress and the legal command that leaves no real choice. A voluntary confession to a magistrate, after warning, is not 20(3).

Article 20 is available to **persons**, not only citizens. A company can claim 20(1) and 20(2) in some settings; 20(3) is built around a natural person who can speak.

# BLOCK D — Articles 21, 21A and 22 (60 minutes)

## Article 21 — life and personal liberty

Text: No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to **procedure established by law**.

Two historical readings:

- Early reading (*A.K. Gopalan*): if a statute exists and the officer followed the printed steps, the court will not ask whether the law is fair.
- Later reading (*Maneka Gandhi*, 1978): “procedure established by law” means a procedure that is fair, just and reasonable. Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not sealed rooms. A law that takes liberty must also survive non-arbitrariness.

**Life** is not animal existence. It includes dignity, livelihood as a condition of living, health, clean environment, shelter as a facet, privacy, education as a precondition of a meaningful life (now also separately 21A), speedy trial, legal aid for an accused who cannot pay, and a long list of derived rights. For exam writing, do not recite fifty case names. Recite the method: the court asks whether the interest is so close to a life with dignity that the State cannot crush it without a fair law and a fair process.

**Personal liberty** is the freedom of the body and of movement in the older, physical sense, and after *Maneka* a wider set of autonomous choices. Passport seizure, arbitrary detention, and forced exile-inside-the-country all hit 21.

**Privacy.** A nine-judge Bench in *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017) held that privacy is a fundamental right, rooted primarily in Article 21, and also touching 14 and 19. It is not absolute. The State may intrude if the law exists, the aim is legitimate, and the means are proportionate. Aadhaar was later read through this test: some uses survived, some were cut.

**Death penalty.** Article 21 permits deprivation of life by a valid procedure. The judicial compromise is that the death sentence is constitutional only in the “rarest of rare” cases, after a special sentencing hearing. Mercy under Articles 72 and 161 sits above that.

**Environment.** Life includes the right to live in a healthy environment. Sustainable development, polluter pays, and precautionary principle entered Indian public law through 21 plus 48A and 51A(g). You will meet those Directive and Duty clauses on Day 5; today remember that 21 is the justiciable hook.

## Article 21A — education

The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years in such manner as the State may, by law, determine.

The 86th Amendment (2002) did three related things: inserted 21A; changed Article 45 to early childhood care and education below six; added Article 51A(k) — a duty of the parent or guardian to provide education to the child between six and fourteen.

The 2009 Act converts the right into school-level duties: neighbourhood school, no capitation, no screening at entry in the specified range, 25 percent seats in private unaided schools for weaker sections and disadvantaged groups (reimbursed by the State), and norms for teachers and infrastructure. Minority institutions under Article 30 have a distinct treatment that you will study with cultural rights.

## Article 22 — arrest and preventive detention

Article 22 splits into two worlds.

### Ordinary arrest — 22(1) and 22(2)

A person who is arrested and detained in custody:

- shall be informed, as soon as may be, of the **grounds** of arrest;
- shall have the right to consult and to be defended by a **legal practitioner** of his choice;
- shall be produced before the nearest **magistrate within 24 hours** (travel time excluded);
- shall not be detained beyond that period without the magistrate’s authority.

These are not courtesies. An arrest that hides the ground, or that parks the person in a lock-up past 24 hours without a magistrate, is unconstitutional on its face.

### Who does not get 22(1) and 22(2)

Article 22(3): those two clauses do not apply to

- an **enemy alien**, or
- a person arrested or detained under any law providing for **preventive detention**.

### Preventive detention — 22(4) to 22(7)

Preventive detention is detention to **prevent** a future act, not to punish a past offence. It is a colonial tool the Constitution kept, with fences.

Fences:

- No detention beyond **three months** unless an **Advisory Board** (judges or persons qualified to be High Court judges) reports that there is sufficient cause.
- Parliament may by law prescribe the circumstances and the class of cases in which a person may be detained longer than three months without board approval, and the maximum period. This is 22(7). It is the clause that makes long preventive detention constitutionally possible.
- The detaining authority must communicate the grounds as soon as may be, and must give the earliest opportunity of representation.
- Facts considered against the public interest may be withheld. That exception is abused when the entire ground is a sealed envelope. Courts strike detention orders that are vague, stale, or copied from a template.

Union and State both may legislate on preventive detention (List I and List III). Classic statutes you will see named in questions: National Security Act; laws on conservation of foreign exchange and on drug trafficking have also used the tool. Learn the constitutional fences first; statute names second.

**Ethics cut inside 22.** A station-house officer who delays the magistrate production to “teaching the boy a lesson” has not been strict. He has broken Article 22. Preventive detention used to silence a inconvenient journalist is a 19 + 21 + 22 failure at once.

# BLOCK E — How to write 19–22 in Mains (15 minutes)

A GS-II core on free speech:

> Article 19(1)(a) is a citizen’s right, limited only by the exhaustive grounds in 19(2) and by the requirement that the limit be a reasonable law. Public order is not the same as the comfort of the administration. After *Maneka*, a speech curb that is arbitrary also fails Articles 14 and 21. The governance task is not to choose between chaos and silence; it is to police incitement and violence without pre-censoring dissent.

A GS-II core on preventive detention:

> The Constitution permits preventive detention but surrounds it with grounds, representation, a three-month board rule, and judicial review of mala fide or vague orders. A republic that uses prevention as routine punishment has swapped Article 21 for convenience.

A GS-IV hinge: producing an arrested daily-wager before a magistrate on time is as much an integrity test as refusing a bribe. Procedure is morality made visible.

# BLOCK F — Drainage systems of India (75 minutes)

A drainage system is the network of a main river and its tributaries that empties a catchment into the sea or into an inland lake. India has two large families: **Himalayan** and **Peninsular**, plus a small inland drainage in parts of the dry west.

## Himalayan versus peninsular — the contrast you must own

| Feature | Himalayan rivers | Peninsular rivers |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Snow-fed plus rain; many rise beyond the Indian crest | Rain-fed; rise in Western Ghats, Central Highlands, or plateau |
| Regime | Perennial; summer snow-melt supports lean months | Seasonal; lean in pre-monsoon |
| Course | Long, deep gorges in mountains, thick alluvium in plains, shifting channels | Shorter, graded, relatively stable courses on hard rock |
| Valley | Youthful in hills (V-shaped, waterfalls), mature and meandering in plains | Mature to old; open valleys, few waterfalls except at plateau edges |
| Delta / estuary | Large deltas (Ganga-Brahmaputra, Indus outside India) | East-flowing: deltas. West-flowing Narmada–Tapi: estuaries |
| Capture and change | Frequent avulsion, ox-bows, braiding | Less dramatic channel change |

Himalayan rivers are **antecedent** in several stretches: the river is older than the rising mountain and has cut through as the range rose (Indus, Sutlej, Brahmaputra gorges). Peninsular rivers are largely **consequent** — they follow the slope of the land.

## The water divide

The main water divide of peninsular India is the **Western Ghats**. Short swift rivers run west to the Arabian Sea. Long rivers run east to the Bay of Bengal, following the general tilt of the plateau.

In the north, the Himalayan crest and the ranges beyond (Karakoram, Kailash) divide the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra catchments. The **Aravali** is a secondary divide between some Bay-bound and Arabian-bound residual streams, but it is not a Himalayan-scale wall.

## Indus system

The Indus rises near **Lake Manasarovar / Kailash** region in Tibet. It flows north-west through Ladakh (near Leh it is a classic antecedent gorge), turns south into Pakistan, and meets the Arabian Sea east of Karachi. Only the upper course and some tributaries are in India.

Left-bank (eastern) tributaries that matter for the Indian map:

- **Jhelum** — rises in Verinag spring, Kashmir; flows through Wular Lake; joins Chenab in Pakistan.
- **Chenab** — formed by Chandra and Bhaga at Tandi (Himachal); largest of the Indus tributaries by volume in the Indian basin.
- **Ravi** — rises west of Rohtang; a comparatively smaller river; important as a boundary-fed stream.
- **Beas** — rises near Rohtang; the only major Indus tributary that lies **entirely in India**; joins Sutlej at Harike in Punjab.
- **Sutlej** — rises near Rakas Lake / Manasarovar region; enters India at Shipki La; Bhakra-Nangal sits on it.

The **Indus Waters Treaty (1960)** allocated the three eastern rivers (Sutlej, Beas, Ravi) to India for unrestricted use, and the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan, with limited specified Indian uses (run-of-river hydropower, some irrigation). This is a current-affairs magnet. Learn the six-river split before any headline.

## Ganga system

The Ganga is a name that begins at **Devprayag**, where **Bhagirathi** (from Gangotri / Gaumukh) meets **Alaknanda** (which has already collected the Vishnu Ganga and other headstreams; the sacred confluence sequence is Vishnuprayag, Nandprayag, Karnaprayag, Rudraprayag, Devprayag). After Devprayag the river is Ganga.

It cuts the Himalaya, enters the plain at Haridwar, flows south-east, and is joined by a long list of tributaries. It splits in its lower course; the main Indian distributary toward the sea is often called the **Bhagirathi-Hooghly** in West Bengal. The main padma channel goes into Bangladesh. The Ganga-Brahmaputra-Meghna meeting builds the world’s largest delta.

**Right-bank tributaries** (generally from the south / peninsula or the south-west):

- **Yamuna** — the largest tributary. Rises at Yamunotri. Touches Delhi, then Agra, then joins Ganga at **Prayagraj (Triveni with the unseen Saraswati in belief; geographically Yamuna + Ganga)**. Yamuna’s own right-bank feeders include Chambal, Sind, Betwa, Ken — the Bundelkhand–Malwa family.
- **Son** — rises in Amarkantak highland; a big southern contributor in Bihar.
- **Damodar** — “sorrow of Bengal” before the dam system; now a regulated coal-plateau river joining the Hooghly system.

**Left-bank tributaries** (generally from the Himalaya or the north):

- **Ramganga, Gomati, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi, Mahananda.**
- **Ghaghara** (Karnali in Nepal) is a major left-bank volume.
- **Kosi** is notorious for channel shift; it is a textbook braided, silt-choked Himalayan river.
- **Gandak** (Narayani in Nepal) rises near the Nepal-Tibet area and joins near Hajipur / Sonepur.

Remember bank by a picture, not a poem: stand facing downstream (toward the sea). Left is north-ish for the Ganga in the plain; right is south-ish. Yamuna and Son come from the right; Kosi and Ghaghara from the left.

## Brahmaputra system

Rises as the **Yarlung Tsangpo** in Tibet, east of Manasarovar. Flows east across the dry Tibetan rain-shadow, then turns sharply around **Namcha Barwa**, enters Arunachal as the **Siang / Dihang**, meets Dibang and Lohit, and becomes the **Brahmaputra** in Assam. In Bangladesh it is the **Jamuna**, then meets Padma, then Meghna, then the sea.

It is braided, wide, and flood-prone in Assam because the valley is a trough, the sediment load is enormous, and the monsoon plus Tibetan melt arrive together. Major left-bank (north) tributaries in Assam include Subansiri, Kameng, Dhansiri (there is name-duplication on Indian maps — be careful), Manas, Sankosh. South-bank feeders include the Kopili and others from the Meghalaya–Karbi side.

The **Meghna** is the name in Bangladesh for the combined Barak-Surma-Kushiyara family plus the Ganga-Brahmaputra outfall. The **Barak** rises in Manipur-Nagaland highlands and is the upper stream of that family inside India (Barak Valley of Assam).

## Peninsular east-flowing rivers

From north to south, the large east-flowers:

- **Mahanadi** — rises in Chhattisgarh (Sihawa / Dhamtari uplands). Hirakud dam near Sambalpur. Delta in Odisha. Drainage of the Chhattisgarh plain.
- **Godavari** — longest peninsular river. Rises at Trimbakeshwar near Nashik. Crosses Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra. Tributaries: Pravara, Purna, Manjra, Pranhita (which itself is Penganga–Wardha–Wainganga), Indravati, Sabari. Delta is a dual-arm fertile tract.
- **Krishna** — rises near Mahabaleshwar. Tributaries: Koyna, Bhima, Tungabhadra (Tunga + Bhadra from Karnataka), Musi (Hyderabad). Almatti, Nagarjuna Sagar, Srisailam are names you will see in water disputes.
- **Cauvery (Kaveri)** — rises at Talakaveri in Kodagu. Grand Anicut (Kallanai) is an ancient weir. The delta in Tamil Nadu is old irrigated country. Tributaries: Hemavati, Shimsha, Arkavati, Kabini, Bhavani, Amravati, Noyyal. The basin is the classic inter-State dispute (Karnataka–Tamil Nadu, with Kerala and Puducherry).

Smaller east-flowers: Subarnarekha, Brahmani, Baitarani, Penneru (Penner), Vaigai, Palar. Do not force them into the Godavari family.

## Peninsular west-flowing rivers

- **Narmada** — rises at Amarkantak. Flows west in a **rift valley** between the Vindhya and Satpura. Estuary at the Gulf of Khambhat. Not a delta. Dhuandhar falls near Jabalpur. Sardar Sarovar is the large terminal dam debate you already meet in ethics and environment.
- **Tapi (Tapti)** — rises in Satpura (Betul / Multai region). Also rift-related. Estuary near Surat. Ukai dam.
- **Mahi** — highland of Madhya Pradesh; crosses the Tropic of Cancer twice; Gulf of Khambhat.
- **Sabarmati** — Aravali–Mewar origin; Ahmedabad on its bank; Arabian Sea.
- Short west-coast streams: Mandovi and Zuari (Goa), Sharavati (Jog Falls), Periyar and Bharathapuzha (Kerala). They are steep, short, and hydro-rich.

Why Narmada and Tapi make estuaries rather than deltas: steep gradient near the sea, strong tides of the Arabian Sea, and a narrower shelf that scours sediment rather than letting it pile as a bird-foot delta.

## Inland drainage

Parts of the Thar and the Rann do not send water to the sea. Streams fade into playas and salt flats. **Luni** is the largest: it rises in the Aravali and dies toward the Rann of Kachchh. Some Great Basin-style lakes of Ladakh (Tso Moriri, Pangong) are closed basins. Inland drainage is scanty rainfall plus a basin that has no outlet.

## Drainage patterns (one pass)

- **Dendritic** — tree-like; uniform rock. Most of the northern plains and much of the peninsula.
- **Trellis** — short tributaries join a long main stream at right angles; folded or ridged rock. Parts of the upper Himalaya and some peninsular scarps.
- **Radial** — off a dome or a hill mass. The Amarkantak high sends Son (Ganga family), Narmada (west), and Mahanadi headwaters (east) in different directions. A single plateau knot feeding three basins is a favourite map question.
- **Centripetal** — into a basin. Inland drainage of Rajasthan playas.
- **Annular** — circular rings around a dome. Rare, textbook more than map.

## Lakes that are not decoration

- Wular (Jhelum regulator, ox-bow / tectonic debate).
- Dal (not a drainage trunk).
- Chilika (lagoon on Odisha coast, not a river lake).
- Pulicat (Andhra–Tamil Nadu lagoon).
- Vembanad (Kerala backwater).
- Sambhar (inland saline, Rajasthan).
- Loktak (Manipur; floating phumdis; National Hydroelectric sits on it — environment later).

# BLOCK G — Paired block: river economy, current affairs, ethics (25 minutes)

## Economy

Rivers are irrigation, inland freight, hydropower, urban water, and waste sinks. The Ganga plain is still India’s densest agricultural belt because alluvium plus canal plus monsoon coincide. Peninsular tanks and canal commands (Cauvery delta, Godavari delta, Nagarjuna Sagar) are older engineering than most IITs.

National Waterways: the Ganga-Bhagirathi-Hooghly stretch is **National Waterway 1** (Haldia–Prayagraj). Brahmaputra is NW-2. West Coast Canal in Kerala is NW-3. A waterway cuts truck diesel but needs assured draft, terminals, and a river that is not first emptied by upstream dams. When a budget line says “inland water transport,” translate it to draft, silt, and last-mile roads, not to a free highway.

Inter-linking of rivers is an engineering sentence with three hidden invoices: the energy cost of lifting water, the ecological cost of punching a canal through a forest or a delta that needs its silt, and the political cost of moving one basin’s monsoon surplus into another basin’s drought. A surplus is not a fact of nature; it is a claim made in a wet year.

## Current affairs explained, tied to today

When a headline says India is holding or reviewing the Indus Waters Treaty, map it onto the six-river split: eastern three to India, western three to Pakistan with limited Indian uses. The constitutional hook inside India is Article 246 / Union list on inter-State rivers plus the treaty power of the Union. The ethics hook is that civilian irrigation on both sides is not a stadium.

When a Cauvery season turns into a protest, the static hook is a rain-fed peninsular river with a finite monsoon pulse, a 19th-century weir, and two intensive irrigated deltas. The legal hook is a tribunal / scheme / Supreme Court supervision, not a slogan about civilisation.

When a city “cleans the Yamuna,” Article 21 (health, dignity, environment) is the justiciable hook. Sewage treatment capacity and environmental flow are the real verbs. A one-day idol-immersion drive is not a drainage policy.

When an arrested protester is not produced in 24 hours, do not write a political essay first. Write Article 22(2), then 21, then 19(1)(b) if it was an assembly case.

## Ethics

Riparian fairness: the upstream State sees a river as its rainfall; the downstream State sees the same water as its inherited canal. A civil servant who hides reservoir data is not being loyal to her State. She is stealing from a common pool that the Constitution placed in the Union-State lists for a reason. Displacement at a dam site is an Article 21 livelihood question, not only a compensation spreadsheet.

# BLOCK H — CSAT: time, work, distance, and a short passage (40 minutes)

Work on paper first.

### Q1. Two pipes

Pipe A fills a tank in 12 hours, pipe B in 18. They open together. After 4 hours, A is shut. Hours more for B to fill the rest?

**Solution.** A = 1/12 per hour, B = 1/18. Together = (3+2)/36 = 5/36 per hour.
Four hours: 20/36 = 5/9 filled. Left = 4/9.
B alone: (4/9) ÷ (1/18) = 8 hours.

### Q2. Downstream and upstream

A boat’s speed in still water is 15 km/h. Stream is 3 km/h. Time for 36 km down and 36 km up?

**Solution.** Down = 18 km/h, up = 12 km/h.
Time = 36/18 + 36/12 = 2 + 3 = 5 hours.

### Q3. Relative speed

Two trains 180 m and 120 m long run at 54 km/h and 36 km/h in opposite directions. Time to cross each other?

**Solution.** Convert: 54 km/h = 15 m/s, 36 km/h = 10 m/s. Opposite: relative = 25 m/s.
Distance = 180 + 120 = 300 m.
Time = 300/25 = 12 seconds.

Same direction would have been 15 − 10 = 5 m/s → 60 seconds. Always name the direction.

### Q4. Work rates

A, B and C can finish a job in 10, 12 and 15 days. All three start. A leaves after 2 days. B leaves 3 days before the work ends. How many days did the work take in all?

**Solution.** Let total days = n.
A works 2 days: 2/10 = 1/5.
B works (n − 3) days: (n − 3)/12.
C works n days: n/15.
1/5 + (n−3)/12 + n/15 = 1.
(n−3)/12 + n/15 = 4/5.
LCM of 12 and 15 is 60:
5(n−3)/60 + 4n/60 = 4/5.
5n − 15 + 4n = 48.
9n = 63.
n = 7 days.

Check: A = 2/10 = 0.2; B = 4/12 = 1/3; C = 7/15. Sum = 0.2 + 0.333… + 0.466… = 1.

### Q5. Average speed

A man drives to a town at 40 km/h and back at 60 km/h. Distance one way 120 km. Average speed for the round trip?

**Solution.** Average of speeds over the **same distance** both ways is the harmonic mean: 2ab/(a+b) = 2×40×60 / 100 = 48 km/h.
Not 50. Time out = 3 h, time back = 2 h, total 5 h for 240 km → 48 km/h.

### Q6. Comprehension

Read: “A river does not belong to the district where the rain first hit the ridge. It belongs to the basin. Article 19 lets a citizen move along that basin; Article 21 lets her ask for water that will not poison her child. Neither article tells an engineer where to put a dam. They tell the engineer that a dam which silences a village without a hearing is not only bad hydrology.”

Q6a. The author’s main claim is that (A) dams are unconstitutional (B) rain belongs only to the upstream district (C) fundamental rights constrain how river projects treat people, not the science of siting alone (D) Article 19 creates a right to a dam.

Answer: **C**.

Q6b. Which inference is supported? That a project hearing is part of treating life and liberty as real. Yes.

Q6c. Which is not supported? That Article 19 is a right to settle on a riverbed. The passage uses 19 only as movement along a basin.
