# BLOCK A — How to read Articles 23 to 32 as one family (20 minutes)

Articles 14–22 were about standing equal and staying free in body and voice. Articles 23–24 are about the body that can be sold. Articles 25–28 are about the mind that worships. Articles 29–30 are about the group that is not the numerical majority. Article 32 is the locksmith: without a remedy, the earlier articles are sermons.

Keep seven gates in your head:

1. **Articles 23 and 24** are available to **persons**, not only citizens. A trafficked foreign child is inside 23. They bind the State and, in substance, they also condemn private bondage. The State must prohibit and punish; it cannot say “this is a family matter.”
2. **Articles 25–28** are **religious liberty** with four rooms: conscience and practice (25), manage a religious institution (26), no tax earmarked for a particular religion (27), no religious instruction in a wholly State-funded school (28). All four live under **public order, morality, health**, and the other provisions of Part III.
3. **Articles 29 and 30** are **cultural and educational rights of minorities**. 29 is script, language, culture, and a no-discrimination rule in State-aided admission. 30 is the right of religious or linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions. 30 is not a right to break the labour law of teachers or the safety code of a laboratory.
4. **Article 31** as a fundamental right to property is gone (44th Amendment, 1978). What remains in this neighbourhood is a cluster of **saving clauses**: 31A, 31B, 31C. They protect certain agrarian and social-reform laws from being struck down as violations of specified fundamental rights. Learn them as shields for land reform, not as a living right to own a bungalow.
5. **Article 32** is itself a fundamental right: the right to move the **Supreme Court** for enforcement of Part III. Dr Ambedkar called it the heart and soul of the Constitution in the Constituent Assembly. **Article 226** in a High Court is wider (it can reach legal rights beyond Part III) but it is not itself listed as a fundamental right.
6. **Articles 33, 34, 35** close Part III. 33 lets Parliament modify rights for the armed forces, police, and analogous forces. 34 deals with martial-law indemnity. 35 reserves certain legislative powers to Parliament (for example, punishments for 17 and 23, and the residuary force-laws). You do not need a case-law forest today; you need the map.
7. **Who is a minority?** The Constitution does not define the word. Judicial practice treats **religious** and **linguistic** groups that are less than 50 percent in the **relevant unit**. For Article 30, the unit that has mattered most is the **State**, not the whole of India, when the institution sits in that State. A group can be a majority in one State and a minority in another.

Emergency reminder, sharpened again: Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended. Article 19 can be suspended under 358 only when the Emergency is war or external aggression. Article 32 can be affected by an Article 359 Presidential Order that suspends the right to move court for specified rights — except 20 and 21. File this; Day 10 on emergency will reuse it.

# BLOCK B — Articles 23 and 24: the body is not a commodity (40 minutes)

## Article 23 — prohibition of traffic in human beings and forced labour

Text, in substance:

- Traffic in human beings, *begar*, and other similar forms of forced labour are prohibited.
- Any contravention is an offence punishable in accordance with law.
- The State may impose **compulsory service for public purposes**, without discriminating only on grounds of religion, race, caste, or class.

**Traffic in human beings** is the trade in persons: selling, buying, recruiting, harbouring, or transferring a person for exploitation. Sexual exploitation, forced domestic work, bonded brick-kiln work, and organ trade sit inside this idea. The constitutional sentence is short; the statutes are long. For this file, hold the constitutional verb: the Republic forbids the market in persons.

**Begar** is work without remuneration, extracted by custom, landlord power, or official arrogance. A village that still expects the landless family to repair the landlord’s bund for free is practising *begar*. A police station that keeps an undertrial sweeping the yard for weeks without wage is in the same constitutional ditch.

**Other similar forms of forced labour** is the phrase that let the Supreme Court read **bonded labour** and **below-minimum-wage work extracted under hunger** as forced labour. The logic: if a person works because the alternative is starvation or a bonded debt that never ends, consent is not free. Payment of less than the notified minimum wage, when the worker has no real exit, has been treated as a 23 problem, not only a labour-inspector problem.

**Compulsory public service** is the exception. Conscription in war, a legal duty to assist in a disaster, a short civic obligation, or a legally created national service scheme can survive. The exception cannot be a caste-coded *begar* renamed as “village duty.” The non-discrimination rider matters: the State cannot draft only one caste into the unpleasant public work.

Article 23 is enforceable against private persons in the sense that the State must criminalise and stop private bondage. A victim does not have to wait for a pious circular. Public interest litigation under Article 32 has been used to push identification, release, and rehabilitation of bonded workers.

## Article 24 — no child labour in hazardous workplaces

No child **below the age of fourteen years** shall be employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other **hazardous employment**.

Read the verbs. The Constitution itself bans the child from factory, mine, and hazardous work. It does not, in this one sentence, ban every form of help in a family farm. Statute law later widened and tightened the field:

- Child labour law now works with a simple idea: **no child under 14 in any commercial employment**, with a narrow family-enterprise and artist exception that cannot swallow the rule, and a ban on **adolescents (14–18)** in hazardous occupations and processes.
- Article 21A (yesterday) and Article 24 are twins: a child who is in Class 5 cannot also be in a cracker factory. When you write Mains, join 24 + 21A + 39(e)–(f) + 45 + 51A(k). The Directive and Duty clauses arrive in tomorrow’s file; today remember that 24 is the justiciable floor.

Hazardous is not a mood. It is processes that poison, crush, burn, or steal growth: mines, power looms with unguarded belts, rag-picking in dumps, brick kilns, some chemical units, domestic work in hidden rooms when it is employment not kinship.

**Ethics cut.** A contractor who keeps a 12-year-old on a loom because “the family asked” has not been kind. He has used hunger as a signature. A tehsildar who returns a bonded family to the same landlord after a “compromise” has not restored peace. He has restored the offence.

# BLOCK C — Articles 25 to 28: conscience with traffic rules (70 minutes)

## Article 25 — freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and propagation

All **persons** are equally entitled to freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and **propagate** religion, subject to **public order, morality, health**, and the other provisions of this Part.

Four words that Prelims loves:

- **Conscience** — the inner freedom to believe or not believe. The State cannot run a census of your soul.
- **Profess** — to declare the faith.
- **Practise** — rituals, diet, dress, worship, so far as they are integral to the religion and not a crime.
- **Propagate** — to spread one’s religion by exposition. Propagation is not a fundamental right to **forcible conversion**. A charity hospital that heals is not, by that fact, a conversion machine. A threat, a bribe, or a concealment that treats a person as a harvest is not 25.

Article 25 is for **persons**, including non-citizens. It is also **equal** entitlement: the State cannot prefer one denomination’s street procession and ban another’s on the same facts.

Two official powers sit inside 25(2):

- The State may **regulate or restrict economic, financial, political or other secular activity** associated with religious practice. A temple’s lockers, a shrine’s helicopter contract, a math’s hotel — these can be regulated as secular activity even if they sit next to a sanctum.
- The State may provide for **social welfare and reform**, and for throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus. This is the constitutional hinge for temple-entry reform and for laws against exclusion of Dalits from public temples. “Hindu” in this clause is read widely to include sections historically treated as within that family of denominations (Sikh, Jaina, Buddhist are expressly included for this clause).

**Essential religious practice.** Courts have asked whether a practice is essential to the religion as the religion understands itself, or whether it is a social accretion. The test is contested and you should not pretend it is a neat formula. For the exam, state the tension honestly: liberty of religion versus reform of religion. Article 25 allows both, and the Court is the uneasy referee.

**Public order, morality, health** are not a policeman’s taste. A loudspeaker at 2 a.m. in a hospital lane is a health and order question. A ban on a peaceful indoor prayer is not. Animal sacrifice, narcotic ritual, and epidemic gatherings are the classic health-morality collisions. Write the ground; do not write your appetite.

## Article 26 — manage religious affairs

Every **religious denomination** or a section thereof has the right, subject to public order, morality and health:

- to establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes;
- to manage its own **affairs in matters of religion**;
- to own and acquire movable and immovable property;
- to administer such property **in accordance with law**.

Denomination means a collective with a common faith, a common organisation, and a distinctive name. A single believer is 25. A church, a sect, a math, a jamaat can be 26.

The split that saves 26 from becoming a parallel State:

- **Matters of religion** (ritual, doctrine, who may enter the sanctum as a priest if that is essential) are for the denomination.
- **Secular administration of property** is “in accordance with law.” A government may audit a public temple’s cash. It may not invent a new god.

Article 26 is a group right. It does not let a denomination violate 14, 15, 17 or 23 in the name of custom when the institution is public and the custom is exclusion or bondage.

## Article 27 — no tax for the promotion of a particular religion

No person shall be compelled to pay any **tax** the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated in payment of expenses for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.

A **tax** is a compulsory levy for public purposes, unrequited. A **fee** for a specific service (a pilgrim facility fee that actually runs toilets and crowd control) is a different animal. Article 27 bites when the State earmarks a tax to run one faith’s worship.

The State may still spend general revenues on conservation of a monument that happens to be a temple or a mosque, because that is heritage and order, not promotion of a creed. The line is motive and earmarking, not the stone.

## Article 28 — religious instruction in educational institutions

Three rooms:

- **28(1)** — no religious instruction in an institution **wholly maintained out of State funds**.
- **28(2)** — that ban does not apply to an institution administered by the State but **established under an endowment or trust** that requires religious instruction.
- **28(3)** — in a State-recognised or State-aided institution, no person shall be required to take part in religious instruction or worship **without consent** (guardian’s consent if the person is a minor).

So: a government district school does not run a morning catechism. A college born from a trust that the State later administers may still carry the trust’s religious instruction. A student in an aided college cannot be forced into a chapel period.

**How 25–28 talk to 14, 19 and 21.** A ban on a book is 19 plus 25. A surveillance camera inside a confessional is 21 plus 25. A municipal rule that lets only one faith take a procession on the only main road is 14 plus 25. Always name the companion article.

# BLOCK D — Articles 29 and 30: minorities without secession (45 minutes)

## Article 29 — language, script, culture; admission

**29(1).** Any **section of the citizens** residing in the territory of India or any part thereof having a distinct language, script or culture of its own shall have the right to conserve the same.

Note the subject: **section of the citizens**, not only a minority. A numerical majority in a State can still claim 29(1) to conserve a script. In practice the clause is the cultural spine of minority protection.

**29(2).** No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution **maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds** on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language.

29(2) is an equality rule in the aided and State classroom. It is not a right to crash a purely private, unaided institution’s every seat. (Article 15(1) already bars State discrimination; 15(4) and 15(5) and 15(6) create the reservation architecture you met on Day 2.)

Tension you must be able to write: a minority institution under 30 wants to reserve seats for its own community; 29(2) says an aided institution cannot deny a citizen admission only on religion. The judicial compromise, in one line: an **aided** minority institution may have a preference for its community but cannot shut the door on others as if the aid did not exist; an **unaided** minority institution has a wider space to conserve its character, still subject to national interest, merit floors in professional courses, and the recognition laws. Do not flatten this into “minority colleges are above the Constitution.”

## Article 30 — establish and administer educational institutions

All minorities, whether based on **religion or language**, shall have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

**30(1A)** (44th Amendment) says that if the State compulsorily acquires the property of a minority educational institution, the law must fix an amount that does not restrict or abrogate the 30(1) right. This is the ghost of the old property right, kept alive only for this institutional purpose.

**30(2).** The State shall not, in granting aid, discriminate against an educational institution on the ground that it is under minority management.

Who is a minority for 30? Religious or linguistic group, typically below half in the **State** of the institution. Linguistic minorities are easy to forget and easy to ask. A Gujarati-medium school in Tamil Nadu can be 30 even if Hindus are a majority in both States.

**Administer** includes governing body, staff selection, a reasonable fee architecture, and a distinctive ethos. It does not include:

- teaching a syllabus that hides the Indian Constitution;
- paying teachers in cash to escape provident fund;
- refusing fire-safety norms;
- a medical college without patients or faculty.

Recognition and affiliation are not charity. The State may set **excellence and infrastructure standards** that apply to all. It may not use those standards as a needle to unpick only the minority governing board.

**National interest and professional education.** In professional colleges, courts have allowed regulation of merit, common entrance, and fee profiteering because the product is a doctor or an engineer for the whole country. Character of the institution survives; a capitation bazaar does not.

Article 30 is available to **citizens’** communities (minorities among citizens). A foreign church may fund; the right-holders are the minority community in India.

# BLOCK E — Articles 31A, 31B, 31C, 32–35 (50 minutes)

## The property ghost and the saving clauses

After the 44th Amendment, you do not write “fundamental right to property.” You write **Article 300A** (yesterday). In Part III, three shields remain because India used them to defend zamindari abolition and later social legislation.

**Article 31A.** Protects specified laws from being void on the ground that they take away or abridge 14 or 19. The specified laws are, in substance:

- acquisition of estates and related rights by the State;
- taking over management of a property for a limited period;
- amalgamation of corporations;
- extinguishing or modifying mineral or company rights of a defined kind;
- modification of mining leases.

“Estate” here is the agrarian unit of the old land system, not a luxury flat. The first constitutional amendments after 1951 exist because courts had struck land reform as a property-and-equality problem. 31A is that political memory written as law.

**Article 31B.** Creates the **Ninth Schedule**. An Act or regulation placed in the Ninth Schedule shall not be void on the ground of inconsistency with Part III. Originally a vault for land-reform statutes. Later, many other Acts were stuffed in. In *I.R. Coelho* (2007) the Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule **after 24 April 1973** (the date of *Kesavananda*) can still be examined for violation of the **basic structure**. Ninth Schedule is not a black hole after that date.

**Article 31C.** Protects laws that give effect to certain Directive Principles. After amendment history and judicial reading, the live core you should carry is: a law giving effect to **Article 39(b) and 39(c)** (ownership of resources distributed to serve the common good; operation of the economic system not resulting in concentration of wealth) is shielded from 14 and 19 attack, provided the law is in truth in service of those clauses. The attempt to make every Directive Principle a complete shield, and to shut out judicial review of the declaration, did not survive basic-structure review. For Prelims: 31C + 39(b)–(c) + basic structure limit.

## Article 32 — remedies

**32(1).** The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part is guaranteed.

**32(2).** The Supreme Court may issue directions or orders or **writs**, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari.

**32(3).** Parliament may empower any other court to issue these writs, without prejudice to 32(2). (High Courts already have 226 by the Constitution itself.)

**32(4).** This right shall not be suspended except as otherwise provided by the Constitution (the 359 route).

### Five writs, in working English

| Writ | Literal job | When you ask for it |
|---|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | Produce the body | Detention that is illegal, or whose legality must be shown |
| **Mandamus** | We command | A public authority refuses to perform a public duty |
| **Prohibition** | Stop there | An inferior court or tribunal is about to exceed jurisdiction |
| **Certiorari** | Be certified / quash | An inferior court or tribunal has already passed an order without jurisdiction or in violation of natural justice |
| **Quo warranto** | By what authority | A person occupies a public office without legal qualification |

Habeas corpus is the emergency lamp of 21 and 22. Mandamus does not lie against the President or Governor in their official constitutional discretion in the old textbook line, nor to enforce a purely private contract. Prohibition is preventive; certiorari is curative. Quo warranto protects the public office, not your rivalry.

**Article 226** versus **32**:

- 32 is a fundamental right; 226 is a constitutional power of High Courts.
- 32 is for Part III. 226 is for Part III **and** for “any other purpose” (legal rights, statutory duties).
- 32 goes to the Supreme Court. 226 is closer, cheaper, and usually the first door.
- Both can run all-India in appropriate cases; 226 territorial limits exist but have been read practically when the cause of action or the authority sits inside the High Court’s reach.

**Public interest litigation** grew from 32 and 226 when the person whose right was broken could not reach the court. The letter of a bonded labourer, or of a journalist writing on her behalf, was treated as a petition. PIL is not a tourist visa to argue every policy disagreement. The petitioner must still show a public right and a public injury, not a private grudge in costume.

## Articles 33, 34, 35

**Article 33.** Parliament may by law determine to what extent any of the rights in Part III shall, in their application to the **members of the Armed Forces, the Forces charged with the maintenance of public order, the intelligence organisations, and persons employed in the telecommunication systems set up for those forces**, be restricted or abrogated so as to ensure discipline and proper discharge of duty. Police associations, restricted speech of serving soldiers, and court-martial space live here. It is Parliament’s law, not a colonel’s circular, that must do the restricting.

**Article 34.** Indemnity for acts done under **martial law** in any area, once Parliament legislates. Martial law is not a synonym for national Emergency under Article 352. Do not fuse them.

**Article 35.** Only Parliament may make laws on:

- items specified in 16(3), 32(3), 33 and 34;
- prescribing punishment for acts declared offences under Part III (notably untouchability and traffic in persons).

States cannot invent their own private criminal code for Article 17 or 23. They can, of course, administer the Union law.

# BLOCK F — How to write 23–32 in Mains (15 minutes)

A GS-II core on conversion and 25:

> Article 25 protects conscience, profession, practice and propagation, not coercion. The State may punish force and fraud without compiling a theological index of true faith. A law that polices charity as if every hospital bed were a conversion is not public order; it is anxiety.

A GS-II core on minority schools:

> Article 30 conserves a community’s right to teach its children through an institution it establishes. Aid does not dissolve that character, and character does not dissolve the Republic’s interest in a qualified doctor or a safe building. The test is whether the regulation is a standard of excellence or a device to replace the minority board.

A GS-II core on Article 32:

> A fundamental right without Article 32 is advice. The writs turn life, liberty and equality into commands that an officer must answer. PIL widened standing so that the bonded and the voiceless could arrive as letters. Abuse of PIL is real; abolition of 32 would be constitutional suicide.

A GS-IV hinge: the constable who takes a fourteen-year-old back to the kiln after “counselling the parents” has failed Article 24 in uniform. Compassion that restores exploitation is not compassion.

# BLOCK G — Soils of India (80 minutes)

Soil is weathered rock plus organic matter plus water plus air, arranged in layers called horizons. For the exam you need formation, the Indian types, where they sit on the map, what they grow, and how they fail.

## How soil is made

Five factors:

1. **Parent rock** — granite, basalt, alluvium, limestone, sandstone. The chemistry of the rock is the first inheritance.
2. **Climate** — heat speeds weathering; rainfall leaches lime and silica or leaves them behind; freeze-thaw cracks rock.
3. **Relief** — steep slope sheds soil; a basin collects it; a floodplain is rebuilt every monsoon.
4. **Organisms** — roots, worms, microbes, forest litter. Humus is the dark gift of death.
5. **Time** — a Himalayan landslide soil is young. A laterite that has been leached for geological ages is old and exhausted of bases.

Indian soils are classified in the older geographical school you must still speak fluently: alluvial, black, red, laterite, arid, forest / mountain, saline-alkaline, peaty-marshy. The newer taxonomic series (Inceptisols, Vertisols, Alfisols…) appear in agriculture papers; for GS the classical map is still the scoring map.

## Alluvial soils

**Where.** The Northern Plain from Punjab to the Brahmaputra valley; deltas of Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery; some coastal strips; patches along peninsular rivers.

**How.** Himalayan and peninsular rivers drop silt, sand and clay. The Indo-Gangetic body is the largest alluvial tract in the country.

**Two textures inside the plain:**

- **Bhangar** — older alluvium, above the flood level, more clay, often kankar (calcareous nodules), less flood-renewed.
- **Khadar** — newer alluvium, floodplain, rebuilt by floods, finer, more fertile in the short run.

**Bhabar** is the porous pebble belt along the Himalayan foot where streams disappear. **Terai** is the wet, marshy, forested belt south of Bhabar where streams resurface. These are landforms that carry soil consequences; do not call Bhabar a soil type.

**Chemistry.** Generally loamy. Potash and lime are often adequate. Nitrogen and humus are often poor unless farmed with pulses, manure, or fertiliser. Colour is light grey to ash.

**Crops.** Intensive grain country: rice, wheat, sugarcane, jute in the wet east, oilseeds, vegetables. This soil plus canal plus monsoon is why the plain feeds the Republic.

## Black soils (regur)

**Where.** Deccan trap country: most of Maharashtra, west Madhya Pradesh, parts of Gujarat, north Karnataka, parts of Andhra and Telangana, a stretch of Tamil Nadu. The parent is **basalt** of the Deccan Traps.

**Feel.** Clayey, deep in valleys, shallow on uplands. They **swell when wet and crack when dry**. The cracks pull surface mulch down — a natural self-ploughing that textbooks love.

**Chemistry.** Rich in iron, lime, calcium, magnesium, potash, alumina. Poor in nitrogen, phosphorus, organic matter. Moisture-retentive. Colour black to deep grey because of titaniferous magnetite and humus complexes, not because the soil is always carbon-rich.

**Crops.** Cotton is the celebrity (hence “black cotton soil”). Also jowar, wheat in stored-moisture belts, linseed, castor, tobacco, citrus in some pockets. Irrigation must be careful: waterlogging and salinity follow lazy flooding of clay.

## Red soils

**Where.** Eastern and southern peninsula where crystalline igneous and metamorphic rocks weather: Tamil Nadu, parts of Karnataka, Andhra, Odisha, Chhotanagpur, parts of Madhya Pradesh and the north-east hills’ lower slopes.

**Why red.** Iron oxides coat the particles. Yellow soils are close cousins with more hydration of the iron.

**Chemistry.** Porous, friable, poor in nitrogen, phosphorus, humus, lime. Better on the lower grounds than on the eroded uplands. Slightly acidic in many belts.

**Crops.** Millets, groundnut, potato, tobacco, pulses, oilseeds; rice in low-lying pockets with water; plantation crops where rainfall and slope allow.

## Laterite soils

**Where.** High-rainfall, high-temperature sheets: Western Ghats summits and some eastern Ghats, parts of Odisha, West Bengal’s laterite shelf, Kerala, Karnataka, Meghalaya plateau, some Andhra and Tamil uplands.

**How.** Intense leaching removes silica and bases; iron and aluminium hydroxides remain. The word laterite is from *later* (brick): the soil hardens on exposure.

**Chemistry.** Poor in nitrogen, potash, lime, organic matter when the forest is gone. Acidic. Low fertility for grain unless manured and limed.

**Crops.** Cashew, rubber, tea, coffee, coconut on suitable lateritic landscapes; tapioca; millets on the poorer sheets. Bauxite and sometimes iron ore sit in lateritic profiles — a mineral sentence hiding inside a soil sentence.

## Arid and desert soils

**Where.** Western Rajasthan, and drier Gujarat–Haryana–Punjab margins.

**Feel.** Sandy to gravelly, low organic matter, high calcium carbonate in many profiles, phosphate variable, nitrogen poor. Wind sorts the grains. Saline patches appear where evaporation wins.

**Crops.** Where irrigation arrives (Indira Gandhi Canal belts), wheat, mustard, cotton, fodder. Without water: bajra, cluster bean, sparse grazing. Over-irrigation without drainage creates a new saline desert on top of the old sandy one.

## Forest and mountain soils

**Where.** Himalaya, Western and Eastern Ghats, north-east hills, some Central Indian uplands under forest.

**Feel.** Thin on steep slopes, thicker in valleys. High organic matter under living forest. Very mixed: acidic in high-rainfall coniferous belts, pod-like in some temperate stretches, rich brown loams in oak-rhododendron belts.

**Crops.** Terraced rice, maize, potato, temperate fruit, tea on eastern Himalayan slopes, spices on Western Ghats. Remove the forest and the soil leaves for Bangladesh or the Arabian Sea in one monsoon.

## Saline and alkaline soils (usar, reh, kallar)

**Where.** Irrigated tracts of the plains with poor drainage: parts of Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Rajasthan, patches of the Deccan and coastal Gujarat.

**How.** Capillary rise brings salts; evaporation leaves them; canal seepage without an exit path builds a white crust. Alkaline soils accumulate sodium that ruins structure.

**Repair.** Gypsum for alkaline (sodium) soils, leaching with drainage for saline soils, salt-tolerant varieties, no flood-irrigation on a closed basin. This is an economy problem disguised as chemistry.

## Peaty and marshy soils

**Where.** Kerala’s Kottayam–Alappuzha belt (kari soils), bits of coastal Odisha and West Bengal, some depressions of the north-east, the Sundarbans margins.

**Feel.** Heavy, black or bluish, high organic matter, often waterlogged, acidic, deficient in potash and phosphate in some kari profiles.

**Crops.** Rice where drained; otherwise reed and wetland ecology that should not be forced into wheat.

## A working map in one paragraph

Stand at Delhi and look east: alluvium to the Bay, jute and rice as rain increases. Look west: alluvium becomes drier, then arid sand. Look south from the Vindhya: black trap soil over Maharashtra, red over the older shield, laterite on the wet ghats. Look at every delta: alluvium again. Look at every steep Himalayan field: forest soil that is one tree-cut away from being a landslide.

## Soil degradation you must be able to explain

- **Erosion** — water on bare slope (Western Ghats, Shivalik, Chhotanagpur), wind in the Thar, river-bank cutting on Kosi and Brahmaputra.
- **Loss of humus** — cropping without rest, burning residues, no legumes.
- **Salinity and alkalinity** — irrigation without drainage.
- **Laterisation and acidification** — already-leached soils further mined of bases.
- **Contamination** — heavy metals around industrial belts, pesticide load in intensive plains.
- **Sealing** — cities pouring concrete on Class I alluvium. That is not development of land; it is retirement of the country’s best factory.

Conservation tools, as sentences not slogans: contour bunding and terracing on slopes; cover crops and mulch; shelter belts in arid tracts; check dams in gullies; gypsum and drainage in usar; forest on the steep; floodplain left as floodplain.

# BLOCK H — Paired block: soil economy, current affairs, ethics (25 minutes)

## Economy

Soil is the original capital good. Alluvial Punjab–Haryana–west UP is high-yield wheat-rice because soil plus canal plus procurement coincide. Black soil without drainage is a cotton story that becomes a salt story. Laterite without trees is a cashew-and-bauxite story. When a budget line funds “soil health cards,” the real verbs are sample, test, and then change the fertiliser ratio. A card that is not read is stationery.

Minimum support price and free power can push a farmer to mine nitrogen from alluvium and to lift water until the aquifer and the soil both collapse. That is not an argument against supporting farmers. It is an argument against supporting only two crops on every soil.

## Current affairs explained, tied to today

When a headline says child labour has risen in a cracker district before a festival, the static hook is Article 24 plus 21A. The economic hook is piece-rate households. The ethical hook is that a supply chain which needs a twelve-year-old’s fingers has priced the Constitution at zero.

When a State announces a survey of “forced conversions,” map it onto Article 25: propagation is protected; coercion is not. Ask whether the statute punishes force and fraud, or whether it punishes charity and marriage by paperwork designed to frighten.

When a minority college fights a fee regulator or a board-takeover, write Article 30 first, then recognition norms, then 29(2) if the college takes aid. Do not begin with a civilisational essay.

When a canal command turns white with reh, write soil chemistry before you write a new dam. Gypsum and drains are less photogenic than a reservoir ribbon.

When bonded workers are “rescued” and then listed in a press note with no land and no wage, Article 23 is unfinished. Release without rehabilitation is a photo.

## Ethics

A soil-conservation grant that is booked but not bunded is the same moral event as a false affidavit. The field does not read the utilisation certificate. A district officer who knows a kiln uses children and waits for a complaint in writing has chosen comfort over Article 24. Integrity here is not a quotation from a saint. It is an inspection that happens on a Sunday.

Riparian ethics yesterday; pedological ethics today: the upstream plough that leaves a slope naked taxes the downstream reservoir with silt. No section of the IPC is named “silt,” so the duty has to live in the officer.

# BLOCK I — CSAT: averages, mixtures, linear arrangement (40 minutes)

Work on paper first.

### Q1. Combined average

The average weight of 20 students is 48 kg. One student of 58 kg leaves and a student of 44 kg joins. New average?

**Solution.** Old total = 20 × 48 = 960. Change = −58 + 44 = −14. New total = 946. New average = 946 / 20 = 47.3 kg.

### Q2. Average of groups

Average of 15 girls = 40 marks. Average of 25 boys = 52 marks. Average of the class?

**Solution.** Total = 15×40 + 25×52 = 600 + 1300 = 1900. Students = 40. Average = 1900/40 = 47.5.

Do not average 40 and 52 to get 46. Different group sizes.

### Q3. Mixture

A 40-litre mixture is milk and water in the ratio 3:1. How many litres of water must be added to make the ratio 2:1?

**Solution.** Milk = 30 L, water = 10 L. Milk stays 30. For 2:1, water must be 15. Add 5 litres.

### Q4. Allegation (two strengths)

How many kg of rice at ₹36/kg should be mixed with 20 kg of rice at ₹48/kg so that the mix is worth ₹40/kg?

**Solution.** Allegation: cheaper 36, dearer 48, mean 40.  
Gaps: 48−40 = 8, 40−36 = 4. Ratio cheaper:dearer = 8:4 = 2:1.  
Dearer is 20 kg, so cheaper = 40 kg.

### Q5. Alligation with water as zero-price

A trader mixes water with milk bought at ₹50/L and sells at ₹50/L, gaining 25 percent. Ratio of milk to water?

**Solution.** Selling at cost price of milk but gaining 25% means 1.25 units are sold for the cost of 1 unit of milk. The extra 0.25 is water. Milk:water = 1 : 0.25 = 4:1.

Check: cost of 4 L milk = ₹200; mix = 5 L sold at ₹50 = ₹250; gain = ₹50 on ₹200 = 25%.

### Q6. Linear arrangement

Five officers — P, Q, R, S, T — sit in a row facing north.

1. R sits second to the right of P.
2. Q is at one end.
3. S sits between P and Q.
4. T is not adjacent to Q.

Who sits in the middle?

**Solution.** Ends are seats 1 and 5. Q is at an end. S between P and Q, so the block is Q–S–P or P–S–Q.

R is second to the right of P. Facing north, right from our left-to-right drawing is toward seat 5 if we number left to right 1→5. “Second to the right of P” means from P’s perspective: P’s right is east, which is toward seat 1 if they face north and we draw west-left? Careful.

If they face north, their right is east. Draw seats from west to east as 1–2–3–4–5 (left to right on paper = west to east). Then “right of P” = higher number.

R is two seats east of P. Possible P positions: 1 (R at 3), 2 (R at 4), 3 (R at 5).

S between P and Q.

Try Q at seat 1 (west end). Then S between P and Q forces P east of S, block 1-Q, 2-S, 3-P. Then R second to P’s right = seat 5. Seats: Q S P _ R. Remaining seat 4 = T. T is not adjacent to Q: T at 4 is not next to Q at 1. Good. Middle = P.

Try Q at seat 5. Block with S between P and Q: P S Q as seats 3-4-5. P at 3, R second to P’s right = seat 5, but 5 is Q. Contradiction. P S Q as 2-3-5 fails adjacency. Only contiguous between is 3-4-5. So Q cannot sit at 5.

Therefore only: Q S P T R. Middle = **P**.

### Q7. Comprehension

Read: “Article 24 does not need a child to file a writ. Article 32 was written so that someone else may carry the child’s name to a court. Soil is similar. The alluvium does not petition. A Republic that pours a township on khadar and then imports grain from a thinner red soil has used its best capital as a plot.”

Q7a. The author’s main claim is that (A) children should file more cases (B) rights and natural capital both need representatives because the bearer cannot speak (C) red soil is useless (D) Article 32 is only about land.

Answer: **B**.

Q7b. Supported inference? That urban conversion of fresh floodplain is a fiscal as well as an ecological error. Yes.

Q7c. Not supported? That laterite should replace alluvium in the Public Distribution System.

# BLOCK J — Active recall with model cores (25 minutes)

Close the file. Answer in a line. Then check.

1. Who is protected by Articles 23 and 24?
   **Core:** Persons, not only citizens.

2. What three things does 23 name?
   **Core:** Traffic in human beings, begar, and other similar forced labour; plus a public-purpose compulsory-service exception that cannot be caste-coded.

3. Age in Article 24?
   **Core:** Below fourteen, in factory, mine, or other hazardous employment.

4. Four verbs in Article 25?
   **Core:** Conscience; profess; practise; propagate — subject to public order, morality, health, and the rest of Part III.

5. Does 25 give a right to forcible conversion?
   **Core:** No. Propagation is exposition, not coercion.

6. What may the State regulate under 25(2)?
   **Core:** Secular activities tied to religion, and social reform including opening public Hindu institutions to all sections of Hindus.

7. Tax versus fee in Article 27?
   **Core:** No tax earmarked to promote a particular religion; a service fee is a different levy.

8. 29(1) is only for minorities — true or false?
   **Core:** False. Any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture.

9. Two kinds of minority in Article 30?
   **Core:** Religious and linguistic.

10. What is the Ninth Schedule’s post-1973 limit?
    **Core:** Laws inserted after 24 April 1973 can still be tested against basic structure (*I.R. Coelho*).

11. Five writs?
    **Core:** Habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto.

12. 32 versus 226 in one line?
    **Core:** 32 is a fundamental right to the Supreme Court for Part III; 226 is a High Court power for Part III and other legal rights.

13. Bhangar versus khadar?
    **Core:** Bhangar older, above flood, kankar; khadar new floodplain, more frequently renewed.

14. Why do black soils crack?
    **Core:** High clay; they swell wet and shrink dry; cracks self-mulch.

15. Laterite forms where?
    **Core:** High heat plus high rain; silica leached; iron and aluminium left; hardens on exposure.
