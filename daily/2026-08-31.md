# BLOCK A — What a Directive Principle is (and is not) (25 minutes)

Part IV is Articles **36 to 51**. Article **36** says “the State” has the same meaning as in Part III: the Union, the States, local authorities, and other authorities under the control of the Government of India. The addressee of the Directives is therefore the same giant that can violate a Fundamental Right — only now the Constitution is giving that giant a job list.

Article **37** is the hinge of the whole Part:

- The provisions in this Part shall **not be enforceable by any court**.
- The principles are nevertheless **fundamental in the governance of the country**.
- It shall be the **duty of the State to apply** these principles in making laws.

Three sentences, three exam traps:

1. **Not justiciable.** You cannot walk into the Supreme Court and demand a writ that forces the State to immediately create a living wage in every workshop. A Directive is not a Fundamental Right.
2. **Not optional poetry.** “Fundamental in the governance of the country” is a constitutional instruction to legislatures, executives, and planning bodies. A law that openly scorns a Directive is bad policy and, in some collisions with rights, a weak law.
3. **Applied while making laws.** The test of a Directive is whether statutes, budgets, and schemes point toward it. A speech on Republic Day is not application.

Why write unenforceable principles into a Constitution at all? Because the 1950 Republic was poor, unequal, and newly free. The Assembly could guarantee speech and equality at once. It could not, on 26 January 1950, guarantee a living wage, free legal aid in every taluk, or nutrition in every house. Directives are the **deferred half of justice**: the map of a welfare State that rights alone cannot draw.

**How Directives talk to Fundamental Rights.** Early judicial years treated rights as trumps and Directives as wishes. Later reading is more adult: both Parts are constitutional. A reasonable restriction on a right can be justified by a Directive. A law giving effect to **Article 39(b) and 39(c)** enjoys the **Article 31C** shield you met yesterday, subject to basic structure. Directives cannot authorise a law that smashes the basic structure or extinguishes Articles 14, 19 and 21 as if they were furniture.

**Classification you must speak**, even though the Constitution itself does not print these labels. Teachers group the Directives by ancestry and theme:

- **Socialistic** — welfare, work, living wage, distribution of resources, equal justice, public health, childhood protection (38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47).
- **Gandhian** — village panchayats, cottage industry, weaker rural sections, ban on intoxicating drinks, cow and other milch and draught cattle, organisation of agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines (40, 43, 46, 47, 48).
- **Liberal-intellectual / international** — uniform civil code, free and compulsory education (now partly lifted into 21A), agriculture and animal science, environment, protection of monuments, separation of judiciary from executive, international peace (44, 45, 48, 48A, 49, 50, 51).

Use the grouping to remember. Do not treat it as a Schedule of the Constitution.

**Amendments that changed Part IV after 1950:**

- **42nd Amendment, 1976** — inserted **39A** (equal justice and free legal aid), **43A** (worker participation in management), **48A** (environment and wildlife). The same amendment created Part IVA (Fundamental Duties).
- **44th Amendment, 1978** — added **38(2)** (minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities, opportunities).
- **86th Amendment, 2002** — recast **Article 45** toward early childhood care and education below six, because elementary education itself moved to Article 21A.
- **97th Amendment, 2011** — inserted **43B** (promotion of cooperative societies). (The amendment’s other parts on State cooperative law have had a complicated judicial life; for GS hold 43B as the Directive.)

# BLOCK B — Articles 38 to 43B, clause by clause (70 minutes)

## Article 38 — social order and inequality

**38(1).** The State shall strive to promote the welfare of the people by securing and protecting a social order in which **justice — social, economic and political** — shall inform all institutions of national life.

This is the Preamble’s justice sentence turned into a standing order for institutions: police station, panchayat, public sector unit, university, court.

**38(2)** (44th Amendment). The State shall, in particular, strive to **minimise inequalities in income**, and endeavour to eliminate inequalities in **status, facilities and opportunities**, not only among individuals but also among **groups of people residing in different areas or engaged in different vocations**.

Read the last phrase. Regional backwardness and occupational caste-like traps are named. A policy that only talks of individual merit and ignores a plateau district or a hereditary occupation has not read 38(2).

## Article 39 — the working principles of economic democracy

The State shall, in particular, direct its policy toward securing:

- **39(a)** — citizens, men and women equally, have the right to an **adequate means of livelihood**.
- **39(b)** — ownership and control of the **material resources of the community** are so distributed as best to **subserve the common good**.
- **39(c)** — the economic system does not result in the **concentration of wealth and means of production** to the common detriment.
- **39(d)** — **equal pay for equal work** for both men and women.
- **39(e)** — workers, men and women, and the **tender age of children** are not abused; citizens are not forced by economic necessity into avocations unsuited to their age or strength.
- **39(f)** — children are given opportunities and facilities to develop in a **healthy manner** and in conditions of **freedom and dignity**; childhood and youth are protected against exploitation and against moral and material abandonment.

**39(b) and 39(c)** are the two clauses that Article 31C specially shelters. “Material resources of the community” is a phrase courts have read widely — land, minerals, spectrum, natural gas — when the question is whether a nationalisation, a redistribution, or a licensing policy is aimed at common good rather than at a private clique. You do not need to memorise every case name today. You need the verb: **distribute so that the common good is served; do not let wealth clot**.

**39(d)** is the constitutional ancestor of equal-remuneration law. It is a Directive, not a writ by itself, but a pay scale that openly pays a woman less for the same work is a 14 + 15 + 39(d) problem when you write Mains.

**39(e) and 39(f)** sit next to Articles 23, 24 and 21A. Yesterday’s child in the kiln is today’s 39 sentence. When you pair them in an essay, you show you have read both Parts.

## Article 39A — equal justice and free legal aid

The State shall secure that the operation of the legal system promotes **justice, on a basis of equal opportunity**, and shall, in particular, provide **free legal aid**, by suitable legislation or schemes, to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of **economic or other disabilities**.

This is why legal services authorities exist. A right is a rumour if the bus fare to the High Court is a month’s wage. 39A is the Directive; the Legal Services Authorities framework is the statute that tries to obey it. Lok Adalats, duty counsel in criminal courts, and legal aid clinics are 39A made visible.

## Article 40 — village panchayats

The State shall take steps to organise village panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of **self-government**.

This is the Gandhian seed of Part IX. The 73rd Amendment later wrote panchayats into the operative Constitution. For today: 40 is the reason a three-tier rural local body is not a municipal afterthought. Self-government means more than a notice-board committee. It means power to plan, tax within law, and be answerable downward.

## Article 41 — work, education, public assistance

Within the **limits of its economic capacity and development**, the State shall make effective provision for securing the right to **work**, to **education**, and to **public assistance** in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement, and in other cases of undeserved want.

The italicised limit matters. 41 is not a blank cheque. It is a duty scaled to capacity — which is why employment guarantees, pensions, disability support, and scholarships are judged as direction of travel, not as overnight Scandinavian budgets. After Article 21A, education has a justiciable floor for ages 6–14; 41 remains the wider educational and social-security horizon.

## Article 42 — just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief

Short and sharp. Factories that have no drinking water, mines without ventilation, and maternity treated as a private inconvenience all fail 42. Maternity benefit law is 42 plus 15(3) plus 21.

## Article 43 — living wage and a decent standard of life

The State shall endeavour to secure, by suitable legislation or economic organisation or in any other way, to all workers — agricultural, industrial or otherwise — a **living wage**, conditions of work ensuring a **decent standard of life** and full enjoyment of leisure and social and cultural opportunities, and in particular to promote **cottage industries** on an individual or cooperative basis in rural areas.

Distinguish three wage ideas for Prelims and ethics:

- **Minimum wage** — the statutory floor below which extraction becomes forced labour territory (yesterday’s Article 23 reading).
- **Fair wage** — between minimum and living; capacity of industry matters.
- **Living wage** — enough for food, shelter, clothing, education of children, health, and a modest social life. Article 43 aims at living wage. India still often delivers, at best, a notified minimum.

Cottage industry in 43 is not nostalgia. It is rural non-farm work that can hold a household when the monsoon fails.

## Article 43A — workers’ participation in management

The State shall take steps, by suitable legislation or otherwise, to secure the participation of workers in the management of undertakings, establishments or other organisations engaged in any industry.

This is industrial democracy as a Directive, not a guaranteed board seat in every firm. Works committees, some public-sector participation experiments, and cooperative factories are the modest furniture of 43A.

## Article 43B — cooperative societies

The State shall endeavour to promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies.

Four adjectives: voluntary, autonomous, democratic, professional. A cooperative captured by a local notable is not 43B. A cooperative professionally run but emptied of members’ votes is not 43B either.

# BLOCK C — Articles 44 to 51 (60 minutes)

## Article 44 — uniform civil code

The State shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a **uniform civil code throughout the territory of India**.

Civil code here means the law of marriage, divorce, maintenance, adoption, succession — the private-law rules that today still largely follow personal laws of religious communities, plus secular optional statutes (a civil marriage law, a guardianship law, and so on).

What 44 is:

- a Directive to the State, not a self-executing code;
- about **citizens** and **civil** relations, not about worship;
- compatible with Articles 25–26 only if you remember yesterday’s split: **matters of religion** versus **secular aspects of personal status** that the State already regulates (age of marriage, monogamy in some codes, registration, cruelty as a ground of divorce).

What 44 is not:

- a licence to mock a community’s faith;
- a claim that every custom is already uniform;
- an excuse to ignore Article 14 when a personal law clause is openly arbitrary.

Goa’s civil code is the usual map pointer: a territory that inherited a common civil statute. It is not a finished national model, but it shows that uniformity of civil law is a possible Indian fact, not a European rumour.

When you write Mains, hold two honest poles: equality before law in the family, and the fear of minorities that “uniform” will mean “majoritarian.” A good answer names both and then asks whether the code is a set of justiciable family rights (consent, maintenance, child’s welfare, gender-equal inheritance) rather than a cultural conquest.

## Article 45 — early childhood care and education

After the 86th Amendment, Article 45 directs the State to endeavour to provide **early childhood care and education for all children until they complete the age of six years**.

The original 45 promised free and compulsory education up to fourteen within ten years of the commencement of the Constitution. That promise was only partly kept, and the justiciable piece moved to **21A**. What remains in 45 is the pre-school and care years: nutrition, health, and the first classroom. Pair with 39(f), 47, and the duty of parents in 51A(k).

## Article 46 — weaker sections, SCs, STs

The State shall promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the **weaker sections** of the people, and in particular of the **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes**, and shall protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation.

This is the Directive twin of Articles 15(4), 16(4), 17, 23 and 24. Reservation architecture lives in Part III and in Part XVI; the moral-policy sentence lives here. “Weaker sections” is wider than SC and ST; the clause still names SC and ST in particular.

## Article 47 — nutrition, living standard, public health; intoxicating drinks

Duty of the State to raise the **level of nutrition** and the **standard of living** and to improve **public health**. In particular, the State shall endeavour to bring about **prohibition of the consumption**, except for medicinal purposes, of **intoxicating drinks and of drugs** which are injurious to health.

Two layers: a broad public-health duty, and a Gandhian prohibition endeavour. States that run prohibition, States that tax liquor heavily, and States that fund nutrition missions are all arguing with 47 in different dialects. A dry law that creates a smuggling industry without reducing harm has not automatically fulfilled 47. Nutrition numbers (stunting, wasting, anaemia) are the unromantic test of the first sentence.

## Article 48 — agriculture, animal husbandry, cows

The State shall endeavour to organise **agriculture and animal husbandry** on modern and scientific lines and shall, in particular, take steps for preserving and improving the breeds, and prohibiting the **slaughter of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle**.

Read the whole article. Science and breed improvement are the first duty. The slaughter clause is particular and is about **milch and draught** cattle — an economic-agricultural sentence with a civilisational echo. State laws on cattle slaughter vary; the Directive is Union-level instruction, not a uniform criminal code. Prelims can ask the exact phrase: cows, calves, other milch and draught cattle.

## Article 48A — environment and wildlife

The State shall endeavour to protect and improve the **environment** and to safeguard the **forests and wild life** of the country.

Inserted by the 42nd Amendment. This is the constitutional hook for forest law, wildlife law, air and water law, and the later reading of **Article 21** as including a right to a healthy environment. 48A is the State’s duty; **51A(g)** will be the citizen’s duty. Always pair them.

## Article 49 — monuments and places of national importance

It shall be the obligation of the State to protect every monument or place or object of artistic or historic interest declared by or under law made by Parliament to be of **national importance**, from spoliation, disfigurement, destruction, removal, disposal or export.

This is why a centrally protected monument is not a quarry and not a private banquet hall. The Ancient Monuments framework is the statute. 49 is the reason the statute exists.

## Article 50 — separation of judiciary from executive

The State shall take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the **public services of the State**.

This is about the criminal-justice front line: the officer who investigates should not be the officer who sits as magistrate on the same facts. District administration historically fused both. Separation at the magisterial level is the Directive’s core. It is not a sentence about judicial review or about the collegium.

## Article 51 — international peace and law

The State shall endeavour to:

- promote **international peace and security**;
- maintain **just and honourable relations** between nations;
- foster respect for **international law and treaty obligations** in the dealings of organised peoples with one another;
- encourage **settlement of international disputes by arbitration**.

Article 51 is the outward face of the Republic. Treaty-making remains an executive act under the Union’s external-affairs power; 51 tells that executive to prefer peace, law, and arbitration over swagger. When you write about international organisations, humanitarian law, or a border crisis, 51 is the quiet constitutional citation.

# BLOCK D — Fundamental Duties, Article 51A (40 minutes)

Part IVA is a single article. It was born in the 42nd Amendment, drawing on the idea that rights without duties make a selfish citizen, and that several other constitutions list civic obligations. The **86th Amendment** added clause **(k)**.

**Who owes the duties?** “It shall be the duty of every **citizen** of India…” Non-citizens have many of yesterday’s rights (life, conscience, against traffic). Duties in 51A are addressed to **citizens**.

**Are they enforceable like Article 32?** No. There is no direct writ to punish a citizen for not “cherishing noble ideals.” But Parliament may create statutes that give a duty teeth (prevention of insults to the national honour, environment protection offences, compulsory education duties of parents). Courts have used Duties as **interpretive aids**: a restriction on a right may look more reasonable if it tracks a Duty; a petitioner who comes with unclean civic hands may find a cooler bench.

## The eleven clauses, in working English

**(a)** Abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the **National Flag** and the **National Anthem**.

Not a demand that you sing on command in every private room. A demand that the public symbols of the Republic are not treated as rags. Statutory honour law sits beside this clause.

**(b)** Cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired our **national struggle for freedom**.

A historical-moral clause. In an essay it supports non-violence as method, equality as aim, and anti-colonial dignity. It is not a ban on criticising a freedom-era leader.

**(c)** Uphold and protect the **sovereignty, unity and integrity** of India.

The same triad you met in the Preamble and in the reasonable restrictions on Article 19. Secessionist speech and assistance to armed rebellion collide here.

**(d)** Defend the country and render **national service** when called upon to do so.

Pairs with Article 23’s exception for compulsory public service. A lawful call-up is not *begar*.

**(e)** Promote **harmony** and the spirit of common brotherhood among all the people of India, transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; **renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women**.

Two duties in one clause: fraternity in action, and a direct sentence against practices that degrade women. Dowry display, sex-selective abortion as a social habit, and workplace humiliation are 51A(e) problems as well as 14, 15 and 21 problems.

**(f)** Value and preserve the rich **heritage of our composite culture**.

Composite is the load-bearing word. Heritage is not one community’s museum. A monument of any community that is part of the Indian story sits inside (f) and inside 49.

**(g)** Protect and improve the **natural environment** including forests, lakes, rivers and wild life, and to have **compassion for living creatures**.

The citizen-side twin of 48A. Planting a tree is the poster. Not dumping construction debris in a lake is the adult version. Compassion for living creatures is the constitutional vocabulary for cruelty-prevention law.

**(h)** Develop the **scientific temper**, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform.

This is the anti-superstition clause written without naming a cult. A civil servant who substitutes astrology for flood modelling has failed 51A(h) in office. Humanism here means concern for the person, not a war on temples.

**(i)** Safeguard **public property** and to abjure violence.

A burnt bus after a riot is a 51A(i) exhibit. So is a stolen municipal cover that becomes a manhole death.

**(j)** Strive towards **excellence** in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement.

Easy to mock; useful in GS-IV. Mediocrity as a work culture in a public office is a duty failure, not only a performance-appraisal failure.

**(k)** A parent or guardian shall provide opportunities for **education** to his child or, as the case may be, ward between the age of **six and fourteen years**.

Inserted with Article 21A. The State must provide the school; the parent must not sell the child’s hours to a loom. Pair 21A + 24 + 45 + 51A(k) whenever the question is a child.

**Swaran Singh committee** recommended Duties; the 42nd Amendment enacted ten; the 86th added the eleventh. A few recommended duties (for example a tax-payment duty in some committee drafts) did not enter 51A. Do not invent a twelfth clause in the exam hall.

# BLOCK E — How to write DPSPs and Duties in Mains (15 minutes)

A GS-II core on justiciability:

> Part IV is not a petition. It is a standard by which laws and budgets can be judged as facing toward justice or away from it. Article 37 forbids the court from executing a Directive as if it were a decree, and it forbids the State from treating the Directive as wallpaper.

A GS-II core on rights versus Directives:

> Fundamental Rights protect the person from the State. Directive Principles tell the State what to build for the person. Harmony is the mature reading: a restriction on a right is easier to defend when it honestly serves a Directive; a Directive cannot be used to hollow out equality, speech, or life.

A GS-II core on Article 44:

> A uniform civil code is a Directive toward equal civil rights in the family, not a project of liturgical uniformity. The test of any draft is whether a woman and a child can claim maintenance, consent, and inheritance without the law changing with the household’s god.

A GS-IV core on Duties:

> Article 51A does not replace the criminal code. It names the citizen the Constitution needs: one who will not burn a bus, starve a child of school, poison a lake, or treat a woman as a procession ornament. An officer who waits for a writ before doing 51A(g) has already failed 51A(j).

# BLOCK F — Natural vegetation of India (80 minutes)

Vegetation is what climate, soil, relief, and human use allow to stand. Classify first by **moisture and temperature**, then pin it to the map, then name the human wound.

## What controls the cover

1. **Rainfall** — the master key. Rough working thresholds: above 200 cm supports wet evergreen; 100–200 cm moist deciduous; 70–100 cm dry deciduous; 50–70 cm thorn and scrub; below 50 cm desert and semi-desert. These are teaching bands, not fences.
2. **Temperature and altitude** — a Himalayan slope repeats the latitudes in a few kilometres: tropical foothill, temperate oak and deodar, alpine birch and rhododendron, then snow.
3. **Soil** — yesterday’s laterite under rain becomes a tea or moist forest story; arid sand becomes thorn; waterlogged peat becomes mangrove or reed.
4. **Drainage and salinity** — Sundarbans are not “just wet.” They are tidal and salt-tolerant.
5. **People** — fire, graze, fell, plough, plant teak in rows, and call the result a forest in a file.

## Tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen

**Where.** Western Ghats (heavy windward side), Andaman and Nicobar, upper Assam and other north-eastern tracts where rain is high and the dry season is short. Pockets of the eastern Himalayan foothills.

**Look.** Tall, dense, multi-storeyed, little seasonal leaf-shed. Sun barely reaches the floor. Lianas, epiphytes, thick undergrowth.

**Trees you should be able to name.** Rosewood, mahogany, ain, mesua, white cedar, toon in some belts, ironwood; dipterocarps in the north-east and islands. Rubber and some spices are planted into this climate, which is not the same as saying they are the natural climax everywhere.

**Semi-evergreen** sits on the slightly drier margins of the same regions: a mix of evergreen and deciduous giants, still tall, still wet.

**Use and wound.** Precious timber invited selective felling. Roads through a Ghats valley dry the understorey. A dam in a Western Ghat gorge floods a gene bank that does not exist on the Deccan plateau.

## Tropical moist deciduous

**Where.** A wide belt: eastern slopes of the Western Ghats, a good part of the north-east’s less-wet tracts, Chhotanagpur, Odisha, West Bengal’s lateritic and plateau margins, the Terai-Duars, foothill Himalaya, and parts of the eastern peninsula where rain is still generous.

**Look.** They **shed leaves in the dry season**. Canopy is high but not as closed as evergreen. This is the country’s working timber forest in the older economic sense.

**Trees.** Teak in the west-central moist belts, sal (*Shorea robusta*) in a broad north and east band (Terai, Chhotanagpur, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh–Chhattisgarh sal belt), shisham, sandal in specified drier-moist pockets, bamboo as under-storey and as a separate economic plant.

**Sal versus teak, one line.** Sal likes well-drained but not arid ground in the north and east; teak is the celebrity of peninsular moist deciduous, especially on trap and good drainage in the west-central belt. Do not plant them in the same mental district without the map.

## Tropical dry deciduous

**Where.** The largest share of India’s forest *area* in the classical map: much of the peninsula inland, rain-shadow Maharashtra and Karnataka, interior Tamil Nadu, western Odisha and Chhattisgarh margins, Uttar Pradesh–Madhya Pradesh Vindhyan country, parts of Punjab-Haryana Siwalik edge.

**Look.** Lower canopy, longer leafless period, grassy floor, fire as a regular visitor.

**Trees.** Teak in the better dry-deciduous pockets, axlewood, palas (flame of the forest), tendu (whose leaf is the bidi wrapper — an economy hidden in a forest type), khair, babul on the dry edge.

## Thorn forest and scrub

**Where.** Rajasthan west of the Aravalli rain-catch, north Gujarat, rain-shadow interiors, the drier Deccan, parts of the southern rain-shadow.

**Look.** Acacia, euphorbia, cactus-like forms, far-spaced trees, seasonal grass. Leaves are small; thorns are an argument with goats and drought.

**Trees and shrubs.** Babul, ber, date palm in oases and depressions, khejri (*Prosopis cineraria*) in Rajasthan — the tree of the community landscape, not a jungle giant.

## Desert vegetation

**Where.** Thar. Stunted, very open, sand-binding grasses, shrubs with deep roots. Irrigation canals invent a new vegetation of crops and weeds; they do not invent a rainforest.

## Montane vegetation — Himalaya

Climb in belts:

- **Foothills / Bhabar-Terai to lower hills** — moist deciduous, sal in the west-central and eastern foothills, tall grass in Terai.
- **Eastern Himalaya lower slopes** — wetter, more evergreen, dense, species-rich; tea on cleared slopes.
- **Temperate** — oak, chestnut, chir pine in the west; deodar (Himalayan cedar) in the western Himalaya; rhododendron increases eastward; wet temperate forests in the east are mossy and thick.
- **Alpine** — birch, juniper, rhododendron scrub, then alpine meadow (*bugyal* in the west, high pasture in the east), then snow.
- **Tree line** falls as you go west and as the slope dries; it is not a single contour for the whole range.

Western Himalaya is relatively drier and more coniferous on many outer slopes. Eastern Himalaya is wetter, more broad-leaved, more bio-diverse. That one contrast earns marks.

## Montane vegetation — peninsula

Nilgiri, Anamalai, Palani, and other high Western Ghat shoulders carry **shola** forests (patches of stunted evergreen in folds) and **grasslands** on the exposed summits. These are not Himalayan copies. They are island ecosystems on a tropical mountain. Tea and plantation forestry have eaten many shola edges.

## Tidal / mangrove forests

**Where.** Sundarbans (West Bengal and across the border), Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery deltas, Andaman coasts, pockets of Gujarat’s gulf shores, Kerala backwater margins in fragments.

**Look.** Salt-tolerant trees on stilts: pneumatophores (breathing roots) in species that need them, vivipary (seeds that germinate on the parent) in some mangroves. Sundari (*Heritiera*) gave the Sundarbans a name. The tiger of the Sundarbans is a mangrove fact as well as a wildlife fact.

**Job.** Cyclone shield, fish nursery, carbon store, silt trap. Cutting a mangrove to extend a shrimp pond is a coastal-defence failure dressed as exports.

## Littoral and swamp

Coastal casuarina belts (often planted), deltaic swamp forests, freshwater swamps of Kerala and the north-east. Do not crush all of these into “mangrove.” Freshwater swamp is a different chemistry.

## Grasslands

India’s grasslands are easy to miss because textbooks worship trees.

- **Terai-Duars** tall grass — rhinoceros and elephant country when the grass is not claimed by a sugar mill.
- **Black-soil savanna patches** in the peninsula.
- **Alpine meadows**.
- **Banni** and other arid grasslands in Kachchh.
- **Shola grasslands** of the high south.

Calling every grassland a “wasteland” in a revenue file is how a habitat disappears.

## Forest cover as an exam sentence, not a brochure

Distinguish three official ideas when a headline quotes a percentage:

- **Forest cover** — canopy as seen from a satellite, including plantations and sometimes orchards if they meet the canopy rule.
- **Recorded forest area** — what the forest department’s books call forest, which may be bare.
- **Dense / moderately dense / open** — canopy-density classes.

A rising forest-cover number can hide a falling natural forest if plantations replace shola or if tea and rubber are counted with a generous eye. Write the distinction. Do not fight the latest percentage in this file; fight the confusion of terms.

## Degradation, fire, inversion of use

- **Fragmentation** — a highway, a mine, a transmission line. Animals need a corridor; files need a hectare.
- **Fuelwood and fodder pressure** — a real rural energy problem, not a morality play. LPG and a well-run community forest reduce axes; sermons do not.
- **Invasive species** — lantana, prosopis in some grasslands, water hyacinth in lakes.
- **Monoculture forestry** — teak or eucalyptus rows that look green and live poorly.
- **Diversion** — forest land for mining and dams. Compensatory afforestation on a different, drier plot does not replace a Western Ghat catchment.

Conservation tools as verbs: notify and then actually protect; joint forest management that shares tendu and bamboo income; sacred groves as living 51A(f) and 51A(g); mangrove restoration on mud that can take a mangrove, not on a tourist brochure; fire lines that are cut before summer, not after the satellite sees the burn.

# BLOCK G — Paired block: forest economy, current affairs, ethics (25 minutes)

## Economy

Natural vegetation is GDP that does not always send an invoice: pollination, monsoon recycling through forest evapotranspiration, silt held on a slope so that a dam lives longer, fish that begin life among mangrove roots, tendu leaves, lac, resin, cane, medicinal bark, grazing. A district that books only timber has under-counted its forest.

Teak and sal were colonial and post-colonial cash. Tendu is still a livelihood for millions of tribal collectors. Bamboo is a construction and paper plant that law has sometimes treated as a tree and sometimes as a grass — the classification changes who may harvest. When a State auctions a tendu season, Article 46 and the forest right of the collector are the constitutional companions of the price.

## Current affairs explained, tied to today

When a court or a regulator pauses a project in a Western Ghat valley, the static hook is 48A + 21 + the vegetation type (evergreen catchment), not a generic “green versus growth” cartoon. Ask what hydrological service the slope performed.

When a State tightens or loosens a cattle-slaughter rule, map Article 48 exactly: scientific husbandry plus milch and draught cattle. Then write the federal fact: the criminal detail is largely State law.

When a government speaks of a uniform civil code draft, begin with Article 44, add 14 and 15, add 25’26 as yesterday taught them, and ask whether the draft is a set of justiciable family claims. Do not begin with a civilisational war.

When legal-aid clinics are understaffed, that is Article 39A failing in furniture. A Directive dies in the taluk court more often than in the textbook.

When forest-cover statistics are released, perform the three-term split above before you celebrate or denounce.

When a child is out of school in a tendu-picking season, 51A(k), 21A, 24 and 46 arrive together.

## Ethics

A forest officer who marks a standing shola as “open scrub” to ease a diversion has lied in a language only the slope will punish. A citizen who burns a State bus to protest a recruitment paper has failed 51A(i) and then asks the same State for a job. A minister who quotes 51A(h) scientific temper and funds an untested miracle cure has used the clause as incense.

Duty ethics is not scolding the poor for needing firewood. It is designing a fuel and forest-right system in which 51A(g) is possible. Integrity for the officer is the inspection that matches the notification. Integrity for the citizen is the refuse that does not enter the lake.

# BLOCK H — CSAT: percentages, simple interest, seating and statements (40 minutes)

Work on paper first.

### Q1. Successive percentage

A forest plot produces 800 cubic metres. Output falls 20 percent in a drought year and rises 25 percent the next year. Final output?

**Solution.** After 20% fall: 800 × 0.8 = 640. After 25% rise: 640 × 1.25 = 800. Back to start. Successive −20% and +25% on the new base cancel here because 0.8 × 1.25 = 1.

A 20% fall is not undone by a 20% rise: 800 × 0.8 × 1.2 = 768. Always multiply.

### Q2. Percentage of a percentage

40 percent of a village is tribal. 25 percent of the tribals collect tendu. Collectors as a percentage of the village?

**Solution.** 0.40 × 0.25 = 0.10 = **10%**.

### Q3. Population growth as compound

A town of 50,000 grows at 8 percent a year for 2 years. Population after 2 years?

**Solution.** 50,000 × (1.08)² = 50,000 × 1.1664 = **58,320**.

### Q4. Simple interest

₹8,000 at 7.5 percent simple interest for 4 years. Interest and amount?

**Solution.** SI = PRT/100 = 8000 × 7.5 × 4 / 100 = 8000 × 30 / 100 = **₹2,400**. Amount = **₹10,400**.

### Q5. Finding the rate

A sum becomes ₹6,360 in 3 years at simple interest from ₹5,300. Rate?

### Q7. Circular seating

Six ministries — P, Q, R, S, T, U — sit around a circular table facing the centre.

1. P sits second to the left of R.
2. Q sits opposite P.
3. T is immediately clockwise from Q.
4. S is not adjacent to R.

Who sits opposite T?

**Solution.** Facing centre, “left” of a person is clockwise around the table if we imagine their left hand. Standard CSAT convention: when all face centre, **right = clockwise, left = anti-clockwise** from the person’s view… Verify from the person: facing inward, their right hand points clockwise. Yes: **immediate right = clockwise; immediate left = anti-clockwise**.

“Second to the left of R” = two seats anti-clockwise from R is P.

Draw seats 1–6 clockwise. Put R at seat 1. Anti-clockwise is 6, 5, 4… So second to the left of R = seat 5 = P.

Opposite of P (seat 5) is seat 2. Q at 2.

T immediately clockwise from Q: clockwise from 2 is 3. T at 3.

Opposite T (seat 3) is seat 6.

S is not adjacent to R (seat 1). Adjacent to R: seats 2 and 6. Seat 2 is Q, so S must not be at 6. Remaining seats 4 and 6 for S and U. S cannot be at 6, so **S at 4, U at 6**.

Opposite T is seat 6 = **U**.
