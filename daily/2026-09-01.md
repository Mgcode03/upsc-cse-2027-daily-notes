# BLOCK A — Why a Constitution is hard to change, and still changeable (20 minutes)

A Constitution that cannot change dies. A Constitution that changes like an ordinary statute is not a Constitution. Article **368** is the locked door with three keys of different weight.

**Where 368 lives.** Part XX. The heading is “Amendment of the Constitution.” The article both *creates the power* and *prescribes the procedure*. That double job matters after the basic-structure cases: Parliament amends *under* 368; it does not sit outside the Constitution as a sovereign convention that can tear the book up.

**What is not an “amendment” in the 368 sense.** Ordinary laws change every week. A Finance Act is not an amendment of the Constitution. Some constitutional *changes of fact* also happen without 368: admitting a new State under Article 2, or forming a new State and altering boundaries under Article 3 (with the special process of a President’s reference to the State legislature, which is *not* a veto). Article 4 says laws under Articles 2 and 3 may amend the First and Fourth Schedules and “shall not be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of Article 368.” So: **new State on the map ≠ 368 amendment**, even if a Schedule is rewritten.

**Who may start a 368 amendment?** Only a **Bill in either House of Parliament**. State legislatures cannot introduce a Constitution Amendment Bill. (They *ratify* some Bills later. They do not originate them.)

**The President cannot refuse a 368 Bill that has completed the prescribed parliamentary (and, where needed, State) process.** After the 24th Amendment, Article 368 says the President *shall* give assent. There is no pocket-veto story here of the kind you will meet for ordinary Bills.

# BLOCK B — Three ladders of amendment (50 minutes)

Speak three ladders. The Constitution does not print the labels “simple / special / special-plus-ratification,” but every Prelims paper expects you to sort provisions onto the correct ladder.

## Ladder 1 — Simple majority of Parliament (not 368)

These look “constitutional” because they live in the Constitution, yet they are changed by an **ordinary law**: majority of members present and voting, subject to quorum, like any other Bill. Examples you must sort correctly:

- Admission or creation of States; alteration of names, areas, boundaries (Arts 2, 3, 4).
- Creation or abolition of Legislative Councils in States (Article 169) — Parliament acts if the State Assembly so resolves by a special majority of *that Assembly*.
- Quorum of Parliament, salaries of MPs (within the constitutional frame), rules of procedure, use of English in courts for a period, certain Schedule changes that the text itself allows by law.
- Abolition or creation of a High Court for a Union Territory, in specified ways.
- Number of puisne judges of the Supreme Court can be raised by law (the Court is in the Constitution; the *headcount above the floor* is statutory).

**Exam trap.** “Everything printed in the Constitution needs 368.” False.

## Ladder 2 — Special majority in each House (the default 368 path)

Article 368(2): a Bill must be passed in **each House** by

1. a majority of the **total membership** of that House, **and**
2. a majority of **not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**.

Two numbers, both required.

Work an example so the arithmetic never betrays you. Suppose Lok Sabha effective total membership for this purpose is 543. Majority of total membership = 272. Suppose 400 members are present and voting. Two-thirds of 400 = 266.67, so 267. The Bill needs **at least 272** in this House because the *total-membership* test is the higher of the two tonight. If instead only 300 are present, two-thirds of 300 = 200, but you still need 272 — you simply cannot pass it with 300 in the House unless 272 of them say yes. If the House is full at 543, two-thirds of 543 = 362, and 362 is the binding number because it already exceeds 272.

**Each House separately.** There is **no joint sitting** for a Constitution Amendment Bill. If Rajya Sabha stalls it, the Bill stalls. That is deliberate rigidity.

**Most of the Constitution** travels this ladder: Fundamental Rights, DPSPs, the Union-State legislative lists in ordinary course, the chapter on the President and Parliament as institutions (except the federal entrenched bits below), emergency provisions as text, and so on — unless a clause is separately listed for ratification.

## Ladder 3 — Special majority **plus ratification by States**

If the Bill touches certain **federal** provisions, it must also be ratified by the legislatures of **not less than one-half of the States** by a *simple* resolution of those legislatures. No special majority inside the State House is written. After half the States have ratified, the Bill goes to the President for the mandatory assent.

**The entrenched list** (learn it as a family, then as clauses):

- The manner of **election of the President** (Arts 54, 55).
- The extent of the **executive power of the Union** and of the **States** (Arts 73, 162).
- The **Supreme Court** and the **High Courts** in the States (Chapter IV of Part V; Chapter V of Part VI).
- The distribution of **legislative powers** between Union and States (Chapter I of Part XI).
- The **Seventh Schedule** lists.
- Representation of States in **Parliament**.
- Article **368** itself.

Why this family? Because these are the joints of the federation. A Union Parliament that could, by its own two-thirds, empty a State List or pack the Rajya Sabha formula or rewrite 368 would not need the States in the room. The ratification rule keeps the States in the room.

**Union Territories do not count as States for this half.** The denominator is States.

**No time limit** is written in 368 for State ratification. Practice has been political time, not a constitutional stopwatch.

# BLOCK C — Basic structure: the lock on the lock (40 minutes)

## The problem 368 created

If Parliament can amend “any provision,” can it repeal Article 32? Delete secularism from the Preamble? Make the Prime Minister unremovable? Convert India into a unitary autocracy with a Constitution-shaped cover?

Early judicial years were divided. One view: 368 is plenary; an amendment is not a “law” that Article 13 can strike. Opposite view: the word “amend” means improve, not destroy.

## The settled teaching line you must speak

Parliament **may amend any part** of the Constitution, including Part III, **but cannot damage or destroy the basic structure** of the Constitution. An amendment is intra vires 368 only if the identity of the Constitution survives.

This is judge-made discipline of a written Constitution. It is not a printed Schedule titled “Basic Structure.” Different benches have named different limbs. You memorise the **recurring limbs**, not a fake official list:

- Supremacy of the Constitution
- Sovereign, democratic, republican form
- Secular character
- Separation of powers
- Federal character
- Judicial review
- Rule of law
- Free and fair elections
- Independence of the judiciary
- Articles 32 and 226 as meaningful remedies (the writ spine)
- Harmony of Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
- Limited amending power itself (Parliament cannot use 368 to kill 368’s limits)
- Welfare-state / egalitarian features as part of the constitutional identity in later articulation
- Dignity of the individual; unity and integrity of the nation

**How to use this in Prelims.** A question that says “basic structure is listed in Article X” is false. A question that says “Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights at all” is also false — it *can*, so long as the structure holds. The 24th Amendment wrote that 368 extends to Part III; the judicial rider is *how far*.

**How to use this in Mains.** Do not dump twelve nouns. Pick two limbs that fit the question (say, judicial review + federalism) and show *how* a hypothetical amendment would snap them. Basic structure is a test of identity, not a veto on every welfare experiment.

## Article 31C again, in today’s language

You already know 31C shelters laws giving effect to 39(b) and 39(c). An attempt to widen that shield to *all* Directives, and to bar judicial review of whether the law even related to the Directive, collided with basic structure. Hold the working rule: **Directive-serving laws may restrict rights within the surviving 31C design; they may not exile the court.**

## The 42nd Amendment and the reply

The 42nd Amendment (1976) tried to swell 368 and shrink review. The 44th Amendment (1978) walked a good part of that back, including the emergency and property story you met with Article 300A. For Day 6, one sentence is enough: **the political system itself treated an over-swollen amending power as a wound**, which is why 44 exists.

# BLOCK D — The President: election, tenure, removal (55 minutes)

## The office

Article **52**: there shall be a President of India. Article **53**: the **executive power of the Union** is vested in the President and is exercised by him **either directly or through officers subordinate to him**, in accordance with the Constitution. That “in accordance with the Constitution” is the whole theory of a parliamentary executive: the President is the formal head; the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister is the political head (Article 74 — you will get the Council in full on Day 8; today you need the hinge).

Article **74(1)** as it now stands: there *shall* be a Council of Ministers with the PM at the head to **aid and advise** the President, who **shall**, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice. The President may require the Council to **reconsider** the advice; he must then act on the advice tendered after such reconsideration. So: **one return ticket, not a second government.**

Article **74(2)**: the advice shall not be inquired into in any court. That is secrecy of Cabinet counsel, not a magic cloak for unconstitutional acts done in the President’s name.

## Qualifications (Article 58)

- Citizen of India.
- **35 years** completed.
- Qualified for election as a member of the **Lok Sabha**.
- Not holding any office of profit under the Union, a State, or a local or other authority subject to the control of any of those governments — except offices the Constitution itself exempts (outgoing President, Vice-President, Governor, Minister of Union or State are not treated as offices of profit for this bar).

Article **59**: the President shall not be a member of either House of Parliament or of a State legislature (seat becomes vacant on entering office); shall not hold any other office of profit; shall be entitled to the official residence, emoluments and allowances fixed by Parliament, which shall not be diminished during the term.

## Election (Articles 54 and 55) — federal and proportional

The President is elected by an **electoral college** of

- elected members of both Houses of **Parliament**, and
- elected members of the **Legislative Assemblies of the States**, including, by later constitutional adjustment, the Assemblies of **Delhi and Puducherry**.

**Not in the college:** nominated members of Rajya Sabha or of Assemblies; members of Legislative Councils; elected members of other Union Territory boards that are not written in.

**Why weighted votes?** To keep the federal bargain: the all-India House strength and the all-State Assembly strength should pull with roughly equal total weight, and bigger States should not drown tiny States by raw MLA headcount alone.

**Value of an MLA’s vote** (the formula you must be able to write):

\[
\text{Value of an MLA vote in a State} = \frac{\text{Population of the State}}{\text{Elected MLAs of the State} \times 1000}
\]

rounded to the nearest whole number (0.5 and above upward).

**Value of an MP’s vote**:

\[
\text{Value of an MP vote} = \frac{\text{Total value of votes of all elected MLAs of all States}}{\text{Total elected MPs (Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha)}}
\]

again rounded.

Population here has been frozen to a specified census year by constitutional practice so that States that controlled population are not punished in the college. For exam writing: name the *formula*, do not invent a live population figure unless a question supplies one.

**System of voting.** Proportional representation by means of the **single transferable vote**, and voting is **secret**. Each elector marks preferences 1, 2, 3… Quota is determined by the usual STV formula for a single-seat election:

\[
\text{Quota} = \frac{\text{Valid votes}}{2} + 1
\]

(the integer form used for one seat). Lower preferences transfer if the first-preference leader has not reached quota.

**Disputes** over the President’s election go only to the **Supreme Court** (Article 71). Parliament may regulate other connected matters by law.

## Term, vacancy, succession

- Term: **five years** from the date of entering office (Article 56).
- Eligible for **re-election** (Article 57) — no printed two-term cap of the American kind.
- May resign to the **Vice-President**.
- May be removed by **impeachment** for **violation of the Constitution** (Article 61).
- Holds office until a successor enters, so the chair is never empty by mere expiry of five years.
- A vacancy by death, resignation, removal, or otherwise: election “as soon as possible,” and in any case within **six months**.
- When the President is unable to discharge functions (absence, illness, vacancy until a new President enters), the **Vice-President** acts. If the Vice-President’s office is also vacant, Parliament has provided that the **Chief Justice of India**, or in his absence the senior-most available Supreme Court judge, acts as President. That last step is statutory contingency, not a third elected office.

## Impeachment (Article 61)

This is not a no-confidence motion. It is a quasi-judicial removal for **violation of the Constitution**.

1. Charge may begin in **either House**.
2. The proposal must be signed by **not less than one-fourth** of the total membership of that House, and **14 days’ notice** must be given.
3. The initiating House must pass the charge by **two-thirds of the total membership** of that House.
4. The other House **investigates**, or causes investigation. The President has the right to appear and to be represented.
5. If the investigating House also sustains the charge by **two-thirds of its total membership**, the President is removed from the date of that passing.

Note the majority: **two-thirds of total membership**, not the 368 mix of total-plus-two-thirds-of-present. Nominated members vote here; this is a House acting as a court-like body, not an electoral college.

No President has been impeached. You still learn the machinery, because Prelims loves unused machinery.

## Privileges and protection

Article **361**: the President (and a Governor) is not answerable to any court for the exercise of official powers, subject to the impeachment process and to the rule that a court may look at official acts when the Constitution so requires through other doors (for example, the validity of an order can still be tested against the Constitution even if the person is not personally sued). No criminal proceeding can be instituted or continued against the President in office; no process for arrest or imprisonment may issue from a court while in office. Civil proceedings for personal acts need two months’ written notice. This is official immunity, not a lifetime pardon for private crime after leaving office.

# BLOCK E — Powers of the President, as working verbs (60 minutes)

Do not memorise a brochure titled “executive / legislative / financial / judicial / emergency.” Memorise **what actually moves**. Emergency powers get their own day (Day 10). Today you need the ordinary chair.

## Executive and military

- All Union executive action is expressed to be taken **in the name of the President** (Article 77). Rules of business allocate the real file to ministries.
- Appoints the **Prime Minister**, and on PM’s advice the other Ministers; they hold office during the pleasure of the President (Article 75) — pleasure that, in a living parliamentary system, follows Commons confidence, not palace mood.
- Appoints the Attorney-General, CAG, Governors, Chairperson and members of UPSC, Election Commissioners, Finance Commission, official language commission machinery, special officers for SCs/STs/linguistic minorities as provided, and other constitutional offices the text names.
- Appoints judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts **as per the constitutional process now in force** (collegium-plus-warrant practice). You will do the judiciary on Day 9; today: the warrant carries the President’s name.
- Supreme commander of the **Defence Forces** (Article 53(2)). Declarations of war and peace, and deployment, are executive acts in the President’s name on Cabinet advice. Parliament still has to finance and, by law, regulate the Forces.

**Discretion that still matters.** In a hung Lok Sabha the President must pick as PM the person most likely to command confidence, then test that person on the floor without undue delay. That is not a second political programme. It is a constitutional referee function. Dismissal of a ministry that still has confidence would be a coup dressed as pleasure. Dismissal of a ministry that has lost the House and refuses to go is the opposite duty.

## Legislative

- Summons each House, prorogues, and may **dissolve Lok Sabha** (Article 85).
- Addresses a joint sitting at the first session after each general election and at the first session of each year (Article 87).
- May send **messages** to either House (Article 86).
- Nominates **12 members to Rajya Sabha** (literature, science, art, social service) and, if the Anglo-Indian nomination provision is in force in the form the current text and later amendment history leave it, the Lok Sabha nomination story must be stated as it legally stands *on the date of the paper*. Teaching line for 2027: the 104th Amendment ended the Anglo-Indian reserved *elected/nominated special seats* in the Lok Sabha and the State Assemblies as a running special representation; do not write the old “two Anglo-Indian nominees” as if they still sit. Rajya Sabha’s 12 cultural nominees remain.
- A Bill becomes an Act only on **assent**. For an ordinary Bill the President may assent, withhold assent, or **return once** (if it is not a Money Bill) for reconsideration. If Parliament passes it again, with or without change, the President **shall assent**. A Money Bill cannot be returned.
- Certain State Bills reserved by a Governor come to the President: assent, withhold, or direct the Governor to return the Bill to the State legislature.

## Ordinance power (Article 123)

When **both Houses are not in session**, and the President is satisfied that circumstances require immediate action, he may promulgate an **ordinance**. It has the force of an Act of Parliament. It must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble, and it ceases at the expiry of **six weeks from reassembly** unless earlier disapproved, or it may fail sooner if a disapproval resolution is passed. An ordinance can be withdrawn. An ordinance cannot do what Parliament cannot do (for example, it cannot amend the Constitution under 368).

**Satisfaction** is the Cabinet’s satisfaction in ordinary times. Courts have been willing, in later reading, to check *mala fide* or the fraud of a permanent ordinance-raj that bypasses the House by serial re-promulgation. An ordinance is an emergency lamp for a dark interval between sessions, not a parallel Parliament.

## Financial

- No Money Bill without the President’s **prior recommendation**.
- The **Annual Financial Statement** (Budget) is laid in the President’s name.
- Contingency Fund advances, votes on account, and the architecture of Articles 112–117 travel in that name.
- Constitutes a **Finance Commission** every five years (Article 280).

## Judicial / clemency (Article 72)

The President may grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment, or suspend, remit, or commute sentences

- in all **Union-law** offences;
- in all **court-martial** sentences;
- in all sentences of **death**, even where the offence is under State law.

Compare, for a later Governor day: a Governor’s corresponding power is narrower (State-law offences; not court-martial as such; death sentences can be commuted by the Governor but the *pardon of death* as a Union-wide special is the President’s distinctive width).

Clemency is on aid and advice, not a private act of grace against the Cabinet. Courts will not sit as a second mercy bench on merits; they will check if the rejection or grant is vitiated by patent arbitrariness, kept-back material, or a mechanical refusal.

## Diplomatic and other

Treaties and credentials travel in the President’s name. Article 51 from yesterday is the moral-policy caption on that file.

# BLOCK F — Climate regions of India (75 minutes)

You already know the monsoon as a seasonal engine. A **climate region** is a patch of the country where the *year-round combination* of temperature, rainfall amount, rainfall season, and dryness looks like one place and not another. Vegetation yesterday was the plant answer to that combination. Crops and houses are the human answer.

## The controls, restated as a map

1. **Latitude** — India is largely tropical and subtropical. The Tropic of Cancer cuts the country; the south has no real winter; the north has a winter that can bite.
2. **Himalaya** — blocks polar outbreaks from the far north, and forces monsoon air to rise. Without that wall, north India would not be the farm it is.
3. **Monsoon pulse** — most of the country is *seasonally wet*, not evenly wet. The same annual total can be a gentle spread or three violent weeks.
4. **Relief** — windward Western Ghats and Meghalaya plateau are wet; interiors behind them are dry. Leeward is a climate region, not a moral failing.
5. **Distance from the sea and continentality** — Delhi’s June is a furnace; Kochi’s June is a wet room. The sea stores heat and water.
6. **Western disturbances** — winter extra-tropical cyclones that give the north-west its rabi moisture and its hill snow.
7. **Bay storms** — October–November cyclones that *are* the Tamil Nadu and Andhra rain clock.

## A working regional scheme (Koppen language without idolatry)

Climatologists letter the world. For GS you need the **Indian faces** of those letters, not a German exam.

### 1. Tropical wet (heavy rain almost all year, short dry)

**Where.** Windward Western Ghats strip (Konkan-Canara-Kerala coast and the crest), the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and the wettest north-east (southern Meghalaya slopes, southern Assam valleys in their wettest pockets).

**Feel.** Mean monthly temperature high all year. Rain often above 200 cm, in Kerala and the Ghats face far above that; Mawsynram–Cherrapunji on the Meghalaya south slope are the textbook extremes because monsoon air is squeezed up a cliff.

**Life.** Evergreen and semi-evergreen forest (yesterday). Rice, coconut, spices, rubber, tea on suitable slopes. Flood and landslide are the tax on orography.

### 2. Tropical monsoon / tropical wet-and-dry

**Where.** Most of peninsular India that is not rain-shadow-dry or Ghats-wet: large parts of Maharashtra’s wetter west-central belt, Odisha, West Bengal plains, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, interior Karnataka’s wetter east-of-Ghats margins, coastal Andhra in the south-west monsoon months.

**Feel.** A real dry season. Temperatures peak just *before* the burst (May furnace). Rain is summer-concentrated.

**Life.** Moist and dry deciduous forest. Rice where irrigation or rain is kind; millets and cotton as you slide drier. This is the “classic Indian monsoon climate” of school maps.

### 3. Tropical semi-arid (steppe)

**Where.** Rain-shadow east of the Western Ghats: much of interior Maharashtra, interior Karnataka, interior Tamil Nadu’s drier districts, western Andhra–Telangana rain-shadow, eastern Rajasthan margins, parts of Punjab-Haryana that are not canal-green in the climate sense.

**Feel.** Rain often in the 40–80 cm teaching band, unreliable, a few wet days, a long thirst. Thorn and dry deciduous.

**Life.** Millets, pulses, oilseeds, cotton on black soil where that soil exists, grazing. Tanks and now borewells. Drought is not an accident here; it is the climate’s grammar. Canals can paint a different crop map without changing the sky.

### 4. Tropical arid (desert and near-desert)

**Where.** Western Rajasthan (Thar), the driest Gujarat fringe, the driest rain-shadow pockets.

**Feel.** Rain often below 25–30 cm in the core Thar, extreme summer heat, large diurnal range, winter that can be sharp at night. Western disturbances may sprinkle the north-west fringe.

**Life.** Thorn, sand-binding grasses, pastoralism, canal colonies as human climate. Indira Gandhi Canal country is an irrigation region sitting on an arid climate; do not confuse the two.

### 5. Subtropical humid (humid subtropical with dry winter) — the northern plain belt

**Where.** Punjab eastward through Haryana, Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, into the Assam valley in a modified form; the belt south of the Himalaya and north of the peninsula’s hard dryness.

**Feel.** Hot summer, distinct winter, rain dominated by June–September monsoon, plus **winter western-disturbance rain** in the north-west that the eastern plain receives more weakly. Kolkata is wetter and milder in winter than Delhi; Delhi is more continental.

**Life.** The wheat-rice argument of Indian food security lives here: rabi wheat on winter moisture plus irrigation in the west; kharif rice on monsoon in the east. Fog is a winter climate fact that becomes an aviation and health fact.

### 6. Mountain / highland climates of the Himalaya

Altitude overwrites latitude.

- **Siwalik and outer valleys** — subtropical, monsoon-wet in the east, drier in the west.
- **Mid-Himalaya** — cool summers, cold winters, snow on the high west; wet temperate in the east.
- **High cold desert of the north-west** — Ladakh, parts of northern Himachal and north-east Jammu & Kashmir rain-shadow: thin air, fierce insolation, almost no monsoon, tiny precipitation, irrigation from snow-melt.
- **Eastern Himalaya** — wetter at every comparable height than the west; snowline and tree line behave accordingly.

A hill station is not a climate region of its own; it is a *point* on this ladder chosen for a colonial summer. Write the belt, then the town.

### 7. Coastal special regimes

**West coast south of Goa (Malabar).** Long wet season, short dry, small annual temperature range. Retreating monsoon still throws rain.

**Konkan-Gujarat coast.** Wet summer, a more visible dry, Gujarat coast sliding toward semi-arid as you go north-west.

**Coromandel (Tamil Nadu coast and adjoining).** The teaching exception: **retreating monsoon / north-east monsoon (October–December)** is the main rain. South-west monsoon is partly rain-shadowed by the Ghats. Cyclones are not footnotes; they are the water budget.

**Sundarbans and deltas.** Humid, cyclone-exposed, saline edge. Climate plus tide.

## Seasons as a four-act play (revision glued to regions)

- **Winter (Jan–Feb).** High pressure over the north-west. Dry continental air. Western disturbances. Tamil Nadu still farming on yesterday’s north-east rain.
- **Summer / pre-monsoon (Mar–May).** Heat low over the interior. Mango showers and nor’westers (*kalbaisakhi*) in the east. Cyclones in the Bay. This is when arid and semi-arid India looks most like a furnace.
- **South-west monsoon (Jun–Sep).** Two branches: Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. Western Ghats and Meghalaya drink first and most. Rajasthan and the Tamil rain-shadow drink least from this branch.
- **Retreating monsoon (Oct–Dec).** Clear skies and festivals in the north; the Bay branch curls and hits Tamil Nadu. This is when Coromandel becomes the wet region and the northern plain becomes the sky-photograph region.

## Climate change as a regional, not slogan, fact

A warmer Arabian Sea is a heavier-rain, fewer-rainy-days problem for the west coast and a cyclone-intensity problem for both basins. A weaker or delayed burst is a semi-arid insolvency. Glacier mass in the west Himalaya is a river-timing problem for the plains you mapped on Day 3. Write the region first, then the trend. “Climate change affects India” scores zero. “The Coromandel water year is a cyclone year; a hotter Bay changes the variance of that year” scores marks.

# BLOCK G — Paired block: climate economy, current affairs, ethics (25 minutes)

## Economy

Climate regions are cropping regions before they are colouring-book regions.

- Arid and semi-arid India is a **millet, pulse, livestock, and groundwater** economy. A procurement regime that only worships wheat and rice will keep punching this climate in the face.
- The humid subtropical plain is a **grain machine** that now also runs on canal and tube-well electricity. Its winter fog and its June heat are labour facts.
- The tropical wet west coast is a **horticulture-spice-fish-tourism** economy sitting on a landslide slope.
- Coromandel’s reservoirs are timed to October, not to July. A national drought map that only looks at July–August will misread Tamil Nadu.
- Mountain cold desert is a **narrow irrigated oasis** economy. A tourism spike is not a monsoon.

Insurance, disaster relief, and crop calendars that ignore regional clocks are not “implementation gaps.” They are climate illiteracy in a secretariat.

## Current affairs explained, tied to today

When a Constitution Amendment Bill is in the news, sort it onto a ladder *before* you take a side. Does it touch the Seventh Schedule, the Supreme Court, or Article 368 itself? Then States must ratify. If a speech calls every reform a “basic structure violation,” test the limb. Federalism is not broken by a GST-style bargain that States join; judicial review *is* broken by a clause that says “no court shall look.”

When an ordinance arrives in the recess of Parliament, the static hook is Article 123 plus the anti-ordinance-raj ethic. Ask: could this have waited six weeks for a sitting House? Is it the same text for the third time?

When a presidential clemency file is discussed, start with Article 72’s three baskets (Union law, court-martial, death). Then say aid and advice. Then say limited judicial check for arbitrariness. Do not write a television morality play.

When a heatwave kills in the arid and semi-arid belt, the geography is today’s region map; the ethics is 21 and 47 (yesterday) plus the officer who opened night shelters without waiting for a trending hashtag.

When a Western Ghat landslide follows an extreme rain day, pair today’s tropical-wet orography with yesterday’s evergreen slope and 48A.

When a draft talks of delimitation or of changing how the President is elected, remember: **Articles 54 and 55 sit on Ladder 3.** Half the States must be in the room.

## Ethics

A President who treats the single return of advice under 74 as a veto has misunderstood the chair. A President who treats a hung House as a chance to install a favourite has also misunderstood the chair. The virtue of the office is **restraint that still refuses an unconstitutional signature**.

A ministry that governs by ordinance because the Rajya Sabha is inconvenient is stealing time from the Republic. A citizen who calls every amendment “anti-national” or “sacrilege” has not read 368. A citizen who thinks 368 can lawfully delete elections has not read basic structure.

Climate ethics for an officer: do not plan a paddy bonus in a 50 cm rainfall taluk and then blame the farmer for a failed borewell. Integrity is matching scheme to region.

# BLOCK H — CSAT: compound growth, calendars, blood relations, data (40 minutes)

### Q1. Compound increase

A semi-arid taluk harvests 20,000 tonnes. Production rises 10 percent a year for 3 years. Final harvest?

**Solution.** $20000 \times (1.10)^3 = 20000 \times 1.331 = $ **26,620 tonnes**.

### Q2. Compound versus simple

₹5,000 for 2 years at 10 percent. Difference between compound interest (annual) and simple interest?

**Solution.** SI = $5000 \times 10 \times 2 / 100 = 1000$.  
CI = $5000 \times (1.1)^2 - 5000 = 6050 - 5000 = 1050$.  
Difference = **₹50**.  
Shortcut for 2 years: difference = $P(R/100)^2 = 5000 \times 0.01 = 50$.

### Q3. Finding time in SI, then thinking CI

A sum doubles in 8 years at simple interest. Rate? Time to treble at the same SI rate?

**Solution.** Double means interest = principal. $R = 100 \times 1 / 8 = 12.5%$ per annum.  
To treble, interest = $2P$. $2 = (12.5 \times T)/100$ ⇒ $T = 200/12.5 = $ **16 years**.

(If the same question ever says *compound* interest, doubling time is not linear. Do not import the 16.)

### Q4. Calendar

1 September 2026 is a Tuesday. What day is 26 January 2027?

**Solution.** Count odd days from 1 Sep 2026 to 26 Jan 2027.

- Rest of September after the 1st: 29 days (2 Sep–30 Sep). Wait — from **1 Sep to 26 Jan** the clean method is: day-count of the interval.

From 1 Sep to 1 Jan is 122 days (Sep 30 + Oct 31 + Nov 30 + Dec 31 = 122), but 1 Sep to 1 Jan is 122 days later *if we move from date to same date*?  
From **1 September to 1 January** = 30+31+30+31 = 122 days, so 1 Jan is 122 days after 1 Sep. 122 = 17 weeks + 3 days, so 1 Jan 2027 is Tuesday + 3 = **Friday**.  
26 January is 25 days after 1 January. 25 = 3 weeks + 4 days. Friday + 4 = **Tuesday**.

Check leap-year: 2027 is not a leap year. February is not in this interval. Safe.

### Q5. Odd days in a year

An ordinary year has 1 odd day; a leap year has 2. From 1 Jan 2025 (Wednesday, given for this question) to 1 Jan 2026: 2025 is not leap, +1 odd day → Thursday. You only need the rule tonight: **365 ≡ 1 (mod 7)**; **366 ≡ 2 (mod 7)**.

### Q6. Blood relations

Pointing to a woman, an officer says: “Her grandson is my mother’s only child’s only son.” How is the officer related to the woman?

**Solution.** “My mother’s only child” is the speaker (assuming the speaker has no sibling). That person’s only son is the speaker’s son. So the woman’s grandson is the speaker’s son. Therefore the speaker is the woman’s **son** (or daughter). “Officer” is ungendered here; the relation to the woman is **child** — more precisely, the woman is the officer’s **mother**. Because the grandson is the officer’s son, the woman is parent of the officer.

If the officer is male, she is his mother. If female, still her mother. **Answer: the woman is the officer’s mother.**

### Q7. Direction puzzle

A survey party walks 8 km north, 6 km east, 8 km south. How far from start, and which direction?

**Solution.** North 8 and south 8 cancel. Left with 6 km east. **6 km east.**

# BLOCK I — Active recall with model cores (25 minutes)

Close the file. Answer in a line. Then check.

1. Who can introduce a 368 Bill?
   **Core:** Only in Parliament, either House. Not a State legislature.

2. Joint sitting for a Constitution Amendment Bill?
   **Core:** No.

3. Write the special-majority double test.
   **Core:** Majority of total membership of the House, *and* two-thirds of members present and voting; each House separately.

4. Name four things that need half-the-States ratification.
   **Core:** Any four of: President’s election (54–55); Union/State executive extent (73, 162); Supreme Court and High Courts; Union-State legislative distribution; Seventh Schedule; States’ representation in Parliament; Article 368 itself.

5. Are Articles 2–3 changes “amendments” under 368?
   **Core:** No. Article 4 says they are not deemed 368 amendments.

6. Can Parliament amend Fundamental Rights?
   **Core:** Yes, by 368, but not so as to damage basic structure.

7. Is basic structure a printed article?
   **Core:** No. Judicially named identity of the Constitution.

8. Electoral college of the President?
   **Core:** Elected MPs of both Houses + elected MLAs of States and of Delhi and Puducherry Assemblies. No nominated members.

9. Formula for an MLA’s vote value?
   **Core:** State population ÷ (elected MLAs × 1000), rounded.

10. President’s minimum age and the House he must be qualified for?
    **Core:** 35; Lok Sabha.

11. Impeachment ground and majority?
    **Core:** Violation of the Constitution; two-thirds of *total membership* in each House in the prescribed sequence.

12. Article 74 advice — can the President send it back?
    **Core:** Once for reconsideration; then must accept the reconsidered advice.

13. Ordinance: when, force, death?
    **Core:** Both Houses not in session + immediate need; force of an Act; laid on reassembly; dies six weeks after reassembly unless converted, or sooner if disapproved.

14. Article 72 three baskets?
    **Core:** Union-law offences; court-martial; all death sentences.

15. Why is Tamil Nadu’s main rain not July?
    **Core:** Coromandel is rain-shadowed in the south-west monsoon; October–December retreating / north-east monsoon plus cyclones fill the tanks.

16. Name the climate type of western Rajasthan, interior Deccan rain-shadow, windward Ghats, northern plain.
    **Core:** Arid; tropical semi-arid; tropical wet; subtropical humid with dry winter (monsoon plain).

17. What overwrites latitude in the Himalaya?
    **Core:** Altitude, plus west-dry / east-wet contrast and the Ladakh cold-desert rain-shadow.
