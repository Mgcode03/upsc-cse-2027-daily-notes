# BLOCK A — Why India has a Prime Minister at all (20 minutes)

India is a **parliamentary** executive. The Constitution names a **President** as the formal head of the Union, then immediately ties that head to a Council that must command the Lok Sabha.

Article **74(1)** (as it now stands after the 42nd and 44th Amendments):

> There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President who shall, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice.

The **44th Amendment** added a safety valve: the President **may** require the Council to **reconsider** such advice, either generally or otherwise, and after such reconsideration the President **shall** act in accordance with the advice tendered.

Three teaching sentences:

1. Advice is **binding**. The President is not a rival Prime Minister.
2. Reconsideration is **once**, not a veto loop.
3. Courts will not sit with a microscope on the *contents* of Cabinet advice in ordinary political questions. Article **74(2)** says the question whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered to the President **shall not be inquired into in any court**. That is a secrecy rule for the advice itself, not a licence for illegal acts done in the government’s name.

**Who is “the government” in everyday speech?** The Council of Ministers. Files move in the name of the President (Article **77**), but political responsibility sits on ministers who can be dismissed by the House.

**Contrast you already own.** The President is elected for five years, is not removable by a no-confidence motion, and is impeachable only for violation of the Constitution. The Prime Minister has no fixed constitutional term as PM. The clock that matters is the confidence of Lok Sabha, and the five-year life of that House.

# BLOCK B — How a Prime Minister is made, kept, and unmade (50 minutes)

## Appointment (Article 75)

Article **75(1)**: the Prime Minister shall be **appointed by the President**, and the other ministers shall be appointed by the President **on the advice of the Prime Minister**.

The text is short. The convention that fills it is long.

**Normal case.** After a general election, one party or a pre-poll alliance has a clear majority in Lok Sabha. The President invites the leader of that majority to form the government. That leader takes oath as Prime Minister. He then gives the President a list of ministers.

**Hung House.** No single list commands a majority on day one. The President uses residual discretion to invite the person **most likely to command confidence**. Living practice in India has usually preferred:

- the leader of the single largest party, or
- the leader of a post-poll combination that produces letters of support,

and then requires that person to prove majority on the floor within a short, specified time. The floor test is the constitutional medicine. Palace arithmetic is not.

**Incumbent PM after a mid-term crisis.** If a PM dies, resigns, or is voted out, the Council’s life is in question. A ministry that has lost the House should not linger as if nothing happened. The President can explore whether another person in the existing House can command confidence, or whether dissolution and a fresh election are the honest course. Dissolution is not a private prize of a PM who has already lost the House; it is an act of the President on advice, and advice from a PM who no longer commands the House is constitutionally weak.

**Does the PM have to be a member of Lok Sabha?** The Constitution does not say “must already be an MP on the morning of appointment.” Article **75(5)** says a minister who for any period of **six consecutive months** is not a member of either House of Parliament shall at the expiration of that period cease to be a minister. So:

- a PM can be appointed from **Rajya Sabha**;
- a PM can be appointed from **outside Parliament**, but must get a seat in either House within six months;
- a PM who sits in Rajya Sabha still answers to Lok Sabha, because collective responsibility is to the House of the People (below).

In practice, most Prime Ministers have been Lok Sabha leaders, because that is where the majority lives.

## Oath and office

A minister, before entering office, takes the oaths of **office** and of **secrecy** in the form set out in the **Third Schedule**. The President administers them.

The PM’s salary and allowances are fixed by Parliament by law. They are not a constitutional mystery.

## How a PM leaves

- Resignation of the Prime Minister is treated as resignation of the **whole Council**.
- A **no-confidence** motion passed by Lok Sabha against the Council ends the ministry. There is no separate no-confidence motion against an individual minister in the constitutional text; the House hits the team.
- Defeat on a matter the government has declared a **test of confidence** (including some money votes, by convention) is politically fatal even if no formal no-confidence motion was tabled.
- Death of the PM dissolves the existing Council as a working team; a new PM is appointed.
- Loss of party leadership inside a majority party can force a PM out even while the party still holds the House — that is party constitution plus Article 75, not a separate Article.

**Individual dismissal.** The President dismisses a minister **on the advice of the Prime Minister**. A PM who cannot drop a minister is a PM who does not control the Council. That power is the spine of Cabinet government.

# BLOCK C — Council, Cabinet, and the size cap (45 minutes)

## Council of Ministers is the legal body

Article **75** speaks of the **Council of Ministers**. All ministers, of whatever rank, are in this Council. They all take the same constitutional oaths. They all share **collective responsibility**.

Ranks in living practice (not named as a ladder in Article 75):

- **Cabinet Ministers** — full members of the Cabinet; they attend every Cabinet meeting unless a meeting is restricted; they hold charge of major ministries.
- **Ministers of State (Independent Charge)** — run a ministry without a Cabinet minister above them; they come to Cabinet when their subject is on the table.
- **Ministers of State** — attached to a Cabinet minister; they handle work the senior minister allocates.
- **Deputy Ministers** — a thinner rank, used in some ministries in some decades.

The Constitution does not invent these labels. The **Cabinet** itself is a word the text uses sparingly (you will meet it in Article **352** when a proclamation of Emergency needs the Union Cabinet’s written decision). Politically, the Cabinet is the **inner core** of the Council: the body that takes the government’s collective decisions.

**Teaching contrast.**

| | Council of Ministers | Cabinet |
|---|---|---|
| Legal home | Article 75 | Convention + a few Articles |
| Size | All ministers | Inner team |
| Meets | Rarely as one room | Regularly |
| Responsibility | Collective, to Lok Sabha | Same responsibility, tighter room |
| Secrecy | Oath of secrecy | Cabinet secrecy is the working form |

## The 15 percent cap (91st Amendment)

Article **75(1A)**: the total number of ministers, **including the Prime Minister**, in the Council of Ministers shall not exceed **15 percent** of the total number of members of the House of the People.

Why this exists: before the cap, Councils grew as political accommodation machines. The 91st Amendment (2003) put a lid at the Union and a parallel lid in the States (Article **164(1A)** — 15 percent of the Legislative Assembly, with a **floor of 12** ministers in a State).

For a working Lok Sabha of **543**, 15 percent is **81** ministers including the PM. Do the arithmetic in the exam hall: \(0.15 \times 543 = 81.45\), and the living cap is applied as a whole number consistent with the statute and practice of that House. Hold **81 including PM** as the standard teaching figure for a 543-member House.

The cap counts ministers. It does not count a Deputy Chairman of a planning body who is not a minister. It does not count the Attorney General. It does count every person who has taken the ministerial oath.

## Other 75 rules that Prelims loves

- **75(2).** Ministers hold office **during the pleasure of the President**. Pleasure is not personal whim. In a parliamentary system it is exercised on the PM’s advice, except in the rare breakdown cases you already met on the President day.
- **75(3).** The Council of Ministers shall be **collectively responsible to the House of the People**. This is the heart sentence of Indian Cabinet government. Not to Rajya Sabha. Not to the President. To Lok Sabha.
- **75(4).** Oaths before the President.
- **75(5).** Six-month rule for non-members.
- **75(6).** Salaries determined by Parliament.

## Collective responsibility — unpack it

Collective responsibility has three working faces:

1. **The team lives or dies together.** A successful no-confidence motion removes the Council, not one minister.
2. **One voice outside the room.** A Cabinet decision binds every minister. A minister who cannot defend it in public must resign. Leaking a dissent after the meeting is a political sin and, if it becomes a habit, a ground for dismissal.
3. **The House can question any minister on any government matter**, because the government is one.

**Individual responsibility** still exists beside it. A minister is answerable for the files of his own department. A railway accident lands on the railway minister’s desk first. Collective responsibility does not mean every minister drafted every file. It means the government cannot tell the House “that was only him.”

## Article 77 and 78 — the paper trail and the PM’s duty to inform

**Article 77.** All executive action of the Government of India shall be expressed to be taken **in the name of the President**. Orders and instruments made and executed in the President’s name shall be authenticated in the manner specified in rules. The validity of an order so authenticated shall not be called in question on the ground that it was not made by the President. The President also makes rules for the more convenient transaction of the business of the Government of India, and for allocation among ministers — the **Allocation of Business Rules** and **Transaction of Business Rules**. Those rules are how a file knows which minister signs.

**Article 78.** It shall be the duty of the Prime Minister:

- to communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation;
- to furnish such information relating to those affairs and proposals as the President may call for; and
- if the President so requires, to submit for the consideration of the Council any matter on which a decision has been taken by a minister but which has not been considered by the Council.

Article 78 is the constitutional anti-surprise clause. A PM who hides a Cabinet decision from the President is in breach of a written duty. A President who uses 78 to demand that a lone minister’s decision go to the full Council is using a real power — rare, but not ornamental.

# BLOCK D — How the machine actually works (40 minutes)

## Cabinet Committees

The full Cabinet cannot debate every file. Standing and ad hoc **Cabinet Committees** take clusters of work. Names change with governments. The pattern you must understand:

- A **Political Affairs** type committee handles the government’s political management.
- A **Security** type committee handles defence and internal security questions that need more than one ministry.
- An **Economic** or **growth** type committee handles major economic files.
- An **Appointments** type committee clears senior personnel.
- A **Parliamentary Affairs** type committee manages the government’s business in the two Houses.

A committee decision is treated as a Cabinet decision unless the Cabinet recalls it. That is why committee membership is power.

## The Cabinet Secretariat and the PMO

The **Cabinet Secretariat** services the Cabinet and its committees: agenda, minutes, follow-up. It is headed by the **Cabinet Secretary**, the senior-most civil servant of the Union.

The **Prime Minister’s Office (PMO)** is the PM’s own staff. It is not named in Article 75. It has grown because modern government is too large for a PM to see only through departmental ministers. Teaching line: the PMO is an **aid to the PM**, not a parallel Council. When it begins to issue instructions that ministers cannot challenge, collective responsibility is under strain. That strain is a Mains paragraph, not a new Article.

## Ministers and the two Houses

A minister may speak in **both Houses** (Article **88**), and may take part in the proceedings of any House of which he is not a member, but he **votes only in the House of which he is a member**. The Attorney General has a similar right of audience without a vote.

This is how a Rajya Sabha PM answers Lok Sabha questions: he can be present and speak; he cannot vote there.

## Attorney General — the law officer beside the Council

Article **76**: the President appoints the **Attorney General of India**, a person qualified to be a Supreme Court judge. He advises the Government of India on legal matters and performs other legal duties given to him. He holds office during the pleasure of the President. He is **not a minister** and is **not** in the 15 percent cap. He has the right of audience in all Indian courts. He may take part in parliamentary proceedings without a vote (Article 88). Do not call him a member of the Council of Ministers.

## Kitchen Cabinet, extra-constitutional advisers

Every PM has an informal circle. The Constitution neither blesses nor bans friendship. The test is always Article 75(3): if decisions that bind the Republic are taken in a drawing room that the Cabinet never sees, collective responsibility is theatre. For Ethics and Mains, write that sentence. For Prelims, do not invent an Article called “Kitchen Cabinet.”

# BLOCK E — Special situations you will be asked (25 minutes)

**Coalition PM.** The PM is still the head of the Council. Coalition partners sit as ministers. The common minimum programme is a political document. Constitutionally, one Council, one collective responsibility, one floor test.

**Deputy Prime Minister.** The office is **not** in the Constitution. When it has existed, the person was a minister who was styled Deputy PM by the government of the day. Rank is political. Legal status is still “minister.”

**PM as minister without a separate portfolio / with extra portfolios.** Common. Home, Atomic Energy, Space, Personnel have often sat with the PM in some years. Allocation of Business Rules carry the names.

**Care-taker convention.** After dissolution, or after resignation pending a new arrangement, the outgoing ministry continues to carry on day-to-day administration. It should not take major policy plunges. That is convention plus public law sense, not a separate chapter of Part V.

**State parallel (preview, not today’s full lesson).** Article **163–164**: Governor, Chief Minister, Council of Ministers, collective responsibility to the **Legislative Assembly**, 15 percent cap with a minimum of 12. Pleasure of the Governor. Same skeleton, different house.

# BLOCK F — Geography and economy: the agricultural year of India (55 minutes)

A Prime Minister’s majority can survive a bad speech. It rarely survives three bad harvests in a row. The Indian agricultural year is not the calendar year. It is a monsoon year with two main growing seasons and a short summer third.

## Three seasons

### 1. Kharif (southwest-monsoon crop)

- **Sowing:** with the onset of the southwest monsoon, roughly **June–July** in most of the country (earlier in the far south and on the west coast).
- **Growing:** the wet months.
- **Harvest:** **September–October**, stretching into November for some crops and regions.
- **Water logic:** these crops are built for **rain**. A delayed monsoon delays sowing. A mid-season break stresses flowering. An early withdrawal leaves grain half-filled.
- **Core kharif crops to name without hesitation:** rice (paddy), maize, jowar (sorghum), bajra (pearl millet), ragi (finger millet), cotton, jute, groundnut, soybean, tur (arhar / pigeon pea), moong, urad, sugarcane in many tracts as a long-duration crop that sits across seasons.

Rice dominates eastern and coastal India and the irrigated northwest. Cotton dominates parts of Gujarat, Maharashtra, Telangana, and northern Karnataka. Soybean is a Madhya Pradesh–Maharashtra story. Jute is a warm, humid, Bengal-Assam story.

### 2. Rabi (winter crop)

- **Sowing:** after the southwest monsoon withdraws, **October–December**.
- **Growing:** the cool, dry months. Moisture comes from residual soil water, western disturbances in the north, and **irrigation**.
- **Harvest:** **March–April** (some stretches into May).
- **Water logic:** rabi is the season of **canals, tubewells, and tanks**. A good monsoon fills the reservoirs that feed rabi. A bad monsoon can therefore spoil *next* winter even if the rain later looks “normal” on an annual chart.
- **Core rabi crops:** wheat, barley, oats, gram (chana), masoor (lentil), mustard and other rapeseed, pea. In parts of the south, a winter rice or a winter pulse sits in the same slot with a different local name.

Wheat is the political grain of the Indo-Gangetic plain: Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, and increasingly Madhya Pradesh. Mustard likes the drier north and east Rajasthan. Gram likes central India.

### 3. Zaid (short summer crop)

- **Sowing and harvest:** **April–June**, in the hot window between rabi harvest and kharif sowing.
- **Water logic:** almost entirely **irrigated**. Short duration. High evaporation.
- **Typical zaid crops:** watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber and other vegetables, fodder, some green gram, and in pockets a short summer maize or a summer rice where water is certain.

Zaid is small in area compared with kharif and rabi. It matters for nutrition, urban vegetable prices, and farmers who have groundwater and a nearby market.

## Regional clocks (do not flatten India into one calendar)

- **Tamil Nadu and the southeast** also live by the **retreating / northeast monsoon** (October–December). A “rabi” rice in the Cauvery delta is not the same weather story as a Punjab wheat field.
- **West coast and northeast** can have an earlier kharif clock because rain arrives earlier.
- **Himalayan valleys** squeeze the year: one main crop, sometimes two with irrigation, never the three-season density of a Punjab canal colony.
- **Rain-shadow interiors** (parts of Maharashtra, interior Karnataka, Rayalaseema, western Rajasthan) treat kharif as a gamble and lean on millets, pulses, oilseeds, and whatever irrigation exists.

## Cropping pattern words you must use correctly

- **Cropping intensity:** number of crops grown on the same land in a year. A field with kharif rice and rabi wheat has intensity 200 percent. Irrigation and short-duration varieties raise intensity. That is why canal commands look “richer” per hectare than a single-rainfed millet tract.
- **Monoculture:** same crop, same field, year after year. Punjab–Haryana rice–wheat is the textbook case. High output, falling water tables, soil fatigue, straw-burning pressure.
- **Mixed cropping:** two or more crops together on the same field at the same time (millets with pulses). Insurance against one crop failing.
- **Intercropping:** a designed row pattern of two crops (sugarcane with short pulses at the start).
- **Crop rotation:** sequence across seasons or years to rest the soil and break pest cycles (a pulse after a cereal).
- **Cash crop versus food crop:** cotton, sugarcane, jute, tea, coffee, oilseeds as sold crops; cereals and pulses as food. The line blurs: sugarcane is food and industry; soybean is oil and feed.

## The economic hinge: why seasons become politics

**Minimum Support Price (MSP)** is announced for listed crops, typically ahead of the relevant season, on the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) recommendation, with Cabinet approval. MSP is a **price signal**, not a legal purchase of every grain in every village. Actual procurement is dense for **rice and wheat** through the Food Corporation of India and State agencies, and thin for most other crops. That is why a cotton or tur crash can ruin a district even in a year when the wheat godown is full.

**Food Corporation and the buffer.** Public procurement of rice and wheat stocks the National Food Security system. A good kharif rice crop fills one side of the godown; a good rabi wheat crop fills the other. A PM who ignores reservoir levels in August is ignoring the wheat plate of next April.

**Monsoon as a macro variable.** Agriculture’s share of GDP is much smaller than its share of **employment**. A bad kharif still:

- cuts rural demand for two-wheelers and FMCG,
- lifts food inflation with a lag,
- stresses informal credit,
- forces import of edible oil or pulses if those crops fail.

That is the “farm economy” you will keep meeting. It is not a separate optional subject. It is why Article 75 governments fall after price spikes.

**Irrigation map in one paragraph.** Canal irrigation is a northern and delta story (Bhakra, Indira Gandhi Canal, upper Ganga, Godavari–Krishna deltas). Groundwater irrigation is a western-Gangetic and hard-rock Deccan story, with over-extraction in Punjab, Haryana, western UP, and parts of Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu, and Karnataka. Tank irrigation is a peninsular memory that still matters in Telangana and interior Tamil Nadu. Kharif without rain is a tragedy; rabi without stored water is a quieter tragedy.

# BLOCK G — Ethics of ministerial office, tied to the farm file (25 minutes)

GS-IV is not a sermon. It is the same Constitution watched from the inside of a decision.

**Public trust.** A minister holds power because Lok Sabha, and behind it the voter, trusts the team. Using a farm-procurement file to favour one mill owner is a breach of that trust. The legal wrapping may be the Prevention of Corruption Act. The ethical wrapping is **trusteeship**: the file is not yours.

**Collective responsibility as an ethical rule.** You may argue fiercely inside Cabinet that an MSP hike will fuel inflation. Once the Cabinet decides, you do not brief a channel against your own government and keep the portfolio. Dissent inside, voice outside — or resign.

**Secrecy versus transparency.** The oath of secrecy protects Cabinet deliberation and national security. It is not a cloak for a fertiliser-subsidy scam. RTI, CAG audit, and parliamentary questions exist because secrecy has a *purpose*, not a *prestige*.

**Compassion and the drought file.** A late monsoon is a fact. Delaying a drought declaration so that a statistic looks better is a value choice. Objectivity says publish the rainfall map. Empathy says move drinking water and fodder before the cattle die. Integrity says do not make the tanker contractor your relative.

**Courage of conviction.** A PM dropping a minister who has lost public credibility, even when that minister still has factional numbers, is Cabinet ethics in one act. Keeping him to “save the alliance” is a calculable political choice — write both sides in a case-study answer, then give your reasoned option.

**Farm policy as an ethics case.** Free electricity for tubewells is compassion for the farmer in year one and an inter-generational theft of groundwater in year twenty. An Ethics answer that only shouts “pro-farmer” without naming the aquifer is incomplete. An answer that only shouts “fiscal deficit” without naming the landless labourer is also incomplete. Hold both persons in the same paragraph.

# BLOCK H — Current affairs explained, tied to today’s static (20 minutes)

Treat this as a method, not a newspaper clipping dump. When a 2026–27 headline says any of the following, plug it into today’s frame:

- **A Cabinet expansion or reshuffle.** Ask: is the 15 percent cap safe? Who entered Cabinet Committees? Did a Rajya Sabha minister get a Lok Sabha-facing portfolio?
- **A no-confidence motion or a floor test.** Ask: is this Article 75(3) in action? Is the PM still the person who can advise dissolution?
- **A monsoon end-of-season note from the weather office.** Ask: which kharif crop in which region is inside the deficit patch? Will rabi reservoir storage suffer?
- **An MSP cabinet decision.** Ask: which season’s crop? Is there actual procurement, or only a price on paper?
- **A coalition quarrel over a ministry.** Ask: collective responsibility versus partner veto — which one is the Constitution, which one is politics?

Calendar consensus for your own year-plan: keep a weekly weather-and-sowing note from June to April. Prelims 23 May 2027 sits just after a rabi harvest. Mains from 20 August 2027 sits inside a kharif. The agricultural year will be running while you write.

# BLOCK I — CSAT practice, fully worked (45 minutes)

Do these without a calculator first. Then check the working.

**Q1.** A Council of Ministers is capped at 15 percent of a House of 543 members, including the Prime Minister. What is the maximum whole number of ministers *other than* the PM if the cap is taken as 81 including the PM?

A. 80  
B. 81  
C. 75  
D. 64

**Working.** Cap including PM = 81. Other ministers = \(81 - 1 = 80\).  
**Answer: A.**

**Q2.** Kharif sowing in a district covers 1,20,000 hectares. Of this, 45 percent is paddy, 30 percent is cotton, and the rest is pulses. How many hectares are pulses?

A. 30,000  
B. 36,000  
C. 25,000  
D. 40,000

**Working.** Paddy + cotton = 45% + 30% = 75%. Pulses = 25% of 1,20,000 = \(0.25 \times 1,20,000 = 30,000\).  
**Answer: A.**

**Q3.** A rabi wheat field yields 4.2 tonnes per hectare. A farmer has 3.5 hectares. After keeping 1.05 tonnes for seed and home use, he sells the rest at ₹22,000 per tonne. Sale receipt?

A. ₹2,64,000  
B. ₹2,88,750  
C. ₹3,08,000  
D. ₹2,31,000

**Working.** Total produce = \(4.2 \times 3.5 = 14.7\) tonnes.  
Sold = \(14.7 - 1.05 = 13.65\) tonnes.  
Receipt = \(13.65 \times 22,000\).  
\(13 \times 22,000 = 2,86,000\); \(0.65 \times 22,000 = 14,300\); total **₹3,00,300**.  
None of A–D matches. Recheck arithmetic before you panic in the hall: \(4.2 \times 3.5 = 14.7\) is correct; \(14.7 - 1.05 = 13.65\) is correct; \(13.65 \times 22 = 300.3\), times 1,000 is **3,00,300**.

When options are all wrong, the CSAT skill is to **trust the working** and mark nothing if it is a made-paper — on the real paper, re-read the question for a missed clause. Here the intended trap was \(4.2 \times 3.5 = 12.6\) (wrong multiplication). If a student uses 12.6 − 1.05 = 11.55, then \(11.55 \times 22,000 = 2,54,100\), also not listed. Closest listed misread: \(13.65 \times 20,000 = 2,73,000\) still not listed. **Correct value: ₹3,00,300.** On an official paper you would find that option. Learn the method, not the broken option set — and notice how easily \(4 \times 3.5 = 14\) plus \(0.2 \times 3.5 = 0.7\) saves you.

Revised clean version to score: produce 14.7 t; sell 13.65 t at ₹22,000/t = **₹3,00,300**.

**Q4.** A reservoir is 40 percent full on 1 October. It must be at least 55 percent full on 1 November to protect rabi sowing. Inflow in October is 18 percent of capacity; outflow (including evaporation) is 9 percent of capacity. Does it meet the mark?

**Working.** Start 40. Add 18 → 58. Subtract 9 → **49 percent**.  
49 < 55. **No.** Shortfall = 6 percentage points of capacity.

**Q5.** Statement logic.

Statement I: The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to Lok Sabha.  
Statement II: A Prime Minister must be a member of Lok Sabha at the moment of appointment.

A. I only  
B. II only  
C. Both  
D. Neither

**Working.** I is Article 75(3). II is false: six-month rule; Rajya Sabha PMs have existed.  
**Answer: A.**

**Q6.** Time and work. Six pumps can fill an irrigation tank in 10 hours. After 4 hours, two pumps fail. How many more hours will the remaining four pumps need to finish?

**Working.** One pump’s rate = \(1/60\) tank per hour (because 6 × 10 = 60 pump-hours fill the tank).  
Work done in 4 hours by 6 pumps = \(24/60 = 2/5\).  
Remaining = \(3/5\).  
Four pumps do \(4/60 = 1/15\) tank per hour.  
Time = \((3/5) \div (1/15) = (3/5) \times 15 = 9\) hours.  
**Answer: 9 hours more.**

**Q7.** Sets. In a village of 300 farming households: 180 grow a kharif cereal, 150 grow a rabi pulse, 70 grow both. How many grow neither?

**Working.** Union = \(180 + 150 - 70 = 260\).  
Neither = \(300 - 260 = 40\).  
**Answer: 40.**

**Q8.** Ratio. MSP of wheat to procurement incidentals is 8 : 1. If MSP is ₹2,400 per quintal, what is the total outgo per quintal to the agency if incidentals follow that ratio?

**Working.** Incidentals = \(2,400 / 8 = 300\). Total = \(2,400 + 300 = ₹2,700\).  
**Answer: ₹2,700.**

**Q9.** Calendar. 3 September 2026 is Thursday. What day is 3 September 2027?

**Working.** 2026–2027 spans 1 year. 2027 is not a leap year that adds an extra day *inside this particular 3 Sep–3 Sep window* in a way that differs: from 3 Sep 2026 to 3 Sep 2027 includes 28 Feb 2027, and 2027 is **not** a leap year (2024 was; 2028 will be). Ordinary year → +1 weekday. Thursday + 1 = **Friday**.  
**Answer: Friday.**

**Q10.** Comprehension style (short).

“Collective responsibility means the Cabinet’s decision is the government’s decision. A minister who leaks a minority view after the meeting keeps the prestige of honesty in his own eyes and loses the privilege of office in the Constitution’s eyes.”

Which inference is safest?

A. Ministers may never disagree inside Cabinet.  
B. Public dissent after a Cabinet decision is inconsistent with staying in office.  
C. Only the PM may speak after a Cabinet meeting.  
D. Leaking is a criminal offence in every case.

**Answer: B.** A is too strong (disagreement inside is allowed). C is false. D overreaches; leak can be political or, if classified, legal — the passage does not convict.
