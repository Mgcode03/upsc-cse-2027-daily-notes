# BLOCK A — Why a written Constitution needs courts (20 minutes)

A written Constitution is a promise that power has a shape. Promises without an umpire become slogans. Articles **124 to 147** build the **Supreme Court of India**. Articles **214 to 231** build the **High Courts**. Together they are the Union and State wings of one integrated judiciary.

Three teaching sentences you must own before any case name:

1. India does **not** have a dual court system like the United States, with a separate federal court ladder and a separate state court ladder that never meet. We have **one hierarchy**. A case can start in a district court, climb a High Court, and end in the Supreme Court.
2. The Supreme Court is **not** only an appellate court. It is also a **court of first instance** for some disputes (original jurisdiction), a **guardian of Fundamental Rights** (writs), an **adviser** to the President (advisory jurisdiction), and a **reviewer of its own judgments**.
3. **Judicial review** is the power of courts to test a law or an executive act against the Constitution and to declare it void if it fails. The phrase “judicial review” is not a single Article number. It is the working name for what Articles **13, 32, 131–136, 142, 143, 226** and the basic-structure doctrine do together.

**Contrast with yesterday.** The Prime Minister can lose office on a Friday afternoon if the House so votes. A Supreme Court judge does not lose office that way. Removal is a parliamentary process so heavy that it has almost never succeeded. Independence is not a courtesy. It is a design.

# BLOCK B — The Supreme Court: composition, appointment, tenure (55 minutes)

## Seat and composition

Article **124(1)**: there shall be a Supreme Court of India consisting of a **Chief Justice of India** and, until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number, seven other judges. Parliament has prescribed a larger number many times. Hold the living idea, not the 1950 number: the Court is a **Chief Justice plus other judges**, strength fixed by **parliamentary law**, not frozen in the original text.

The Court sits in **Delhi**. Article **130** allows it to sit in such other place or places as the Chief Justice may, with the approval of the President, appoint. In ordinary years the Court sits in Delhi. Circuit sittings are a possibility the text already imagined.

## Who can be a Supreme Court judge

Article **124(3)**. A person is qualified if he is a citizen of India and

- has been a High Court judge for at least **five years**, or
- has been an advocate of a High Court (or of two or more such Courts in succession) for at least **ten years**, or
- is, in the opinion of the President, a **distinguished jurist**.

The third door — distinguished jurist — exists so that a scholar who never sat on a High Court bench can still be considered. It has been used sparingly. Prelims likes the three doors. Mains likes the fact that the Constitution imagined more than a closed guild of sitting judges.

There is **no minimum age** written in Article 124 for appointment as a Supreme Court judge. There is a **retirement age: 65 years** (Article 124(2)). A High Court judge retires at **62**. That two-year gap is why a High Court Chief Justice in the early sixties is still in the conversation for elevation.

## Appointment — the living rule, taught without slogans

Article **124(2)** says every judge of the Supreme Court shall be appointed by the **President by warrant under his hand and seal after consultation with such of the judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts in the States as the President may deem necessary for the purpose**, and that in the case of appointment of a judge other than the Chief Justice, the **Chief Justice of India shall always be consulted**.

The word in the text is **consultation**. What “consultation” means has been the longest running constitutional argument of the Republic.

**Teaching sequence, not gossip:**

- For decades after 1950, the political executive had the louder voice. Consultation was real, but the last word sat with the President acting on ministerial advice.
- From the 1990s, the Supreme Court read “consultation” in this clause as effective **concurrence** of the judiciary for appointments, and built the **collegium**: the Chief Justice of India plus the next senior-most judges of the Supreme Court (four others for Supreme Court appointments; two others for High Court appointments, along with the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned).
- Parliament later tried to replace that system with a **National Judicial Appointments Commission** through a constitutional amendment and a statute. The Court struck the amendment down as damaging the **basic structure**, specifically judicial independence. The collegium therefore returned as the operating system.
- A later memorandum of procedure and a public portal for some vacancy data are administrative attempts to make the same system more transparent. They do not repeal Article 124.

For Prelims: the **formal appointer is the President**. The **living recommending body for judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts is the collegium**. Do not write “the Prime Minister appoints judges.” Do not write “the collegium is named in Article 124.” The collegium is judicial construction of the word consultation.

**Chief Justice of India.** Convention, not a numbered Article, has almost always sent the **senior-most judge of the Supreme Court** to the President as the next CJI. Seniority here means seniority on the Supreme Court bench, not age and not High Court Chief Justice seniority from earlier years. A government that skips seniority enters a political storm. The Constitution does not print “senior-most shall be CJI.” Practice does.

## Oath, salary, conduct

A Supreme Court judge takes an oath before the **President** (or a person appointed by him) in the form in the **Third Schedule**: to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, to uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India, to duly and faithfully perform the duties of the office, and to uphold the Constitution and the laws.

Salaries and allowances are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India**. They cannot be varied to the judge’s **disadvantage** after appointment (Article **125**, read with the spirit of independence). Privileges and rights in respect of leave and pension are also protected against adverse variation.

## How a Supreme Court judge leaves office

Four lawful exits:

1. **Retirement** at 65.
2. **Resignation** by writing addressed to the President.
3. **Removal** under Article **124(4)**: a judge may be removed from office by an order of the President passed after an address by each House of Parliament, supported by a **majority of the total membership** of that House and by a majority of not less than **two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**, has been presented to the President in the same session, for **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**. The procedure for investigation and proof is laid down by parliamentary law (the Judges Inquiry framework). This is often called **impeachment** in newspapers. The constitutional word is **removal**. It is deliberately hard.
4. **Death** in office.

A judge of the Supreme Court **cannot plead or act in any court or before any authority in India** after retirement (Article **124(7)**). The Republic does not want yesterday’s constitutional umpire appearing as today’s hired voice in an Indian court. (Advisory and some statutory tribunals, and work abroad, have produced their own later debates. Hold the Article first.)

**Ad hoc and retired judges.** Article **127** lets the CJI, with the President’s previous consent, request a High Court judge to sit as an ad hoc Supreme Court judge if the quorum is short. Article **128** lets the CJI, with the President’s previous consent, request a retired Supreme Court judge (or a retired High Court judge who was qualified for the Supreme Court) to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge. These are emergency benches, not a parallel court.

# BLOCK C — Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (50 minutes)

Learn five boxes. Almost every Prelims question on the Court is a box-sorting question.

## 1. Original jurisdiction — Article 131

The Supreme Court has original jurisdiction in any dispute

- between the **Government of India and one or more States**, or
- between the **Government of India and any State or States on one side and one or more other States on the other**, or
- between **two or more States**,

if the dispute involves any question (whether of law or fact) on which the existence or extent of a **legal right** depends.

This is a **federal umpire** clause. It is not a clause for two private citizens. It is not a clause for a citizen against a State (that citizen uses writs or ordinary suits). Water-sharing, boundary, and Union–State legal-right fights are the classic 131 country.

**What 131 does not cover.** Disputes arising out of a treaty, agreement, covenant, engagement or similar instrument entered into before the Constitution and continuing to operate, or which provides that the jurisdiction of the Court shall not extend — Article **131** itself carries this limit in its proviso. Also, ordinary commercial fights dressed up as “the State versus the Union” still need a legal right, not a political grievance.

A suit under 131 starts in the Supreme Court. That is why it is called original.

## 2. Writ jurisdiction — Article 32

You already met Article 32 on the Fundamental Rights days. Restate it as a court power.

Article **32** is itself a Fundamental Right: the right to move the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Court may issue directions, orders or writs, including **habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto**. Parliament may empower any other court to exercise these powers within its local limits, but it cannot take away the Supreme Court’s 32 power.

**Teaching contrast with Article 226.** High Courts under 226 can issue writs not only for Fundamental Rights but also for **any other purpose** — that is, for legal rights that are not Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court’s 32 is narrower in subject (Fundamental Rights) and wider in territory (all India). In practice, the Court has also used other Articles and its appellate power to reach far. For the exam, keep the textbook contrast clean: **32 = FR + Supreme Court; 226 = FR or any other purpose + High Court.**

## 3. Appellate jurisdiction — Articles 132 to 136

This is the everyday Supreme Court.

- **Article 132.** Appeal to the Supreme Court from a High Court in a civil, criminal or other proceeding if the High Court certifies that a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution** is involved.
- **Article 133.** Civil appeals from a High Court when a **substantial question of law of general importance** is involved and the High Court thinks the Supreme Court should decide it (after the 30th Amendment, the old “value of the property” money test was dropped; law, not rupees, is the filter).
- **Article 134.** Criminal appeals in specified cases: for example, where a High Court on appeal reverses an acquittal and sentences the accused to death, or withdraws a case from a subordinate court and itself convicts and sentences to death, or certifies that the case is fit for appeal. Parliament can enlarge criminal appellate jurisdiction.
- **Article 134A.** The High Court can grant the certificate for 132/133/134 on its own or on an oral application by the party, immediately after the judgment.
- **Article 136.** Special leave to appeal. The Supreme Court **may**, in its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by **any court or tribunal in the territory of India**, except a court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the **Armed Forces**.

**Article 136 is the wide gate.** Most of the Court’s docket enters here. It is discretionary. Nobody has a right to 136 leave. The Court uses it to correct grave injustice, settle law, or take a matter of public importance. It is not a third round of facts for every unhappy litigant — though the pressure to treat it that way is constant.

**Article 135** is a transitional remnant: the Court inherited some federal-court jurisdiction that existed before 1950 where Parliament had not yet legislated. Do not build a career on 135. Know that it exists.

## 4. Advisory jurisdiction — Article 143

If at any time it appears to the **President** that a question of law or fact has arisen, or is likely to arise, which is of such a nature and of such public importance that it is expedient to obtain the opinion of the Supreme Court, he may refer the question. The Court **may**, after such hearing as it thinks fit, report its opinion.

A second clause covers disputes arising out of pre-Constitution treaties and similar instruments; on those, the Court **shall** report.

**Teaching points that Prelims hunts:**

- Only the **President** can refer (on ministerial advice, in the ordinary course).
- The opinion is **advisory**. It is not a decree between two parties. Governments have usually treated it with high respect. It is still not a judgment under Article 141 in the same way a contested decision is, though the line has been discussed in later cases.
- The Court can, in a 143(1) reference, in substance decline to answer if the question is political in a way that does not fit a court, or is too vague. The “may” matters.

## 5. Review, curative, complete justice, law of the land

- **Article 137.** The Supreme Court may **review** its own judgment, subject to parliamentary law and its own rules. Review is not a second appeal. It is for an error apparent on the face of the record, new evidence that could not be produced, or other limited grounds the Court has accepted.
- A later judicial innovation, the **curative petition**, sits after a review is dismissed, for a still narrower set of situations (bias that was not known, violation of natural justice). It is not in the original 1950 text. Treat it as Court-made last-ditch process.
- **Article 141.** The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on **all courts** within the territory of India. This is how one judgment in Delhi becomes the law in every district court.
- **Article 142.** The Court may pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing **complete justice** in any cause or matter pending before it, and an order so made is enforceable throughout India. 142 is a powerful, much-litigated tool. Teaching line: it is a power to do complete justice **in a pending cause**, not a roaming ministry of justice.
- **Article 144.** All authorities, civil and judicial, in India shall act in aid of the Supreme Court.
- **Article 145.** The Court makes rules, with the President’s approval, for practice and procedure. A substantial question of constitutional interpretation is heard by a bench of at least **five** judges (a Constitution Bench).
- **Article 146.** Officers and servants of the Court; administrative independence of the Court’s establishment.
- **Article 147.** “Interpretation of this Constitution” includes interpretation of the Government of India Act 1935 and related instruments for the purpose of the appellate clauses.

# BLOCK D — Independence, judicial review, and public interest (40 minutes)

## The independence kit

Independence is a bundle, not a mood.

- **Appointment** not in the sole gift of the ministry of the day.
- **Tenure** till a fixed age, not at Cabinet pleasure.
- **Removal** only by a special parliamentary process for proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
- **Salary** charged on the Consolidated Fund; no adverse variation after appointment.
- **Conduct of a judge** not to be discussed in Parliament except on a removal motion (Article **121**; similar shield in State legislatures, Article **211**).
- **Contempt power** to protect the administration of justice.
- **Ban on practice** after retirement from the Supreme Court (124(7)).
- **Separate establishment** under the Chief Justice (146).
- **Charged expenditure** so that a budget vote cannot casually starve the Court.

None of these make a judge unaccountable. They make a judge hard to bully.

## Judicial review — the working doctrine

**Article 13** says laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are void. That is the first engine. Judicial review also tests:

- whether a law is on a subject the legislature was competent to touch (Union list / State list / Concurrent list — you will do this on the federalism day);
- whether a constitutional amendment damages the **basic structure** (you did this on the amendment day);
- whether an executive order is *ultra vires* the parent statute or the Constitution;
- whether procedure that took away life or liberty was fair, as Article 21 has come to require.

**What judicial review is not.** It is not the Court substituting its Budget for Parliament’s Budget. It is not the Court becoming the civil service. When a court writes a detailed scheme, Mains answers must show both the rights vacuum that pulled the Court in and the separation-of-powers cost of staying there.

## Public Interest Litigation

PIL is the practice of letting a public-spirited person or body move the Court for the rights of people who cannot easily reach the Court themselves — bonded labourers, undertrials, residents of a poisoned settlement. Procedure loosened: a letter could be treated as a petition; standing (who may sue) widened; continuing mandamus kept a department under watch.

**Use and cost, both needed in a Mains paragraph.**

- Use: rights became real for people who would never have hired a lawyer in Delhi.
- Cost: the docket swelled; some petitions are politics by other means; some orders are hard to enforce; the line between rights and policy blurs.

Ethics sentence: a PIL that is a neighbour’s business rivalry dressed as the public interest is an abuse of the Court’s open door.

## High Courts in one page (preview you need today)

- Every State has a High Court; Parliament may establish a common High Court for two or more States (Article **231**).
- Judges appointed by the President after consultation (living collegium practice), retire at **62**, removal process same as the Supreme Court (Article **217, 218**).
- Article **226** writs for FR and for any other purpose.
- Article **227** superintendence over all courts and tribunals in the territory over which the High Court exercises jurisdiction (with armed-forces exceptions in the usual way).
- A High Court judge can be transferred to another High Court by the President after consultation with the CJI (Article **222**).

You will spend a later day on the subordinate judiciary, district judges, and the All India Judicial Service debate. Today, remember that the High Court is both a constitutional court and the administrative head of the State’s ordinary courts.

# BLOCK E — Ethics of the bench (20 minutes)

A judge sells only one product: the belief that the hearing was fair.

**Values in play.** Objectivity, integrity, confidentiality of the conference, courage against both the ministry and the mob, restraint in public speech, and refusal to sit in a matter where a reasonable person would see bias.

**Recusal.** If a judge’s family, past client, or prior political stand would make the losing party say “the result was born before the argument,” the judge should not sit. Recusal is not cowardice. Sitting through a clear conflict is.

**Delay.** Justice postponed until the appellant is dead is a moral failure even when every procedural box was ticked. Courts that lecture the executive on timelines must watch their own.

**Post-retirement office.** The Constitution bars practice after a Supreme Court tenure. It does not bar every public office. The ethical question remains: will a judge in the last two years on the bench hear the Union differently if a later commission chairmanship is in the air? There is no Article that answers this. There is an appearance test.

**Comment on pending cases.** Ministers, newspapers, and retired colleagues all fail this test at times. A pending case is not a studio topic.

Model Ethics core: *Independence is for the case, not for the person. A judge who cannot be frightened must also refuse to be flattered.*

# BLOCK F — Geography and economy: minerals of India (55 minutes)

A mine is a geological accident plus a legal lease plus a labour camp plus a truck route. Prelims wants the accident (where the rock is). Mains wants the lease and the camp.

## How to think about a mineral

- **Metallic minerals:** iron ore, manganese, chromite, bauxite, copper, gold, lead-zinc. These feed metal.
- **Non-metallic minerals:** limestone, mica, gypsum, dolomite, phosphate, sulphur. These feed cement, fertilizer, insulation, chemicals.
- **Energy minerals:** coal, lignite, petroleum, natural gas, uranium, thorium. These feed heat and power. (Petroleum geology is its own map; today the solid fuels and the metallic map come first.)
- **Atomic minerals:** uranium, thorium, beryl, and related ores. The Union holds these tightly.

**Reserve versus resource.** A resource is “it is in the rock.” A reserve is “we can extract it with today’s prices and technology.” Exam language is sloppy. Prefer “major producing district” over fake-precise tonnage you will forget.

## Iron ore

India’s iron is mostly **haematite** (red, rich, peninsular sedimentary belts) and **magnetite** (black, often in the south, sometimes leaner).

**Belt to visualise:** a crescent of the old plateau —

- **Odisha:** Keonjhar, Sundargarh, Jajpur, Mayurbhanj. Odisha is the heavyweight producer in living years.
- **Jharkhand:** Singhbhum — the classic Noamundi–Gua country.
- **Chhattisgarh:** Bailadila (Dantewada) and Dalli-Rajhara (Durg). High-grade haematite that built the Bhilai story.
- **Karnataka:** Bellary–Hospet (Ballari), and magnetite in the Western Ghats tracts of the west.
- **Maharashtra, Goa, Andhra Pradesh, Rajasthan** add smaller or special pockets.

**Port logic.** Odisha and Jharkhand ore looks toward Paradip and Haldia. Karnataka and Goa ore looks toward the west coast. A ban or a cap in one cluster immediately shows up in coastal freight.

## Manganese

Steel’s cleanser and alloyer. **Odisha, Maharashtra (Nagpur–Bhandara), Madhya Pradesh (Balaghat), Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh.** The central Indian manganese belt is the picture to draw: Balaghat–Nagpur–Bhandara sitting near the iron belts, which is why integrated steel imagined itself there.

## Chromite

Stainless steel and chemicals. **Odisha (Sukinda, Jajpur)** dominates Indian chromite to a degree students under-learn. Small occurrences exist elsewhere. If a question says “almost all Indian chromite,” think Odisha.

## Bauxite (aluminium ore)

Bauxite likes laterite cappings on plateau surfaces — a weathering story, not a deep-vein story.

- **Odisha** again (Koraput, Kalahandi, Rayagada, Sundargarh).
- **Gujarat** (coastal laterite).
- **Jharkhand** (Lohardaga and the old Chotanagpur laterites).
- **Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra (Kolhapur), Madhya Pradesh, Tamil Nadu.**

Aluminium is frozen electricity. A bauxite hill without cheap power is just red dirt. That is why smelters sit near power, not always on the mine gate.

## Copper

Lean ore, costly concentration. **Balaghat (Madhya Pradesh), Singhbhum (Jharkhand), Khetri (Rajasthan)** are the three names that must come in one breath. India remains import-dependent for copper metal even when mines work.

## Lead-zinc

**Zawar, Rajpura-Dariba, Rampura-Agucha** in **Rajasthan** — the country’s lead-zinc heart. A little in Andhra Pradesh and elsewhere. Rajasthan is the sentence.

## Gold

**Kolar** is the historical name (Karnataka); production there collapsed. Living and revived interest sits in **Hutti** (Raichur, Karnataka) and in scattered greenstone belts. Gold in India is a small-mine story plus a huge jewellery-import story. Do not confuse domestic mine output with domestic jewellery demand.

## Silver, diamonds, gems

Silver often rides with lead-zinc (Rajasthan). Diamond history is **Panna** (Madhya Pradesh). Gemstones scatter through Rajasthan, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir (sapphire country in the northwest Himalaya). These are useful map facts; they are not the industrial core.

## Mica

Once India’s calling card in electrical insulation. **Kodarma–Giridih–Hazaribagh** (Jharkhand) is the mica belt you must be able to point to. Andhra Pradesh and Rajasthan add. Synthetic substitutes reduced mica’s old glamour; the belt remains a standard question.

## Limestone and cement

Limestone is everywhere there was an old sea or a Vindhyan shelf. Cement clusters follow limestone plus coal plus a market: **Satna–Katni** (Madhya Pradesh), **Andhra–Telangana** belts, **Rajasthan**, **Chhattisgarh**, **Gujarat**, **Tamil Nadu**. Limestone is also a steel flux. A steel town without limestone nearby pays extra freight.

## Gypsum, phosphate, sulphur, potash

- **Gypsum:** Rajasthan (the desert evaporite story), used in cement and plaster and as a soil amendment on alkali land.
- **Rock phosphate:** Rajasthan (Jhamarkotra), Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh. India is still import-heavy in phosphatic fertilizer.
- **Sulphur:** often recovered from oil and gas rather than mined as native sulphur.
- **Potash:** India is import-dependent; this is a fertilizer-security fact more than a mine-map fact.

## Coal and lignite (energy minerals you must place)

**Gondwana coal** (Permian, damodar and son-mahanadi-wardha basins) is the industrial coal of India: bituminous, in seams, in **Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Telangana, Maharashtra**. The Damodar valley is the classic picture — Raniganj, Jharia, Bokaro, Karanpura.

**Tertiary coal** is younger, in the northeast and a few other pockets: **Meghalaya, Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland**. Different geology, different problems (including the ethics of rat-hole mining where it has been practised).

**Lignite** (brown coal): **Tamil Nadu (Neyveli)** first, then **Gujarat, Rajasthan**. Lignite is bulky and low-heat; power plants sit on the field.

**Who owns coal in law.** Coal and lignite are in the Union’s legislative grip as listed minerals of national importance; mining rights are a Union–State tangle of the Mines and Minerals framework, coal-bearing areas law, and commercial auction policy. Teaching line for today: **States sit on the land; the Union writes the mineral rules for major minerals; districts live with the dust.**

## Atomic minerals

**Uranium:** Jaduguda and nearby belts in **Singhbhum (Jharkhand)** are the old core; later finds and mines in **Andhra Pradesh / Telangana** (the Cuddapah basin country), **Meghalaya**, and exploration elsewhere. **Thorium** sits in **monazite beach sands** of **Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh**. Beach-sand minerals (ilmenite, rutile, zircon, monazite, garnet, sillimanite) are a strategic coastal story. The Union treats atomic minerals as a security subject.

## The mineral economy in four mechanisms

1. **Freight is destiny.** Iron ore is cheap per tonne and expensive to move. Steel plants were born near ore, near coal, or near a port that could marry imported coking coal to domestic ore.
2. **Auction and royalty.** Major mineral concessions are increasingly given by auction. Royalty accrues largely to the **State**. A mineral State’s budget can swing with Chinese steel demand even when its monsoon was fine.
3. **District Mineral Foundation.** A share of royalty is meant to reach the mined district — roads, drinking water, clinics — because the national steel house is built on a local lung. Implementation quality is an Ethics and GS-II paragraph.
4. **Import dependence is selective.** India is strong in iron ore, bauxite, chromite, and limestone. India is weak in coking coal, copper in refined form, rock phosphate, potash, and many battery minerals (lithium, cobalt, nickel). Energy transition does not abolish mining. It changes the shopping list.

## Environment and people

Open-cast mines eat villages and forests. Underground mines eat men. Tailings ponds fail. Adivasi land and Fifth Schedule protection collide with lease maps in Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh. The legal tools you already have — Article 21, 23 (forced labour in some mine histories), 46 (DPSP on Scheduled Tribes), Forest rights statute — all meet at the pit head.

Ethics sentence: *A nation that wants cheap steel must put a price on the hill and on the miner, or it is only exporting the cost to a district that cannot refuse.*

# BLOCK G — Current affairs explained through today’s static (20 minutes)

You do not need a newspaper name. You need the handle.

**Vacancies and pendency.** A Constitution Bench cannot sit if the Court is busy as a third High Court of appeal. Every debate on “why so few five-judge benches” is a debate about Article 145 plus Article 136 discipline.

**All-India service of judges / district judge recruitment.** That is the subordinate judiciary preview: independence at the taluka, not only at Tilak Marg.

**Offshore minerals and critical minerals lists.** When the Union publishes a list of critical minerals, map it onto Block F: lithium and rare earths are the new manganese — small in tonnage, large in strategy. Beach-sand monazite is old geology in a new geopolitical file.

**Mine auctions and State budgets.** Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Rajasthan feel global commodity prices in their own treasuries. That is royalty plus auction premium, not magic.

**Women in underground mines.** A labour-law change that allows women in more mine categories is an Article 15 / 16 / 21 / 42 (just conditions of work) crossing. Safety is the real clause.

Label any specific “this month’s” case or ordinance you hear outside this file as something to verify against the judgment text. The static handle above will still be correct.

# BLOCK H — CSAT practice (50 minutes)

Paper II is qualifying. One-third negative mark. Show every step.

## Q1. Percentages and a mine royalty

A State collects royalty on iron ore at ₹100 per tonne. In year 1 it sells 12 lakh tonnes. In year 2 output falls 15 percent but the royalty rate rises 20 percent. What is the percentage change in royalty receipts?

**Worked solution.**

Year 1 receipts: \(12,00,000 \times 100 = 12,00,00,000\).

Year 2 output: \(12,00,000 \times 0.85 = 10,20,000\) tonnes.

Year 2 rate: \(100 \times 1.20 = 120\).

Year 2 receipts: \(10,20,000 \times 120 = 12,24,00,000\).

Change: \(24,00,000\) on a base of \(12,00,00,000 = 2\%\) increase.

Shortcut: multiplier \(0.85 \times 1.20 = 1.02\). Receipts become 102 percent of earlier. **+2 percent.**

## Q2. Pipes (reservoir logic you can reuse)

Pipe A fills a tank in 12 hours. Pipe B fills it in 18 hours. Pipe C empties it in 36 hours. All three are opened together. After 3 hours C is closed. How much longer will A and B take to fill the rest?

**Worked solution.**

A’s 1-hour work \(= 1/12\). B \(= 1/18\). C \(= -1/36\).

Together: \(\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{18} - \frac{1}{36}\).

LCM of 12, 18, 36 is 36: \(\frac{3}{36} + \frac{2}{36} - \frac{1}{36} = \frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9}\).

In 3 hours they fill \(3 \times 1/9 = 1/3\).

Remaining \(= 2/3\).

A + B: \(\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{18} = \frac{3+2}{36} = \frac{5}{36}\).

Time \(= \frac{2/3}{5/36} = \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{36}{5} = \frac{24}{5} = 4.8\) hours \(= 4\) hours 48 minutes.

## Q3. Data sufficiency

Question: What is the retirement age of judge X?

Statement I: X is a judge of a High Court in India.

Statement II: X was appointed under Article 124.

Options: (a) I alone (b) II alone (c) either alone (d) both together (e) not even both.

**Worked solution.**

I alone: High Court retirement is 62. Sufficient, if we assume X is a sitting High Court judge in the present constitutional scheme and not something exotic.

II alone: Article 124 is the Supreme Court. Retirement 65. Sufficient.

Either statement alone answers, and they answer *different* ages, which is fine because they describe different offices. **Answer (c).**

CSAT trap: students think both are needed. No. Each statement identifies a different court and therefore a different known age.

In a code, JUDGE is written as LWFGI. How is BENCH written?

## Q5. Sets

In a district, 40 percent of surveyed villages have a mine lease nearby, 55 percent have a primary health centre, 20 percent have both. What percent have neither?

**Worked solution.**

Union \(= 40 + 55 - 20 = 75\).

Neither \(= 100 - 75 = 25\) percent.

## Q6. Number system for CSAT stamina

How many zeros at the end of \(50!\)?

**Worked solution.**

Trailing zeros = number of times 10 = 2×5 divides 50!. There are more 2s than 5s, so count 5s:

\(\lfloor 50/5 \rfloor + \lfloor 50/25 \rfloor = 10 + 2 = 12\).

**12 zeros.**

## Q7. Statement–assumption (CSAT comprehension style)

Statement: “The Supreme Court should not grant special leave as a routine third appeal.”

Possible assumption: High Courts are generally competent to settle ordinary questions of fact and law.

Is the assumption implicit?

**Worked solution.**

Yes. The recommendation to ration Article 136 only makes sense if the High Court is treated as a real court of error for ordinary cases. If you assumed High Courts were generally incompetent, you would want *more* 136, not less.
