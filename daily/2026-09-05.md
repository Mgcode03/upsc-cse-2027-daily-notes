# BLOCK A — What kind of federation is India? (25 minutes)

A federation is a political design in which **two levels of government** each have a constitutionally protected existence and a constitutionally listed field of work. Neither is a mere department of the other.

India’s own words are careful. Article **1**: *India, that is Bharat, shall be a **Union of States**.* The Drafting Committee preferred “Union” to “federation” for two teaching reasons that still matter:

1. The Union is **indestructible**. A State cannot secede. Parliament can, by law, form a new State, alter boundaries, or change a State’s name (Article **3**), after the President has referred the Bill to the legislature of the State concerned for its **views**. Those views are not a veto. The Union can redraw the map; a State cannot walk out of it.
2. The units were not fully sovereign States that came together by treaty (the classic United States story). Many provinces and princely units were **integrated into** a new Republic. The Centre is therefore stronger on paper than in several older federations.

**Hold both truths together.** India is federal in structure (two lists of governments, a written Constitution, an independent judiciary, bicameralism at the Centre with a Council of States) and **unitary in tilt** (single citizenship, a single integrated judiciary, All-India Services, Emergency powers, residuary legislation with the Union, Article 3 flexibility). Exam language that still works: **federal with a strong Centre**, or **quasi-federal**. Do not waste a paragraph arguing the label. Show the clauses.

**Territory vocabulary (Article 1 and related).**

- **States** — full constitutional units with their own legislatures and Councils of Ministers.
- **Union territories** — held by the Union; some now have legislatures (Delhi, Puducherry, and, after later reorganisation law, Jammu and Kashmir as a UT with a legislature on the statute book). UTs without legislatures are run more directly through an Administrator.
- **Acquisition of territory** and the power to admit or establish new States sit with Parliament (Article **2**).

Article **4** says a law under Articles 2 or 3 is **not** a constitutional amendment under Article 368. That is why States can be split or renamed by ordinary parliamentary law plus the Article 3 process.

# BLOCK B — Who makes which law: the Seventh Schedule (50 minutes)

## The three lists

Article **246** and the **Seventh Schedule** divide subjects.

- **Union List (List I).** Defence, foreign affairs, atomic energy, war and peace, railways, national highways, banking, currency, stock exchanges, census, UPSC, audit of Union accounts, inter-State trade and commerce in many of its commanding heights. On these, Parliament has **exclusive** power.
- **State List (List II).** Public order (not including use of Union armed forces), police, public health, agriculture, local government, intoxicating liquors, land, markets and fairs — the everyday State. On these, the State legislature has exclusive power **in ordinary times**.
- **Concurrent List (List III).** Criminal law and procedure, marriage and divorce, forests (after the 42nd Amendment moved forests here from the State List), protection of wild animals and birds, education (also moved in 1976), economic and social planning, population control and family planning, labour welfare in several heads, electricity. Both Parliament and State legislatures may make laws. If they clash, **Union law prevails**, subject to a special window in Article **254(2)**.

**Article 254 clash rule, taught slowly.**

If a State law on a Concurrent subject is **repugnant** to a Union law on the same subject, the Union law wins and the State law is void to the extent of the repugnancy. **Exception:** if the State law was reserved for the President and received his **assent**, it can operate in that State even against an earlier Union law. Parliament can still **override** that State law later by enacting a new Union law on the same matter. So the exception is real, but not permanent armour.

**Residuary power — Article 248.** Subjects not listed in any of the three lists belong to **Parliament**. This is the opposite of the United States residual rule (there, unlisted powers stay with the States). Cyber, space-adjacent regulation, and many new technologies are often argued as residuary or as stretching an existing Union entry. Prelims wants the default: **residuary = Union**.

## When the Centre can enter the State List even without Emergency

Memorise the doors. They are favourite MCQ traps.

1. **Article 249.** If Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by a **two-thirds majority of members present and voting**, saying it is necessary in the **national interest** that Parliament legislate on a named State List matter, Parliament may do so. The resolution lasts **one year** and can be renewed. The law lasts six months after the resolution stops.
2. **Article 250.** While a **Proclamation of Emergency** is in operation, Parliament may legislate on any State List matter. The law expires six months after the Emergency ends.
3. **Article 252.** Two or more State legislatures can **request** Parliament to legislate on a State subject for those States. Other States may adopt later.
4. **Article 253.** Parliament may make any law to implement a **treaty**, international agreement, or decision of an international conference — even if the subject is on the State List. This is how global environment and trade pacts reach State fields.
5. **Article 256–257.** States must exercise executive power so as to **comply with Union laws** and not impede Union executive power. The Union can give directions. Persistent failure is one of the political roads that can lead toward Article 356 (taught below), though 356 has its own textual trigger.

**Article 245** is the opening frame: Parliament may make laws for the whole or any part of India; a State legislature for the whole or any part of the State. Neither is wholly extra-territorial without a hook, but Parliament’s extra-territorial reach is expressly recognised.

# BLOCK C — Money, coordination, and the living federation (40 minutes)

Law lists are only half of federalism. **Cash and forums** are the other half.

## Fiscal federalism, beginner core

- The Union and the States each have their **own tax heads** in the Seventh Schedule (for example, Union: customs, corporation tax in its classical place; States: a basket that has been rewritten by the Goods and Services Tax era).
- **GST** created a shared consumption tax with a **GST Council** (Article **279A**). The Council is a constitutional body where the Union Finance Minister chairs and State finance ministers sit. It is cooperative machinery, not a State legislature. How recommendations bind, and what happens if the Union and a group of States disagree, is a living debate. For today: GST is the biggest post-1950 rewrite of Indian fiscal federalism; it pooled many State taxes into a common design with input-credit across State lines.
- **Finance Commission** (Article **280**). Constituted by the President every five years (or earlier). It recommends how taxes that the Constitution says are to be **shared** should be divided between Union and States (**vertical devolution**) and among States (**horizontal devolution**), plus grants-in-aid. The Commission is the Constitution’s peace treaty against annual political bargaining over the divisible pool. Recommendations are not self-executing statutes; they are made to the President and laid before Parliament. Practice has given them high weight.
- **Grants.** Article **275** grants-in-aid to States charged on the Consolidated Fund; Article **282** allows the Union or a State to make grants for any public purpose even if it is not on their legislative list. Centrally sponsored schemes often travel through this political-grant world. That is why States complain of “conditional money” even when the subject is State List.

## Inter-State machinery

- **Article 262.** Parliament may by law provide for adjudication of **inter-State river water** disputes and may bar the Supreme Court from that field. The Inter-State River Water Disputes statutory framework lives here. Article 131 original jurisdiction and Article 262 can sit in tension; the exam line is: **water-dispute law can keep ordinary courts out** if Parliament so provides.
- **Article 263.** The President may establish an **Inter-State Council** if it appears that public interest would be served by a body to inquire, discuss, and recommend on inter-State or Union–State subjects. The standing Council that exists today is a later notification-built forum. Know the Article first, the current membership second: Prime Minister as chair, Chief Ministers, and specified Union ministers in the usual design.
- **Zonal Councils** are statutory (States Reorganisation era), not Article 263 bodies. They are consultative.
- **All-India Services** (Article **312**). Rajya Sabha can declare that an All-India Service is necessary. IAS, IPS, and IFoS officers are recruited by the Union and serve in States. This is a unitary steel rod inside a federal house.

## Trade across State lines

Articles **301–307**: trade, commerce and intercourse throughout India shall be **free**, subject to reasonable restrictions Parliament may impose in the public interest, and subject to a State’s limited power in specified cases (and a special Bill procedure involving the President in some restriction clauses). GST was also a trade-freedom project: a truck should not pay a new tax at every border check-post.

# BLOCK D — Emergency provisions: three switches (50 minutes)

Part XVIII, Articles **352 to 360**. Teach them as three different machines. Newspapers say “Emergency” as if there were one. There are three.

## 1. National Emergency — Article 352

**Grounds (after the 44th Amendment):** war, **external aggression**, or **armed rebellion**. The older word “internal disturbance” was removed because it was too wide. A Proclamation can be made for the whole of India or a part.

**Process, current text:**

- The President proclaims. He acts on written Cabinet advice — the 44th Amendment requires the decision of the **Union Cabinet** to be communicated in writing before a 352 Proclamation. A lone Prime Ministerial whisper is not enough on paper.
- The Proclamation must be laid before each House and approved by **special majority** (majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting) within **one month**. Once approved, it lasts **six months** and can be renewed indefinitely in six-month steps with the same special majority.
- Lok Sabha can force revocation: a notice by one-tenth of members, a special sitting, and a simple-majority disapproval resolution. The President must then revoke.
- The President can revoke at any time by a later Proclamation.

**Effects you must separate:**

- **Executive.** The Union can give States directions on any matter. State governments do not automatically fall.
- **Legislative.** Parliament can make laws on State List subjects (Article 250). Such laws sleep six months after the Emergency ends.
- **Financial.** The President may modify the distribution of revenues between Union and States while the Proclamation lasts (Article **354**), with a limited afterlife.
- **Fundamental Rights.** Article **358** suspends the operation of Article **19** automatically when the Emergency is on the ground of **war or external aggression** (not on armed rebellion alone, after the 44th). Article **359** lets the President suspend the right to move court for other Fundamental Rights named in the order — **except Articles 20 and 21**, which cannot be suspended. That 44th Amendment lock is one of the most important rights facts in the syllabus.
- **Term of Lok Sabha.** Article **83** allows extension of Lok Sabha, one year at a time, during a 352 Emergency, but not beyond six months after the Emergency ends.

National Emergencies in the Republic’s history: 1962 (China war), 1971 (Pakistan war), 1975 (internal disturbance — the controversial year that produced the 44th Amendment’s tightening). Teach the 1975 lesson as **why the text was later narrowed**, not as gossip.

## 2. President’s Rule — Article 356 (State Emergency / failure of constitutional machinery)

If the President, on receipt of a report from the Governor **or otherwise**, is satisfied that the government of a State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution, he may proclaim.

**Effects:**

- The President may assume to himself the functions of the State government and the powers of the Governor.
- Parliament may be authorised to exercise the State legislature’s powers. In practice, Parliament often delegates that work to the President, who then legislates for the State by **President’s Acts**.
- The State Council of Ministers goes. The Assembly may be **suspended** or **dissolved**. Suspension keeps a door to revive the House if a majority reappears; dissolution starts the election clock.
- The High Court is not taken over. Judiciary remains.

**Duration.** Approval by each House within **two months** (simple majority). Then six months at a time. Normally a maximum of **three years**, and the stretch beyond one year needs extra conditions (Emergency in operation in the whole of India or the State, plus Election Commission certification that elections cannot be held). These outer locks came after misuse became a political pattern.

**Judicial review.** For decades 356 was treated as a political thicket. Later the Court held that the Proclamation is reviewable: there must be relevant material; the power is not a tool to topple a majority government because the Centre dislikes its colour; floor tests are preferred to Raj Bhavan arithmetic. Mains line: **356 is a safety fuse, not a campaign instrument.**

**Article 365** is the direction-default cousin: if a State fails to comply with a Union direction under the Constitution, it becomes lawful for the President to find that 356 is attracted. 365 is a bridge, not a separate Emergency.

## 3. Financial Emergency — Article 360

If the President is satisfied that the financial stability or credit of India, or any part of it, is threatened, he may proclaim a Financial Emergency.

**Approval:** each House within **two months** (simple majority). Once approved, it can continue indefinitely until revoked. There is no six-month renewal ritual like 352.

**Effects:** Union directions to States on financial propriety; reduction of salaries of Union and State servants, including judges; all Money Bills of States reserved for the President’s consideration.

**India has never imposed Article 360.** That fact itself is exam-useful. The clause exists as a last fiscal brake.

## Comparison strip (memorise as a table in your head)

| | 352 National | 356 State | 360 Financial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | War, external aggression, armed rebellion | Constitutional machinery fails in a State | Financial stability or credit threatened |
| Whose government falls? | Not automatically | State Council of Ministers | Neither automatically |
| FR hit | 19 (war/aggression); 359 order except 20 and 21 | No automatic FR suspension | No FR suspension |
| Approval window | 1 month, special majority | 2 months, simple majority | 2 months, simple majority |
| Renewal | 6 months, special majority | 6 months; 3-year outer cap with conditions | No six-month cap once approved |
| Used? | Yes (1962, 1971, 1975) | Many times | Never |

# BLOCK E — Geography and economy: population of India (40 minutes)

Federalism is a map of governments. Population is the map of **who those governments serve**.

## The big numbers, taught as ideas not as a data dump

India is the world’s most populous country in the mid-2020s estimates, having crossed China in UN and national statistical conversation. The last full Census enumeration cycle that students still use as a structural baseline is **Census 2011** (1.21 billion; density about 382 persons per sq km; sex ratio 943 females per 1000 males; child sex ratio 919; literacy 74.04 percent). A later Census round was delayed; until a new full count is published, questions often still test 2011 structure plus SRS and NFHS trends. When a new Census is notified and released, replace the stock numbers; **do not replace the concepts below.**

**Density pattern.** Highest densities sit on the **fertile plains and coastal belts**: Bihar, West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Kerala, Tamil Nadu. Lowest densities sit in the **Himalayan and desert/very dry States**: Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Mizoram, Andaman and Nicobar, then Rajasthan in large-area terms. Density is people divided by area. A large desert State can have many people and still look “sparse.”

**Growth pattern.** The **Deccan and the South** generally moved earlier into lower fertility. The **Gangetic heartland and parts of the East** stayed higher for longer. This is why political fights over **delimitation and Finance Commission weights** are really fights about **whose population grew after a freeze year**. You already met delimitation on the Parliament day. Tie it here: a long freeze of Lok Sabha seats by State rewards States that slowed births and punishes them if seats are later reopened without a bargain.

**Demographic transition, four stages you must be able to draw in words.**

1. High birth, high death — population almost flat, young and vulnerable.
2. Death rate falls (public health, food, sanitation); birth rate stays high — **explosion**.
3. Birth rate falls (schooling, later marriage, contraception, urban cost of children) — growth slows.
4. Both rates low — ageing, even decline.

India as a whole is in **late stage 3**, but **States are not in the same stage**. Kerala and Tamil Nadu look more like stage 4. Parts of the northern plains still show stage-3 momentum. That is the whole political geography of population.

**Demographic dividend.** When the share of working-age people (roughly 15–64) is high relative to children and the old, a country can save, invest, and grow faster — **if** those working-age people have health, skills, and jobs. Without jobs, the same bulge is unemployment and unrest. Dividend is a **window**, not a gift. Windows close when the same cohort ages.

**Sex ratio and child sex ratio.** Overall sex ratio can improve while the child sex ratio stays cruel, because older women survive longer. Child sex ratio is the better mirror of **son preference and sex-selective practices**. That is why it is an ethics issue as much as a statistics issue.

**Literacy and work.** Female literacy is the strongest widely taught lever on fertility decline. Work participation of women in official counts often looks low because much work is unpaid, seasonal, or home-based and poorly captured. Read a low female work-participation number as a measurement-plus-social fact, not as “women do not work.”

**Migration.** People move from farm interiors to metro edges, from eastern States to western industrial belts, from hills to plains in lean seasons. Remittances keep origin districts alive. Destination cities strain water, housing, and local-government lists. Internal migration is federalism from below: a Bihar-born worker pays GST in Maharashtra and votes in a village roll unless the roll has moved with him.

**Population policy tone for Mains.** Rights-based family planning, spacing, women’s education, and quality public health beat coercive targets. India’s official line after the Emergency-era sterilisation shock has been **voluntary and welfare-linked**. Coercion returns in some State slogans about two-child norms for local-body seats; weigh those against Article 21 dignity and against the fact that fertility is already falling in most of the country.

# BLOCK F — Paired current-affairs explained + ethics (25 minutes)

## Current-affairs explained (tied to today, not a newspaper scrapbook)

Whenever you see a headline about **GST compensation, a Finance Commission weight, a State refusing a centrally designed scheme, a Governor–Chief Minister stand-off, a river-board award, or a proposal to freeze or unfreeze Lok Sabha seats**, you are reading Day 10.

Translate the headline into a clause:

- “State says the scheme is voluntary” → Concurrent or State List plus Article 282 money.
- “Centre says national security requires a uniform law” → Union List or Article 253 or 249.
- “Governor sends a report” → possible 356 material; courts now ask whether the floor was tested.
- “Seats must reflect today’s population” → federal bargain versus arithmetic; South versus high-growth North is the real map.

You do not need a date-wise diary. You need this translation habit.

## Ethics for GS-IV and for the person who will hold a district

Federal courtesy is an ethical practice, not only a legal one.

- A Union officer who treats a State government as a field office of the ministry insults Article 1.
- A Chief Minister who treats Union law as optional insults Articles 256–257.
- Using 356 because an election was lost is **partisan use of a safety fuse**.
- Population policy that targets the poor woman’s body and ignores unpaid care, school quality, and clinic access fails the tests of dignity and justice.
- Demographic data can be used to plan schools and water, or to stigmatise a community. The same table serves both ends. The officer chooses.

**A one-line code for tonight:** *Power is concurrent; contempt is optional.*

# BLOCK G — CSAT with worked solutions (40 minutes)

CSAT is qualifying. Accuracy first. These five are in the Day-10 quantitative family: time-speed, work, and tables — the same skills you need when a district file mixes density, growth rates, and scheme coverage.

**Q1.** A train covers 180 km at 60 km/h and returns the same 180 km at 90 km/h. What is the average speed for the round trip?

**Worked solution.** Average speed is **not** the arithmetic mean when time spent at each speed differs.  
Time out = 180/60 = 3 h. Time back = 180/90 = 2 h. Total distance = 360 km. Total time = 5 h.  
Average speed = 360/5 = **72 km/h**.  
Formula when two equal distances are done at u and v: $2uv/(u+v) = 2\times60\times90/150 = 72$.

**Q2.** A can finish a job in 12 days, B in 18 days. They work together for 4 days. Then A leaves. How many more days does B need?

**Worked solution.** A’s one-day work = 1/12. B’s = 1/18. Together = (3+2)/36 = 5/36 per day.  
In 4 days they do 20/36 = 5/9. Remaining = 4/9.  
B alone does 1/18 per day, so days = (4/9) ÷ (1/18) = (4/9)×18 = **8 days**.

**Q3.** The population of a town is 80,000. It rises 10% in the first year and falls 10% in the second year. Population at the end of two years?

**Worked solution.** 10% up then 10% down is **not** a return to start.  
After year 1: 80,000 × 1.10 = 88,000.  
After year 2: 88,000 × 0.90 = **79,200**.  
Shortcut: successive +x% and −x% multiplies by $(1-x^2/10000)$. Here $1-100/10000=0.99$, and 80,000×0.99=79,200.

**Q4.** A table:

| District | Area (km²) | Population |
|---|---|---|
| P | 400 | 1,20,000 |
| Q | 600 | 1,50,000 |
| R | 200 | 80,000 |

Which district has the highest density, and what is that density?

**Worked solution.** Density = population / area.  
P: 1,20,000/400 = 300 per km².  
Q: 1,50,000/600 = 250.  
R: 80,000/200 = **400**.  
Highest is **R at 400 persons per km²**. Small area plus moderate population beats a large sparse district. This is the Bihar-versus-Rajasthan lesson in miniature.

**Q5.** A man walks from A to B at 5 km/h and returns at 4 km/h. The round trip takes 4.5 hours. Distance AB?

**Worked solution.** Let AB = d. Time = d/5 + d/4 = 4.5.  
$(4d+5d)/20 = 4.5$ → 9d/20 = 9/2 → 9d = 90 → **d = 10 km**.

**CSAT habit.** For two equal distances, harmonic logic. For population percentages, never add +10 and −10. For density, divide; do not compare raw population.

# BLOCK H — Active recall and model cores (20 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in one or two sentences. Then check the core.

**1.** Why does Article 1 say “Union of States” rather than “federation”?  
*Core:* The Union is indestructible and the units did not bargain from full sovereignty; Parliament may reshape States under Article 3; secession is not a constitutional option.

**2.** If a State law and a Union law on a Concurrent subject clash, who wins?  
*Core:* Union law, except that a later State law reserved for the President and assented to can operate in that State until Parliament legislates again (Article 254).

**3.** Name four ways Parliament can legislate on a State List subject without calling it “Emergency politics.”  
*Core:* Rajya Sabha 249 resolution; two-or-more States requesting under 252; treaty implementation under 253; and, separately, 250 when a 352 Emergency is already on.

**4.** Which Fundamental Rights cannot be suspended under Article 359?  
*Core:* Articles 20 and 21.

**5.** Does a National Emergency automatically dismiss State governments?  
*Core:* No. The Union gains direction and legislative reach; State ministries do not fall by the mere fact of 352. Dismissal is the 356 machine.

**6.** Why is Article 360 famous for never having been used?  
*Core:* It is a last-resort fiscal brake — salary cuts, State money bills reserved, Union directions on propriety. Political cost is huge; other tools have been used instead.

**7.** What is a demographic dividend, and why can it fail?  
*Core:* A high working-age share that can raise growth only if health, skills, and jobs exist; otherwise the bulge is unemployment and the window closes as the cohort ages.

**8.** Why is child sex ratio a sharper ethical indicator than overall sex ratio?  
*Core:* Overall ratio can rise because women live longer; child ratio records whether girl children are allowed to be born and to survive.

# BLOCK I — Memorise tonight (10 minutes)

Say these aloud twice.

- Article **1**: Union of States. Article **3**: Parliament may form or reshape States; State legislature gives **views**, not a veto. Article **4**: such a law is not a 368 amendment.
- **246 + Seventh Schedule**: Union / State / Concurrent. **248**: residuary → Parliament. **254**: Union prevails on Concurrent clash, with the Presidential-assent exception.
- Doors into the State List: **249** (Rajya Sabha 2/3, one year), **250** (during 352), **252** (two States request), **253** (treaty).
- **280** Finance Commission. **279A** GST Council. **262** river-water disputes. **263** Inter-State Council. **312** All-India Services.
- **352**: war, external aggression, armed rebellion; Cabinet in writing; 1-month special-majority approval; 6-month renewals; Article **19** auto-pause only for war/aggression; **20 and 21 never suspendable**.
- **356**: State machinery fails; 2-month approval; 6-month steps; 3-year cap with conditions; High Court stays; reviewable.
- **360**: financial stability; never used.
- Density = people/area. +x% then −x% ≠ zero change. Dividend = workers / dependents × skills × jobs.
- Calendar that is official: notification **13 Jan 2027**, apply by **2 Feb 2027**, Prelims **23 May 2027**, Mains from **20 Aug 2027**. Anything else about interviews is calendar consensus until notified.
