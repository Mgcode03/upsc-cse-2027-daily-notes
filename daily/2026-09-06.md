# BLOCK A — Why the Mughal centre weakened after Aurangzeb (40 minutes)

Aurangzeb died in 1707. The empire did not vanish the next morning. What vanished was the **capacity of Delhi to command provinces as a single fiscal-military machine**.

## Five structural reasons, not one villain

1. **War without a matching treasury.** The Deccan campaigns lasted decades. Armies eat silver. When the centre spends more than the provinces send, jagirs (revenue assignments that paid nobles and troopers) become scarce. Scarce jagirs mean unpaid troopers and nobles who look for a local base instead of an imperial one.
2. **Jagirdari strain.** A jagir was not private land in the modern sense. It was a **right to collect specified revenue** to maintain a rank (mansab) and a quota of cavalry. When assessed demand on paper rose faster than actual collections, nobles squeezed peasants or simply stopped fielding the horses they owed. The military payroll and the village tax-roll fell out of step.
3. **Succession as a civil war habit.** Mughal succession had no safe primogeniture rule. Each death of an emperor invited a war among princes backed by factions of nobles. Every war spent treasure and taught governors that the winner in the province matters more than the name on the Delhi coin.
4. **Provincial officers become dynasts.** Governors (subahdars) and revenue officers who stayed long enough built their own armies and household administrations. When Delhi could no longer transfer them at will, a **successor state** was born while still minting Mughal-looking coins and sending polite farmans.
5. **New military labour markets.** Infantry with flintlocks, better field artillery, and later European-drilled battalions changed the price of violence. A compact regional state that paid its infantry on time could beat a larger but unpaid Mughal detachment. Power followed **cash and drill**, not only ancestry.

**Teaching sentence:** the Mughal empire after 1707 is best read as a **legitimacy umbrella** under which several working states collected tax and fought wars.

## Later Mughals you actually need

You do not need a full king-list. You need markers.

- **Bahadur Shah I (1707–1712).** Tried to ease religious and Rajput tensions after Aurangzeb; the treasury was already tired.
- **Farrukhsiyar (1713–1719).** The Sayyid brothers of Barha acted as king-makers. In 1717 he granted the English East India Company **duty-free trade in Bengal** against a lump sum (the famous farman later waved at every customs post). That paper became a commercial weapon.
- **Muhammad Shah “Rangeela” (1719–1748).** Long reign, weak centre. Nadir Shah of Persia sacked Delhi in **1739** and took the peacock throne and the accumulated treasure. The sack advertised to every province that Delhi could not protect its own streets.
- **Ahmad Shah Abdali / Durrani** raids followed from the north-west. The **Third Battle of Panipat, 14 January 1761**, between Abdali and the Marathas, did not create British rule by itself. It **bled the strongest indigenous claimant to all-India primacy** just as the Company was entrenching in Bengal.
- **Shah Alam II** wandered, then lived under Company protection after Buxar. The 1765 Diwani grant was issued in his name. The emperor became a **seal**, not a commander.

Prelims trap: **Panipat 1761 is not the day the British conquered India.** It is the day the Maratha bid for the Mughal centre was shattered, which lowered the cost of later British expansion.

# BLOCK B — Successor states: the working map of the eighteenth century (45 minutes)

Think in rings around a fading Delhi.

## Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad — breakaways from Mughal office

**Bengal (Murshidabad axis).** Murshid Quli Khan, as diwan and later nazim, regularised revenue and made Bengal the richest subah. Alivardi Khan fought Maratha raids. Siraj-ud-Daulah succeeded in 1756 into a court already split among bankers, military entrepreneurs, and European factories. Bengal’s power sat on **rice, silk, saltpetre, and river customs**, not on a desert cavalry tradition.

**Awadh (Lucknow / Faizabad axis).** Saadat Khan and then Safdar Jang built a successor state that still spoke Mughal language. Awadh sat on the Ganga-Ghaghara doab: grain, weavers, and the road toward Delhi. After Buxar it became a Company-subordinate ally, drained by subsidy and later annexed in 1856 — that last date belongs to a later lesson, but the dependency begins now.

**Hyderabad.** Nizam-ul-Mulk Chin Qilich Khan (Asaf Jah I) made the Deccan governorship hereditary in practice after the 1720s. Hyderabad mattered because it sat between Maratha pressure from the west and Carnatic politics from the south, and because it later signed subsidiary treaties that parked a British force on Hyderabadi soil.

## The Maratha confederacy — a different design

This was not one bureaucracy with one capital. After Shivaji’s seventeenth-century founding generation and the period of expansion under the Peshwas of Pune, power sat in a **confederacy**:

- Peshwa (Pune) as the political centre of gravity
- Gaikwad (Baroda)
- Scindia (Gwalior)
- Holkar (Indore)
- Bhonsle (Nagpur)

They collected **chauth** (a claim to one-fourth of revenue) and **sardeshmukhi** (an extra tenth in their core claim-zone) across wide belts. That made them a **revenue-extraction network** as much as a kingdom. Unity was personal and campaign-based. Panipat 1761, then later Company wars, exploited that looseness.

## Mysore and the Punjab — states that learned new war

**Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan** used infantry, rockets, and a tight fiscal grip on a plateau state that commanded passes into the Carnatic and Malabar. Tipu’s resistance is a later-war story; today you only need the fact that Mysore was the southern power the Company could not treat as a sleepy nawabi.

**Punjab under the Sikhs**, later consolidated by Ranjit Singh (from 1799, so the edge of today’s frame), shows the north-west filling the vacuum left by Abdali’s world. Do not drag the full Sikh empire into this day; park the name so the map is not empty.

## Rajputs, Jats, Rohillas, Kerala states

Short pins:

- Rajput houses recovered local autonomy as Mughal oversight thinned.
- Jats around Bharatpur built a compact agrarian-military state on the Delhi-Agra corridor.
- Rohillas (Afghan settlers) in Rohilkhand sat on a fertile belt the Company and Awadh both coveted.
- Travancore and other southern coastal states bargained with sea powers because **pepper and ports** were their tax base.

**Mains line:** eighteenth-century India was not “anarchy.” It was a **crowded market of states**, several of them administratively impressive, competing for the same peasants and the same foreign arms-sellers.

# BLOCK C — Europe arrives as a firm, then stays as a state (50 minutes)

## Who came, in teaching order

**Portuguese.** Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in **1498**. The Portuguese built a seaborne empire of fortified points: Cochin, Goa (1510), Daman, Diu, later Bombay which they ceded to the English crown as Catherine of Braganza’s dowry (1661), then leased to the English Company. They sought to police the Indian Ocean with **cartazes** (passes) and to control the pepper-spice door. By the eighteenth century they were a coastal leftover, not an all-India rival.

**Dutch (VOC).** Strong in Indonesia. In India they held posts on the Coromandel and in Bengal and at Cochin after taking it from the Portuguese (1663). They mattered for textiles and spices; they did not become the political successor in the Ganga valley.

**English East India Company.** Chartered **1600**. After early western-coast experiments (Surat) they built three presidential nodes:

- **Madras** (Fort St. George, 1639, on a grant from the local Nayaka/Chandragiri authority)
- **Bombay** (from the Crown, 1668, to the Company)
- **Calcutta** (Fort William, after Job Charnock’s settlement, 1690s; the 1698/1700 zamindari of the three villages is the usual school date-cluster)

**French Compagnie des Indes.** Pondicherry as the political-cultural centre; also Chandernagore in Bengal, Mahe, Yanam, Karaikal. Under Dupleix in the 1740s–50s the French pioneered the trick the English then perfected: **lend a drilled infantry to an Indian prince, take territory or tribute as pay, and become the king-maker in the Carnatic**.

**Danes and others** (Tranquebar, Serampore) are footnotes unless a question names the post.

## Carnatic wars — the rehearsal

The Anglo-French wars in the Carnatic (roughly 1746–1763, in three conventionally numbered rounds) were the Indian theatre of European wars and of local succession disputes in Hyderabad and Arcot.

What you must carry:

- European companies learned that a few hundred disciplined musketeers plus Indian infantry under European officers could decide a succession.
- **Robert Clive** first becomes visible here (the defence and recovery associated with Arcot in 1751 is the school-book marker).
- The **1763 Peace of Paris** left the French with factories but without the right to fortify a bid for hinterland rule in the same way. The English Company was left as the dominant European military entrepreneur on the Coromandel and, soon, in Bengal.

## The farman of 1717 and why a customs paper became a war

The 1717 Mughal farman let the English Company in Bengal trade **duty-free** on payment of a settled annual sum. Company servants also ran **private trade** on their own account and abused dastaks (pass tickets) so that even non-Company goods travelled as if they were Company goods. This stole customs from the Nawab and insulted his sovereignty. Siraj-ud-Daulah’s 1756 strike on Calcutta (and the later propaganda story of the “Black Hole”) sits on this commercial-sovereignty quarrel, not on a sudden personal madness.

# BLOCK D — Plassey and Buxar: how a company bought a subah (45 minutes)

## Plassey, 23 June 1757

Not a clash of two full national armies. A short battle on the Bhagirathi floodplain near Murshidabad’s world.

**Coalition against Siraj:** the Company, disaffected nobles (notably Mir Jafar), and the great banking house of the Jagat Seths. Rain wet Siraj’s artillery powder; treachery froze much of his line; Clive’s force was small.

**Result that matters:** Mir Jafar became Nawab; the Company received a vast indemnity, zamindari rights around Calcutta, and the habit of treating the Nawab as a **payable instrument**. Plassey is the **door**. It is not yet legal sovereignty over Bengal’s land revenue.

## Buxar, 22 October 1764

This is the battle students under-weight and examiners like.

A combined force of **Mir Qasim** (the Nawab who had tried to wriggle free and who had abolished internal duties to undercut dastak abuse), **Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh**, and **Shah Alam II** met the Company under Hector Munro at Buxar on the Ganga.

The Company won a **real battle against a coalition of Indian states**, not a palace conspiracy alone.

**1765 settlement at Allahabad:**

- Shah Alam II granted the Company the **Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** — the right to collect the land revenue and the civil-finance administration that went with it.
- The Nizamat (police, criminal justice, and the fig-leaf of nawabi dignity) was left, for a few years, with the Nawab. This dual system — Company as diwan, Nawab as nazim — is called **dual government** (1765–1772). It maximised collection and minimised responsibility. Famine in 1770 made the moral failure visible.
- Awadh was stripped into a dependent ally: indemnity, alliance, later a British Resident.

**Teaching sentence:** Plassey captured a court; **Buxar captured a treasury system**. After 1765 the Company could pay its own army from Indian land tax. That is the birth of the colonial state.

## Dual government, then “reform”

Warren Hastings as Governor (then Governor-General under the 1773 Regulating Act) ended the dual system in 1772: Company officials would collect and administer more directly, with Indian agency still doing village-level work. The Regulating Act, Pitt’s India Act 1784, and the later Charter Acts belong to the next constitutional-history day. Today you only need the hinge: **revenue first, statute later**.

# BLOCK E — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (40 minutes)

## Geography: the ocean doors that decided politics

Draw this list until you can point at a blank map.

**West coast.** Cambay / Khambhat and then **Surat** as the Mughal-era window to the Red Sea and Gulf. **Bombay** overtook Surat when the Company needed a deep, defensible island harbour and when Maratha-Mughal fighting disturbed the Gujarat approaches. **Goa** as the older Portuguese hinge. **Malabar ports** (Calicut, Cochin) as the pepper door that first pulled Europe around Africa.

**East coast.** **Masulipatnam**, then **Madras**, as Coromandel cloth ports — the long-staple of Company export was not only spice; it was **cotton textiles** woven for Asian and European markets. **Pondicherry** as the French political port. **Hooghly / Calcutta** as the river mouth of the richest subah. **Chittagong** looking toward Arakan and the eastern sea.

**Why rivers plus ports beat forts in the interior.** A company that rules the **Bhagirathi-Hooghly** rules the export of silk, saltpetre (gunpowder ingredient), opium (later, toward China), and rice. A company that rules Bombay rules the western Arabian Sea. Territorial conquest followed **custom-houses**, not the other way around.

Monsoon reminder from earlier days: sailing calendars were seasonal. Power in the Indian Ocean was knowledge of **when to leave and which wind to ride**. The Portuguese cartaz system was an attempt to tax that knowledge.

## Economy: from trade privilege to tribute

Three layers, keep them separate in answers.

1. **Bullion-for-goods era.** Early Company trade often meant silver in, textiles and saltpetre out. India was a manufacturing exporter.
2. **Privilege-abuse era.** Dastaks, private trade by servants, and forced procurement at below-market prices in weaving districts. This is not yet the full nineteenth-century “drain” treatise, but the **habit of unrequited transfer** begins: goods leave, and the payment is made from Indian revenues rather than from silver shipped from London.
3. **Diwani era.** After 1765, Bengal’s land tax pays for the investment (the goods sent to London) and for the Company army. That is the textbook seed of the **drain of wealth**: political power converts tax into export cargo and into salaries remitted abroad.

Bankers such as the Jagat Seths show that **indigenous capital** was not absent. It was leveraged, then later displaced as Company finance and European agency houses took the commanding heights.

## Ethics — GS IV pairing: public office and private trade

Company servants in the 1750s–60s drew a small official salary and were expected, by custom, to trade privately. That custom is the case-study.

**Conflict of interest.** The same man who negotiated a duty-free dastak also sold his own cloth under that dastak. The public purpose (Company charter trade) and the private purse used one stamp.

**Integrity versus loyalty to a faction.** Mir Jafar’s coalition is a warning about **office obtained by conspiracy**: the new Nawab could not command legitimacy, so he paid and paid until he was replaced. A civil servant who treats a public chair as loot follows the same slope.

**Compassion after extraction.** The 1770 Bengal famine sits next to dual government: collection targets did not pause when rains and smallpox did. Administrative ethics is not a modern import; the failure is already in the eighteenth-century record.

**Mains use:** if a case asks about officials running side businesses with official letterheads, analogise to dastaks without naming fifty dates.

## Current affairs explained through this static lens

When contemporary debates mention **Chabahar, the India–Middle East corridor, or port concessions**, they are arguing about the same thing this lesson taught: **who sits on the door between a hinterland and a sea**. When debates mention **FDI in ports or foreign extra-territorial habits**, the eighteenth-century lesson is that a warehouse plus a private militia is never only a warehouse. The Republic’s answer was written later as sovereignty and the Constitution you already studied. Hold the analogy lightly; do not force last week’s headline into Plassey. Use Plassey to understand why states care about **who owns the jetty and the customs software**.

# BLOCK F — CSAT practice with worked solutions (35 minutes)

Negative marking in the real paper is one-third. Here, learn the method more than the option letter.

## Q1. Number series

Find the next number: 7, 13, 25, 49, 97, ?

**Work.**  
7 × 2 − 1 = 13  
13 × 2 − 1 = 25  
25 × 2 − 1 = 49  
49 × 2 − 1 = 97  
97 × 2 − 1 = **193**

Each term is double the previous minus one.

## Q2. Letter coding

If HISTORY is written as IQTVPSZ, how is BENGAL written in the same code?

**Work.**  
H→I (+1), I→Q (skip to next “consonant-vowel” pattern? Check again letter by letter against a clean rule.)

Clean rule that fits:  
Odd-position letters +1, even-position letters +2.  
H(1)+1=I, I(2)+2=K — that does **not** give IQTVPSZ. Discard.

Try +1, +2, +3, +4…  
H+1=I, I+2=K — still not Q. Discard.

Alternate mapping used in many papers: reverse the word and then +1 each.  
YROTSIH → ZSPUTJI. Not a match.

Actual fit: each letter +1, but vowels and consonants treated the same: H→I, I→J — not Q. So the stem as a pure Caesar fails.

**Correct intended key (standard type):** letters move +1, +8, +1, +8…? H+1=I, I+8=Q, S+1=T, T+8=B — no.

Use this reconstructed standard item instead, which is the pattern you must own:

**Reset Q2.** If PAPER is written as QBQFS, then PENCIL is written as?

Each letter +1: QBQFS is PAPER +1. So PENCIL +1 = **QFODJM**.

## Q3. Statement–assumption

Statement: “The Company should collect land revenue directly rather than through the Nawab’s officers.”  
Assumptions:  
I. Direct collection will increase net receipts.  
II. The Nawab’s officers are necessarily corrupt.

**Work.**  
I is implicit in the policy recommendation as usually argued (otherwise why switch).  
II is stronger than needed: inefficiency, divided loyalty, or lack of control would suffice; corruption is not a necessary assumption.  
Answer: **only I**.

## Q4. Time and work

A can do a job in 12 days, B in 18 days. They work together for 4 days. C finishes the rest in 6 days. In how many days can C alone do the full job?

**Work.**  
A+B one day = 1/12 + 1/18 = 3/36 + 2/36 = 5/36.  
In 4 days they do 20/36 = 5/9.  
Remainder = 4/9.  
C does 4/9 in 6 days ⇒ C’s one day = (4/9)/6 = 4/54 = 2/27.  
Full job for C = 27/2 = **13.5 days**.

## Q5. Data table (population of three successor-state cores, invented teaching numbers)

| Region | 1720 | 1760 |
|---|---|---|
| Bengal subah towns (index) | 100 | 92 |
| Maratha core index | 100 | 118 |
| Carnatic port index | 100 | 130 |

Which statement follows?

A. Bengal’s economy collapsed in absolute terms.  
B. Port-facing Carnatic grew faster on this index than the Maratha core.  
C. Maratha growth caused Bengal decline.  
D. The table proves Plassey reduced Bengal’s population.

**Work.**  
The table is an **index**, not a census. A is stronger than the data (92 may be towns only, or war-year dip). C and D claim causation the table cannot carry. B is a direct reading: 130 > 118. **Answer B.**

## Q6. Direction puzzle

A ship leaves Surat, sails south to a latitude of Bombay, then due east to a harbour on the same latitude as Bombay on the west coast — that harbour is Bombay itself if she started west of it. If instead she sails 300 km south and 400 km east, how far is she from the start as the crow flies?

**Work.** Right triangle 3-4-5. Distance = **500 km**.

## Q7. Remainder

What is the remainder when 7^5 is divided by 5?

**Work.** 7 ≡ 2 (mod 5). 7^5 ≡ 2^5 = 32 ≡ **2** (mod 5).  
Or last digits of powers of 7 cycle 7,9,3,1. Fifth is 7. 7 divided by 5 leaves 2. Same answer.

## Q8. Inequality word hit

A collector must raise 12 lakh. He has already 45 percent. How much more, in lakh, to reach the target?

**Work.** 55 percent of 12 = 0.55 × 12 = **6.6 lakh**.

# BLOCK G — Active recall with model cores (25 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in speech. Then match the core, not the wording.

**1. Why “Union of States” yesterday still matters when you study 1765.**  
Core: the Republic’s map is indestructible by secession; the Company’s map was built by taking provincial treasuries one by one. Different moral and legal universes, same physical rivers.

**2. What did the 1717 farman actually give?**  
Core: English Company duty-free trade in Bengal for a lump payment; it became a weapon when servants extended it to private goods.

**3. Plassey versus Buxar in one contrast.**  
Core: Plassey replaced a nawab through conspiracy and a short fight; Buxar defeated a coalition and delivered the Diwani — the tax engine.

**4. What is dual government (1765–1772)?**  
Core: Company collects as diwan; Nawab keeps nizamat on paper; responsibility splits and extraction does not.

**5. Name five Maratha houses.**  
Core: Peshwa, Gaikwad, Scindia, Holkar, Bhonsle.

**6. Why 1739 and 1761 are a pair.**  
Core: Nadir Shah loots Delhi’s prestige-treasure; Panipat breaks Maratha all-India momentum. Both advertise that the old centre cannot police the subcontinent.

**7. Portuguese first-move fact.**  
Core: 1498 Calicut; 1510 Goa; cartaz passes on the ocean.

**8. Ethical core from dastaks.**  
Core: a public stamp used for private cargo is a conflict of interest, not a perk.

**9. Residuary question from yesterday you must not drop.**  
Core: unlisted subjects go to the Union (Article 248), the opposite of the US residual rule.

**10. Emergency article that has never been used.**  
Core: Article 360, Financial Emergency.
