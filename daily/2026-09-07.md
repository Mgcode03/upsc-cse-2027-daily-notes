# BLOCK A — Dual government dies; Parliament notices the Company (45 minutes)

## Dual government, 1765–1772

After Allahabad 1765 the Company held the **Diwani** of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa: the right to collect land revenue and run the civil-finance side. The Nawab kept the **Nizamat**: police, criminal justice, and the ceremonial title. That split is dual government.

**Why it was attractive to the Company.** Collection without the full cost of being seen as the king. Indian collectors still sat in the districts. Company servants took a cut through private trade and presents. London received investment goods paid for out of Bengal tax, not only out of silver shipped from Europe.

**Why it failed as administration.** Power without responsibility. Demand was kept high. The **Bengal famine of 1769–70** killed a large share of the population in the worst-hit districts. Grain left the province; relief was thin; revenue targets were not honestly paused. The scandal reached Parliament because the Company was a **chartered English corporation**, not a hidden private club.

**Teaching sentence:** dual government maximised extraction and minimised duty; the famine made that design politically expensive in London.

Warren Hastings, as Governor of Bengal from 1772 (Governor-General under the later statute), ended the dual system in practice: Company officers would supervise collection more directly; the Nawab’s stipend continued as a political pension. Village-level work still ran through Indian agency. Direct rule did not mean Englishmen in every village. It meant **Englishmen at the district treasury**.

## Why British Parliament intervened

Three pressures, keep them distinct:

1. **Moral-political scandal.** Reports of presents, private fortunes, and famine.
2. **Company finance.** The Company could be rich in Bengal and still shaky in London if wars, dividends, and tea-China accounts misaligned. A bankrupt Company that ruled a subah was a state problem.
3. **Constitutional jealousy.** No private board in Leadenhall Street should wage war and make treaties without the Crown and Parliament having a handle.

## Regulating Act, 1773 — the first statute you must own

This is the first major parliamentary attempt to regulate the Company as a **political** body.

Carry these operative points:

- A **Governor-General of Bengal** with a four-member **Council**. Warren Hastings was the first Governor-General under this Act.
- The Governors of Madras and Bombay were, in theory, made subordinate to Bengal in questions of war and peace. In practice the presidencies still quarrelled; later Acts had to tighten this.
- A **Supreme Court at Calcutta** (1774) with Sir Elijah Impey as the first Chief Justice. Jurisdiction over British subjects was clearer than jurisdiction over Indians; conflict with the Governor-General-in-Council produced famous collisions (the Nandakumar case is the school-book illustration of judicial-executive friction, not a law you must recite).
- Company directors in London faced new reporting duties to the British government.

**Prelims trap:** 1773 does **not** create a Governor-General of *India*. That title and all-India statutory frame arrive later (1833). 1773 creates a Governor-General of *Bengal* with a claim to oversee the other two presidencies.

**Pitt’s India Act, 1784** — second statute you must own.

- Created a **Board of Control** in Britain (commissioners appointed by the Crown) to supervise the Company’s civil, military and revenue affairs. Court of Directors kept commercial patronage and some appointments. This is **dual control at the London end**: Board for politics, Directors for trade and jobs.
- Strengthened the Governor-General relative to his Council (the habit of a deadlocked council had paralysed Hastings).
- Stated more clearly that conquest for its own sake was not the Company’s official purpose — a pious sentence that Wellesley would ignore in practice.

**Charter Act of 1793** renewed the Company’s commercial monopoly for twenty years and continued the 1784 machinery. You need it only as a renewal peg. The big monopoly-break is 1813 (next history day). Do not dump 1813, 1833 and 1853 into today; park them.

**Teaching sentence:** 1773 put a statutory Governor-General and a Crown court in Calcutta; 1784 put a Crown board over the Company in London. Revenue had created a state; statute now admitted it.

## Hastings in one working paragraph

Do not write a biography. Write functions.

- Ended dual government (1772) and tried to stabilise revenue through five-year farm-out experiments that largely failed and were later replaced.
- Moved against the Rohillas in alliance with Awadh (Rohilla war, 1774) — later used by his enemies as a charge of aggressive politics.
- Fought the First Anglo-Maratha War framework and the First Anglo-Mysore War at the same time as presidency rivalries — the Company was not yet a single brain.
- Impeachment in the British Parliament after his return (trial 1788–1795) ended in acquittal. For GS, the trial is evidence that **metropolitan politics now audited Indian governors**. For Prelims, acquittal is the fact; Burke’s speeches are colour, not a date-list.

# BLOCK B — Cornwallis, the civil service idea, and Permanent Settlement (50 minutes)

Charles Cornwallis arrived as Governor-General in 1786 with a soldier’s brief: clean the service, fix the law courts, lock the land revenue.

## The Cornwallis code habit

Three linked reforms, always keep together:

1. **Separation of revenue and justice at the district**, then partial re-welding. Collectors were stripped of judicial power for a time so that the man who demanded tax would not also sit as judge in the same quarrel. Later generations found pure separation clumsy in a peasant society and adjusted it. The *principle* you need is the recognition of **conflict of interest** inside one officer.
2. **Europeanisation of the higher covenanted service.** Indians were shut out of the top offices. Cornwallis believed patronage and private trade among Company men, and what he saw as “native corruption,” required a well-paid European elite bound by rules. This is the seed of the **covenanted civil service** idea: salary high enough that private trade could be banned.
3. **Private trade ban for collectors and judges.** The dastak-era habit from yesterday’s lesson is formally attacked here. A public officer was to live on pay.

Police and criminal law were also restated in regulations. You do not need section numbers. You need the picture: **written regulations, European collectors, zamindar as the state’s revenue counterpart in Bengal**.

## Permanent Settlement of Bengal, 1793

This is a core Prelims and Mains item. Learn the mechanism, not a slogan.

**What was settled.** The land revenue demand payable by the **zamindar** to the Company was fixed in perpetuity for Bengal, Bihar and (with local variation) Orissa. The zamindar became, in Company law, the proprietor so long as he paid that fixed tax on time. Failure meant sale of the estate at auction.

**What was not settled.** The rent the peasant (raiyat) paid to the zamindar was **not** given the same iron permanence in practice. Law later tried to define occupancy rights; enforcement was patchy. So “permanent” describes the **state–zamindar** line more honestly than the **zamindar–peasant** line.

**Why Cornwallis and the Court of Directors wanted it.**

- Predictable Company income for war and dividend.
- A loyal rural aristocracy that would support the regime, on the analogy of English landlords.
- An end to yearly haggling and to Hastings-era farming of revenues to the highest bidder.

**What followed on the ground.**

- Many old zamindari houses could not pay the first high demand and lost estates at **sunset sales**.
- A new purchaser class — often urban moneyed groups — bought paper rights over villages they barely knew.
- Agricultural improvement, the hoped-for English-landlord miracle, was uneven. A zamindar who could raise rent without investing in irrigation often chose rent.
- The Company froze its own claim just before prices and cultivation in some belts rose, so later generations of officials complained that Bengal “under-paid” the state compared with Madras and Bombay.

**Teaching sentence:** Permanent Settlement privatised the state’s revenue intermediary, froze the government demand, and left the peasant under a landlord whose own title depended on punctual payment to the collector.

## The other two systems — learn the contrast table in words

Do not wait for a later day. Examiners love a three-column contrast.

**Ryotwari** (developed most clearly in Madras under Thomas Munro and in Bombay under later settlements).

- The state settled with the **raiyat** (the cultivating occupant), not with a great zamindar.
- Demand was not perpetual in the Bengal sense; it was revised in survey cycles.
- The peasant was treated as the proprietor so long as he paid, but the state remained a very present landlord.
- Survey and measurement became the administrative obsession: fields, soils, and a register.

**Mahalwari** (North-Western Provinces, later parts of Punjab and Central India — Holt Mackenzie’s thinking, then modifications).

- Settlement with a **mahal** (a fiscal village or estate) through the village body or a joint body of co-sharers (sometimes a lambardar as the payer-of-record).
- A middle path: not one giant Bengal zamindar, not always a pure individual ryot plot.
- Revision possible; village custom and recorded shares mattered.

**Memory hook.**  
Bengal: pay through the **landlord**.  
Madras-Bombay: pay as the **plot cultivator**.  
Ganga-Yamuna upper country: pay as the **village / co-sharer block**.

**Mains line:** revenue systems were not technical footnotes. They decided **who was the state’s face in the village**, who could be sold up, and who later joined or opposed the 1857 rising. Park 1857; keep the causal hook.

# BLOCK C — Mysore wars: the southern power that fought like a state (40 minutes)

Mysore under **Haidar Ali** and **Tipu Sultan** was not a sleepy nawabi. It was a plateau kingdom that taxed tightly, drilled infantry, used rockets, and sat on the passes between the Carnatic coast and the western pepper ports.

## Why Mysore collided with the Company

- Control of the **Carnatic** (the Coromandel hinterland around Arcot and Madras) was the Company’s southern oxygen.
- Mysore wanted access to the Malabar coast and refused to be a quiet buffer.
- French officers and French hopes lingered around Tipu even after 1763; London read Mysore as a possible French door.
- Maratha and Nizam pressure from the north meant every war was a **four-player** game: Company, Mysore, Nizam, Marathas. Treaties signed with one were often broken when the other two moved.

## Four wars, four outcomes — this is the list you recite

**First Anglo-Mysore War, 1767–1769.**  
Haidar Ali fought the Company and the Nizam, reached the outskirts of Madras, and forced the **Treaty of Madras (1769)**: mutual restoration and a defensive alliance. The Company did not yet look invincible in the south.

**Second Anglo-Mysore War, 1780–1784.**  
Part of the wider world war in which France was again hostile to Britain. Haidar and then Tipu (Haidar died in 1782) fought Sir Eyre Coote and others. **Treaty of Mangalore, 1784**: status-quo exchange of conquests. This is the rare treaty in which the Company did **not** dictate a new map. Prelims likes that fact.

**Third Anglo-Mysore War, 1790–1792.**  
Cornwallis in person. Company + Nizam + Marathas against Tipu. **Treaty of Seringapatam, 1792**: Tipu ceded about half his territory, paid a heavy indemnity, and gave two sons as hostages. Malabar and the Salem-Baramahal belts moved toward Company or allied hands. Mysore was cut down, not yet killed.

**Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, 1799.**  
Wellesley. Seringapatam stormed. **Tipu killed in May 1799.** The Company annexed the strategic belt (including the coast it needed) and installed a restored Wodeyar child under a **subsidiary** arrangement. Mysore as an independent military peer ended.

**Teaching sentence:** Mysore taught the Company that a compact Indian state with a paid army could threaten Madras; 1799 removed that peer.

**Rockets.** Tipu’s iron-cased rockets impressed British officers and later influenced Congreve’s work in Europe. A one-line science-and-war extra, not a full science lesson.

# BLOCK D — Maratha wars and Wellesley’s subsidiary system (40 minutes)

## The confederacy problem, restated

Peshwa at Pune, Gaikwad at Baroda, Scindia at Gwalior, Holkar at Indore, Bhonsle at Nagpur. After Panipat 1761 they recovered regionally but not as one cabinet. Company diplomacy bought one house to fight another.

## Three Anglo-Maratha wars

**First, 1775–1782.**  
Triggered by Bombay’s meddling in a Peshwa succession (Ruganath Rao / Raghunathrao as the Company’s client). Bengal under Hastings did not want Bombay’s war. Fighting and intrigue ended in the **Treaty of Salbai, 1782**: twenty years of relative quiet with the Peshwa line recognised in a way the Company could live with. Status quo, not conquest.

**Second, 1803–1805.**  
The real smash. **Treaty of Bassein, 1802**: Peshwa Baji Rao II, humiliated by Holkar, accepted a British subsidiary force. Scindia and Bhonsle read this as the Peshwa selling the confederacy. Wellesley’s armies under Arthur Wellesley (later Wellington) and Lake won at **Assaye (1803)**, Argaon, Delhi-Laswari and allied fields. Delhi’s Mughal emperor passed more completely under Company protection. Huge territorial slices in the Doab, Orissa (from Bhonsle), parts of western India. Holkar fought on into 1805; the war closed with the Company as the **arbiter of north-western and central politics**, even if Holkar was not fully crushed.

**Third, 1817–1819.**  
Lord Hastings (Moira), not Warren Hastings — **name trap**. Peshwa Baji Rao II rose, was broken, and was pensioned to Bithoor near Kanpur. The Peshwa office ended. Satara was kept as a small dynastic sliver for a while. Scindia, Holkar, Gaikwad, Bhonsle survived as **dependent princes** on shorter leashes. Pindari bands, which had nested in the same war-economy, were hunted in the same campaigning season.

**Teaching sentence:** Salbai paused the contest; Bassein split the confederacy; 1818 abolished the Peshwa and left Maratha houses as princely clients.

## Subsidiary alliance — Wellesley’s template

Do not recite a catalogue of every signer. Learn the **clauses that always appear**:

1. The Indian ruler pays for a **British-officered contingent** stationed in or near his territory.
2. He does **not** host other European officers without Company leave (the anti-French clause).
3. He lets the Company guide **external relations**.
4. He accepts a **Resident** at court.
5. In return he is promised protection against enemies — a promise that also makes him unable to build an independent coalition.

Classic early signers to keep: **Nizam (1798)**, **Mysore after 1799**, **Awadh** in tightening forms, **Peshwa at Bassein 1802**. Tanjore and Travancore appear in many lists; use them only if a question names them.

**Fiscal effect.** The subsidy was often more than the court could pay without assigning districts. Assigned districts became Company-run. Alliance ate sovereignty from the treasury end.

Wellesley also used **outright annexation** when he could (parts of Mysore, the Carnatic settlement after the Nawab of Arcot’s debts, western slices). His recall in 1805 shows London’s fear of endless war bills, not a change of the underlying logic. The logic returned under Lord Hastings.

# BLOCK E — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (40 minutes)

## Geography you must be able to point at

**Mysore plateau and gates.**  
Seringapatam (Srirangapatna) sits on an island in the Kaveri — that is why it was a fortress-capital. The **Gajalhatti and other passes** link the plateau to the Carnatic plains around Salem, Coimbatore and Arcot. Malabar (Calicut, Cannanore, Cochin neighbourhood) is the western pepper-and-port door Tipu needed and the Company denied him. When you see “why Madras feared Mysore,” answer: **the Carnatic is a coastal plain with no wall; whoever holds the plateau edge holds the supply roads to Fort St. George.**

**Maratha belt.**  
Western Ghats and the Desh around Pune; Khandesh; the Narmada crossing toward Malwa; Gwalior watching the Chambal-Yamuna approach to Agra-Delhi; Nagpur on the Wardha-Wainganga and the road to Cuttack-Orissa; Baroda on the Gujarat plain. Assaye (1803) is in the Godavari-Purna country of present-day Maharashtra — a reminder that Deccan black soils fed the cavalry states you are studying.

**Bengal revenue geography, restated.**  
Permanent Settlement sat on a **floodplain rice-silk country** with older zamindari titles and river commerce. Ryotwari sat more naturally on **peninsular landscapes** where the Company, after wars, faced villages rather than one Murshidabad-type aristocracy. Settlement form followed **who already stood between the peasant and the state**, and then froze that person in law.

## Economy: land tax as the Company’s steel plant

Three cash facts to keep clean:

1. After 1765 the Bengal surplus paid for China tea purchases and for Indian infantry. That is why a famine in Bengal was also a **balance-of-payments** event for a corporation.
2. Permanent Settlement traded future upside for present certainty. When agricultural prices later moved, the state could not automatically share the gain; zamindars and intermediaries could.
3. Subsidiary subsidies were **land revenue by another name**. A prince who assigned districts to pay the contingent was already half-annexed.

Weavers in the Carnatic and Bengal still mattered, but today’s lesson is the **agrarian foundation of the army**. No punctual kist (instalment), no battalion.

## Ethics — GS IV pairing: rule-bound office versus conquest by contract

**Cornwallis and integrity.** Banning private trade and paying collectors better is the classic public-service argument: **reduce the need to steal**. It came packaged with racial exclusion. An ethics answer can hold both: the rule “no side business while you hold a collectorate” is sound; the rule “only Europeans may hold the collectorate” is a justice failure.

**Permanent Settlement as a values choice.** Certainty for the treasury versus security for the cultivator. A policy that sells a defaulting estate at sunset privileges **contract with the payer-of-record** over **continuity of the village**. When a GS IV case asks you to choose between meeting a revenue target and preventing dispossession, this is your historical mirror.

**Subsidiary alliance as consent under duress.** A treaty signed by a prince whose capital is one march away from a Company army is not free consent. Modern public ethics still asks whether a weaker party’s signature is enough when the stronger party wrote every clause.

**Compassion.** Dual government plus the 1770 famine remains the warning: an officer who says “collection is my only KPI” has already left the people outside the frame.

## Current affairs explained through this static lens

When contemporary Indian debate discusses **land records, farm-loan waivers, or who is recorded as the cultivator on a digital title**, you are hearing echoes of ryotwari versus zamindari: **the state must know whom it is taxing and whom it is protecting**. When debate discusses **foreign military basing, visiting forces, or extra-territorial logistics agreements**, subsidiary alliance is the cautionary tale — not because a modern pact is the same as 1802, but because **a security guarantee can hollow out independent external choice**. Use the analogy in one sentence; then return to present facts. Do not pretend Tipu is a headline from this week.

Port modernisation on the Malabar and Coromandel coasts still sits on the same geographic truth: **the plateau and the plain meet at passes, and the passes meet the sea at a few working harbours**.

# BLOCK F — CSAT practice with worked solutions (35 minutes)

Negative marking in the real paper is one-third. Learn the method.

## Q1. Ratio and land revenue

A mahal pays a total demand of Rs 12,000. Three co-sharers A, B and C hold shares in the ratio 3 : 5 : 4. B sells one-fifth of his share to A. What is the new ratio of A : B : C in the demand?

**Work.**  
Parts = 3 + 5 + 4 = 12. One part = 12,000 / 12 = 1,000.  
A = 3,000, B = 5,000, C = 4,000.  
B sells one-fifth of his share: (1/5) × 5,000 = 1,000.  
A becomes 4,000; B becomes 4,000; C stays 4,000.  
New ratio = **1 : 1 : 1**.

## Q2. Ages

The present ages of a Collector and a Deputy are in the ratio 7 : 3. After 8 years the ratio will be 3 : 2. Find the present age of the Collector.

**Work.**  
Let present ages be 7x and 3x.  
(7x + 8) / (3x + 8) = 3/2  
2(7x + 8) = 3(3x + 8)  
14x + 16 = 9x + 24  
5x = 8  
x = 8/5 = 1.6  
Collector = 7 × 1.6 = **11.2 years**.

That is absurd for officers, which is the point of checking: if options in the real paper were all adult, you would re-read the stem. Here the algebra is correct; the lesson is **always test the answer against life** before bubbling. A standard exam variant uses “after 8 years ratio 15 : 7” or similar to keep ages adult. Method first.

**Adult variant you should also run.** Present ratio 7 : 3; after 8 years 15 : 7.  
(7x + 8)/(3x + 8) = 15/7  
7(7x + 8) = 15(3x + 8)  
49x + 56 = 45x + 120  
4x = 64  
x = 16  
Collector = **112 years** — still silly.  
Better standard: present 8 : 3, after 6 years 5 : 2.  
(8x+6)/(3x+6) = 5/2  
2(8x+6) = 5(3x+6)  
16x + 12 = 15x + 30  
x = 18  
Ages **144 and 54** — still strained.  
Use this clean one: present 5 : 3; after 10 years 7 : 5.  
(5x+10)/(3x+10) = 7/5  
5(5x+10) = 7(3x+10)  
25x + 50 = 21x + 70  
4x = 20  
x = 5  
Present ages **25 and 15**. Collector-type figure if restated as father-son. **Answer for the clean twin: 25 years** for the elder.

## Q3. Partnership

Three officers invest in a regulated-trade account (imagined). A puts Rs 40,000 for 12 months, B puts Rs 60,000 for 8 months, C puts Rs 80,000 for 6 months. Profit is Rs 26,000. Find B’s share.

**Work.**  
Capital-months: A = 40,000 × 12 = 4,80,000  
B = 60,000 × 8 = 4,80,000  
C = 80,000 × 6 = 4,80,000  
Ratio 1 : 1 : 1. B’s share = 26,000 / 3 = **Rs 8,666.67** (or 26,000 × 1/3).

If the paper wants integers, profit Rs 27,000 → **Rs 9,000**.

## Q4. Syllogism

Statements:  
All zamindars are proprietors.  
Some proprietors are indebted.  
No indebted person is a covenanted officer.

Conclusions:  
I. Some zamindars are indebted.  
II. No zamindar is a covenanted officer.  
III. Some proprietors are not covenanted officers.

**Work.**  
All Z ⊂ P. Some P are I. No I is C.  
I does not follow: the indebted proprietors need not be the zamindar subset.  
II does not follow: zamindars who are not indebted could still be officers (the statements never block that).  
III follows: the indebted proprietors are a living “some P” that cannot be C. So **only III**.

## Q5. Percentage and settlement revision

A ryotwari demand is raised by 20% at revision. Next cycle it is reduced by 20% of the new demand. Is the cultivator back to the original demand?

**Work.**  
Let original = 100.  
After +20% = 120.  
After −20% of 120 = 120 − 24 = 96.  
Net **4% less** than original, not the same. Successive +x% and −x% never cancel.

## Q6. Time and work

A survey party of 12 amins can measure a taluk in 18 days. After 6 days, 4 amins are pulled to a court commission. How many more days will the remaining party need to finish?

**Work.**  
Total work = 12 × 18 = 216 amin-days.  
Work done in 6 days = 12 × 6 = 72.  
Left = 144.  
Remaining men = 8.  
Days more = 144 / 8 = **18 days**.

## Q7. Direction

A Resident travels 8 km east from a court, then 6 km north, then 8 km west, then 10 km south. How far and in which direction from the start?

**Work.**  
East 8 and west 8 cancel. Net north 6, south 10 → **4 km south**.

## Q8. Statement–assumption (CSAT style)

Statement: “The Company should ban private trade by collectors.”  
Assumptions:  
I. Collectors currently have the opportunity to trade privately.  
II. A ban will automatically end famines.

**Work.**  
I is necessary — otherwise the ban is idle.  
II is a wild leap. Revenue ethics and famine relief are linked in history, not in this statement’s logic. **Only I.**

# BLOCK G — Active recall with model cores (25 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in one or two sentences. Then check the core.

**1. What was dual government?**  
Core: Company held Diwani (revenue and civil finance) of Bengal-Bihar-Orissa from 1765; the Nawab kept Nizamat (police and criminal justice) until Hastings ended the split in practice in 1772.

**2. Name two operative parts of the Regulating Act 1773.**  
Core: Governor-General of Bengal plus a four-member Council; Supreme Court at Calcutta; Madras and Bombay placed under Bengal on war and peace in theory.

**3. What did Pitt’s India Act 1784 add at the London end?**  
Core: A Board of Control of Crown commissioners to supervise civil, military and revenue affairs, leaving Directors with commerce and patronage — dual control in Britain.

**4. What was “permanent” in the Permanent Settlement?**  
Core: The government demand on the zamindar, and the zamindar’s proprietory title so long as that demand was paid; peasant rents were not equally iron-clad.

**5. Contrast ryotwari and mahalwari in one line each.**  
Core: Ryotwari — state settles with the individual cultivator and revises in cycles (Madras-Bombay type). Mahalwari — state settles with a village/mahal body or co-sharers (North-Western Provinces type).

**6. Which Mysore treaty did not redraw the map in the Company’s favour?**  
Core: Treaty of Mangalore, 1784, Second Anglo-Mysore War — mutual restoration.

**7. Why is the Treaty of Bassein, 1802, a hinge?**  
Core: The Peshwa accepted a subsidiary force, which Scindia and Bhonsle treated as a betrayal of the confederacy and which opened the Second Anglo-Maratha War.

**8. List the five usual subsidiary-alliance clauses.**  
Core: Paid British contingent; no other European officers without leave; Company-guided foreign relations; a Resident; protection promised by the Company.

**9. Warren Hastings versus Lord Hastings.**  
Core: Warren Hastings — first Governor-General of Bengal, 1770s–85. Lord Hastings (Moira) — Governor-General who finished the Third Maratha War, 1817–19.

**10. Why did subsidiary alliance eat territory without always annexing it on day one?**  
Core: Princes assigned districts to pay the contingent; assigned land passed into Company management.
