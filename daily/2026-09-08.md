# BLOCK A — Three Charter Acts: monopoly dies, India is named, the Company is hollowed (50 minutes)

Parliament renewed the Company’s charter about every twenty years. After 1773 and 1784 (yesterday), three later statutes change the *character* of the Raj. Learn them as a chain, not as three isolated dates.

## Charter Act of 1813 — the commercial lock breaks

**What changed.**

- The Company’s **monopoly of trade with India ended**, except the China tea trade and the trade in tea generally, which stayed reserved a little longer.
- British merchants and missionaries could now legally enter Company territories under licence.
- One lakh rupees a year was to be set aside for **education and the revival of literature** among the Company’s Indian subjects — the first statutory education grant. How to spend it (Sanskrit colleges versus English classes) became the later Macaulay fight; park the fight, keep the grant.
- The Crown’s sovereignty over the Company’s territorial acquisitions was stated more plainly. The Company still ruled, but it ruled **under** Parliament.

**Why 1813, not earlier.** The Napoleonic wars were ending. British manufacturers wanted Indian markets. Evangelicals wanted legal room to preach. The old argument — only a closed corporation can keep India quiet — no longer carried the Commons.

**Teaching sentence:** 1813 turned India from a Company shop-floor into an **open British market with a missionary door**, while leaving the Company as the tax-and-army contractor.

## Charter Act of 1833 — the all-India title and the law factory

This is the statute beginners under-learn. It is the legal birth of **British India as one government**.

Carry these operative points:

- The Governor-General of Bengal became the **Governor-General of India**. William Bentinck was the first to hold the all-India title in this statutory sense.
- The Governor-General-in-Council received power to make laws and regulations for **all territories** of the Company. Madras and Bombay lost independent law-making in the old presidency style.
- A **fourth ordinary member** was added to the Council for legislative work. T. B. Macaulay was the famous first Law Member. This is the seed of a central legislature, still tiny and official.
- The Company was required to **close its commercial business**. It became a purely administrative trustee of territory. Trade accounts and political accounts were no longer supposed to sit in one till.
- A pious clause said that no Indian subject should be debarred from office **by religion, place of birth, descent or colour**. Practice ignored the spirit for decades; the clause still matters because later nationalists quoted it as a broken promise.

**Prelims trap:** 1833 creates the Governor-General *of India*. 1773 created the Governor-General *of Bengal*. Do not swap the titles.

**Teaching sentence:** 1833 nationalised the Company’s remaining shop, named one Governor-General for the whole map, and opened a central law-making desk.

## Charter Act of 1853 — the last renewal, and open civil-service competition

- The charter was **not** renewed for a fixed twenty years in the old comfortable way. The Company’s political life was now on parliamentary sufferance.
- The Governor-General’s Council was enlarged for legislative business: more members, including representation from the presidencies in the legislative sittings. Law-making began to look like a small official parliament, still with no Indian elected member.
- **Open competitive examination** was accepted as the method for the covenanted civil service, replacing pure Directors’ patronage. The first examinations followed in London. Indians could sit in theory; cost, language and location kept the service almost entirely British until much later.
- The Law Member became a full ordinary member of the executive council as well as the legislative side — a small but clean administrative fact.

**Teaching sentence:** 1853 admitted that the Company might not get another comfortable twenty-year political lease, and that collectors should be chosen by exam, not only by a letter from Leadenhall Street.

## One chain for revision

| Year | Political fact | Commercial fact |
|---|---|---|
| 1773 | GG of Bengal + Supreme Court | Company still a trader-ruler |
| 1784 | Board of Control in London | Dual control: Crown politics, Directors trade/jobs |
| 1813 | Education grant; missionaries licensed | India trade monopoly ends |
| 1833 | GG of India; central laws; Law Member | Company trade ends |
| 1853 | Legislative council grows; ICS exam | Charter no longer a cozy 20-year political gift |

1858 (Crown takeover after the revolt) is **tomorrow’s neighbourhood**, not today’s statute. Mention it only as the cliff the 1853 Act already hinted at.

# BLOCK B — Bentinck: war-bill politics becomes social-legal engineering (40 minutes)

William Bentinck (Governor-General 1828–1835) is not a saint in a textbook painting. He was sent to **cut costs** after expensive wars. Social measures rode on that fiscal brief and on a new evangelical-utilitarian mood in London.

## Why the treasury forced reform

The First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–26) had been ruinously costly. The Company needed a quieter map and a cheaper civil list. Bentinck reduced allowances, pruned some military expenses, and looked for political peace with Indian states where he could. Reform and retrenchment arrived together. If you write only “humanitarian Governor-General” you miss the budget.

## Sati Regulation, 1829

**What the law did.** In the Bengal Presidency, the burning or burying alive of a Hindu widow was declared a criminal offence. Abetment was punishable. Later extension covered other presidencies.

**What the law did not magically do.** It did not instantly empty every funeral pyre in every district. Enforcement depended on collectors, Indian deputies, and local opinion. Raja Rammohan Roy’s campaign against sati gave Bentinck an Indian public argument, which mattered in a government that feared a religious explosion.

**Teaching sentence:** 1829 made widow-burning a crime of the colonial state, using both utilitarian criminal law and an Indian reform voice; it did not, by itself, rewrite household power.

## Thugi and dacoity operations

Organised robber bands that used deception and strangling on roads — labelled **thugi** in Company records — were hunted through a special department associated with William Sleeman. The campaign mixed police work, informers, and a moral story about “civilising the roads.” For Prelims: Bentinck-era suppression of thugi is the peg. For Mains: road safety was also **trade safety**; a safer Grand Trunk belt meant safer revenue and troop movement.

## Other Bentinck pegs you actually need

- **Abolition of the provincial courts of appeal and circuit** in their old form and a cheaper, more unified judicial stack — detail only if a question names the courts.
- **English** as the language of higher administration and of the higher courts took a decisive push in this generation (Macaulay’s 1835 Minute sits on Bentinck’s desk). Persian lost official primacy in the Company’s law world.
- **Annexation of Coorg (1834)** and pressure on some small states show that Bentinck was not only a social reformer. Fiscal and strategic annexation continued.
- **Treaty calm with Ranjit Singh** on the Satluj line (the 1809 Treaty of Amritsar still framed the frontier). Bentinck met Ranjit Singh at Ropar in 1831 — a diplomatic pageant, not a conquest.

**Mains line:** Bentinck is the moment when the Company, already a sovereign, started using **statute and police** to rewrite selected Indian social crimes, while still governing as a foreign army on Indian taxes.

# BLOCK C — Punjab: Ranjit Singh’s state and the two Anglo-Sikh wars (45 minutes)

## Why Punjab is a separate story

The Sikh misls of the late eighteenth century were cavalry republics in a shattered Mughal-Afghan frontier. **Ranjit Singh** (Maharaja from 1801, died 1839) welded them into a kingdom with its capital at **Lahore**, a second heart at **Amritsar**, and a frontier that at its height looked at Kashmir, Multan, Peshawar and the hills.

**What made the state strong.**

- A regular army that hired European officers (French, Italian and others) to drill infantry and artillery, while Sikh cavalry remained the political core.
- Revenue from the canal-irrigated doabs of the Punjab plains.
- A court that was Sikh-led but employed Muslims, Hindus and Europeans — a military monarchy, not a closed sect-state.
- A clear understanding, after 1809, that the **Satluj** was the red line with the Company. Ranjit Singh expanded west and north, not south into the Company’s protected Cis-Satluj Sikh chiefs.

**Teaching sentence:** Ranjit Singh built the only north-western Indian power that the Company treated as a peer while he lived; the peerage was personal and died with the machine that held the khalsa army together.

## Collapse after 1839

Succession murders, army panchayats that named their price, and court factions (Dogras at court, Sandhanwalia intrigues — names only if a question gives them) turned the army into a kingmaker. The Company watched from Ferozepur and Ludhiana. A powerful, unpaid, self-governing army across the Satluj was, in Calcutta’s eyes, a loaded gun.

## First Anglo-Sikh War, 1845–46

**Spark.** Crossings of the Satluj and a breakdown of the old 1809 restraint, after months of mutual suspicion. Do not waste lines on every battlefield rumour.

**Battles to recognise.** Mudki, Ferozeshah, Aliwal, **Sobraon** (February 1846) on the Satluj. British-Indian forces under Hugh Gough took heavy casualties; Sikh artillery was excellent; political betrayal inside the Lahore court is a standard reading of why the khalsa lost a war it could have made far costlier.

**Treaty of Lahore, 1846.**

- Heavy indemnity.
- Cession of the Jalandhar Doab (between Satluj and Beas).
- Kashmir sold by the Company to Gulab Singh of Jammu (Treaty of Amritsar, 1846) — the origin of Dogra rule in the Kashmir valley and a long later constitutional shadow. Learn the mechanism: **war indemnity → territory monetised → a new princely client.**
- A British Resident at Lahore; the Sikh army reduced.

A follow-on **Treaty of Bhairowal (1846)** tightened the Resident’s grip and a regency for the child Maharaja Duleep Singh.

## Second Anglo-Sikh War, 1848–49

**Spark.** The Multan revolt of Diwan Mulraj, then a wider rising that pulled in parts of the khalsa. The Company decided that a supervised Sikh kingdom was no longer cheaper than annexation.

**Battles to recognise.** Chillianwala (January 1849) — a bloody shock for British arms; **Gujrat** (February 1849) — the decisive defeat of the Sikh field army.

**Outcome, March 1849.** Punjab annexed by Lord Dalhousie. Duleep Singh pensioned. The Koh-i-Noor passed to the British Crown as part of the settlement story. A Board of Administration (the Lawrence brothers in the famous pairing) began to settle land revenue, disband dangerous units, and police the Afghan edge.

**Teaching sentence:** 1846 clipped Punjab and sold Kashmir; 1849 abolished the Lahore kingdom and gave the Company the Indus door.

# BLOCK D — Dalhousie: rails, wire, postage, and the Doctrine of Lapse (45 minutes)

James Andrew Broun-Ramsay, Earl of Dalhousie, Governor-General 1848–1856, is the last great Company moderniser and the man whose map most directly feeds the 1857 explosion. Hold both truths in one paragraph.

## The infrastructure bundle — learn as a set

1. **Railways.** The first passenger line, Bombay to Thane, opened in **1853**. A general plan: trunk lines from the ports inland so that troops and grain could move on a timetable. Gauge and guarantee debates are later economy lessons; today you need **1853 + port-to-interior logic**.
2. **Telegraph.** A line linking Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Peshawar was pushed in the early 1850s. In 1857 the wire would matter as much as the rifle. For now: **information became a government weapon at the speed of current, not of a horse.**
3. **Postage.** Uniform cheap postage (the 1854 reforms associated with this generation) tied the subcontinent into one mail market.
4. **Public Works Department** habit and the Ganga canal completion in the north-west (the canal’s origins are older; Dalhousie’s years see the big working system). Irrigation was revenue as well as famine insurance.
5. **Wood’s Despatch, 1854.** A structured education policy: departments of public instruction, universities at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (charters 1857 — park the revolt year, keep the university idea), and a filter from vernacular schools up to English colleges. This is the skeleton of the later colonial classroom state.

**Teaching sentence:** Dalhousie laid the **physical and postal skeleton** of a single Indian state while still ruling in the Company’s name.

## Doctrine of Lapse — the rule, not the slogan

**The rule Dalhousie applied.** If a subordinate princely ruler died without a **natural heir**, the Company would not automatically recognise an adopted son. The state could “lapse” to the paramount power. Adoption was a recognised Hindu political practice; refusal to recognise it was a **political choice dressed as a legal doctrine.**

**States classically listed as lapsed or absorbed in this logic (memorise the set):** Satara (1848), Jaitpur and Sambalpur, Baghat, Udaipur (the small one, not Mewar — name-trap), Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854). Berar was assigned by the Nizam under financial pressure (1853) — not a pure lapse, but the same decade’s tightening.

**Awadh, 1856.** Not a lapse. Annexed on the charge of **misgovernment**, after a long subsidiary relationship. The Nawab’s court at Lucknow was removed. Talukdars lost confidence in their titles. This is the single annexation most tightly wired to the 1857 rising in the Ganga-Yamuna belt. Keep Awadh **out** of the lapse list when you write; examiners treat the mix-up as a beginner error.

**Title and pension politics.** The refusal to continue certain Mughal and Peshwa family privileges, and the later insult to Nana Sahib’s pension claim, belong to the same climate of **paramountcy without courtesy**. Detail tomorrow; climate today.

**Teaching sentence:** lapse attacked adoption; Awadh attacked a living dynasty on a moral file; together they told princes and pensioners that Company protection was a one-way door.

## Second Anglo-Burmese War, 1852

Dalhousie annexed **Pegu (Lower Burma)** after a short war justified as a commercial-diplomatic quarrel at Rangoon. The rice frontier and the Bay of Bengal sea-lane mattered. Upper Burma remained independent until a later war (1885 — park it). For today: the Company was now an **Indian Ocean territorial power**, not only a Gangetic one.

# BLOCK E — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (40 minutes)

## Geography you must be able to point at

**Punjab five doabs.** A doab is the tongue between two rivers. From east to west in the Punjab plains: Jalandhar (Bari is further west — learn a clean order) — practical exam order:

- Between Satluj and Beas: Jalandhar (or Bist) Doab — ceded in 1846.
- Between Beas and Ravi: Bari Doab (Amritsar-Lahore heart).
- Between Ravi and Chenab: Rechna Doab.
- Between Chenab and Jhelum: Chaj Doab.
- Between Jhelum and Indus: Sindh Sagar Doab.

**Passes and gates.** The Satluj crossing at the Ludhiana-Ferozepur belt is the Company’s military door. Beyond the Indus, the road to Peshawar and the Khyber is the Afghan door Ranjit Singh had already pushed into. Kashmir is the high basin reached from Jammu; selling it in 1846 put a Dogra prince on a Himalayan saddle that still structures North Indian geopolitics.

**Why rails followed ports.** Bombay–Thane sits on a western-coast island-harbour system that needed an inland cotton and troop line into the Deccan. Calcutta needed the Ganga plain. Madras needed the Carnatic hinterland. Railway geography is **harbour + garrison + cash crop**, not a tourist map.

## Economy: from tribute to a modern fiscal-military state

Three clean points.

1. **End of Company trade (1833)** meant the Raj lived on **land revenue, opium, salt, and customs**, not on a corporate shop. Opium to China remained a pillar of the Indian budget into the later nineteenth century — a moral-economic fact, not a side colour.
2. **Punjab settlement** after 1849 favoured a reliable cultivating peasantry and recorded rights more in a mahalwari-ryotwari mixed style than a Bengal zamindari copy. The British wanted cavalry-recruiting peasants who paid on time. That social bet later built the “martial” recruiting map.
3. **Railway guarantees** (private British companies promised a minimum return, paid from Indian revenues if traffic was thin) socialised risk onto Indian taxpayers and privatised profit when lines paid. Remember the principle for later economy answers.

## Ethics — four tensions a GS-IV mind should name

1. **Paramountcy versus promise.** Subsidiary treaties said “we protect your dynasty.” Lapse said “not if you adopt.” A public servant today still faces the temptation to reread a rule when the treasury wants the asset.
2. **Civilising crime versus cultural consent.** Sati abolition used criminal law on a practice some communities defended as sacred. The ethical test is harm to a person who cannot consent (the widow under family and religious pressure) versus the colonial habit of deciding which Indian customs count as crime. Hold both: the widow’s life, and the legitimacy problem of an alien legislature.
3. **Infrastructure as power.** A telegraph that summons troops is not a neutral public good. Development and domination can share a wire.
4. **Sale of Kashmir.** Treating a valley and its people as payment toward a war bill is the sharpest annexation-ethics case in this lesson.

## Current affairs explained through today’s static

You do not need this week’s newspaper to use the lesson. When any modern government debates **railway expansion in border plains**, **university language policy**, **adoption and succession in customary law**, or **Indus-water politics with Pakistan**, the 1846–54 layer is the base map. The Indus and its doabs became a colonial hydrological unit after 1849; later the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) partitioned that unit. When a question mentions Satluj-Beas link canals or Punjab grain surplus, start from the doab, not from a slogan.

Wood’s Despatch logic — a state that wants clerks, engineers and loyal graduates — is the ancestor of every later fight over what a public university is for. Write that ancestor in one clause; do not drag the whole NEP into this file.

# BLOCK F — CSAT with worked solutions (40 minutes)

All questions are original practice in the CSAT spirit. Work on paper first.

## Q1. Profit and loss

A Company contractor buys 120 maunds of grain at Rs 80 a maund. He sells 40 percent of the stock at 25 percent profit. One-eighth of the original stock spoils and is written off. He sells the remaining grain at 10 percent loss. What is his overall profit or loss percent on the original outlay? (Nearest one decimal if needed.)

**Worked solution.**

Cost of 120 maunds $= 120 \times 80 = 9600$.

Sold at 25 percent profit: $0.4 \times 120 = 48$ maunds. Selling price $= 48 \times 80 \times 1.25 = 4800$.

Spoiled: $120/8 = 15$ maunds. Revenue $= 0$.

Remaining: $120 - 48 - 15 = 57$ maunds. Selling price $= 57 \times 80 \times 0.90 = 4104$.

Total revenue $= 4800 + 4104 = 8904$.

Net result $= 8904 - 9600 = -696$.

Loss percent $= 696/9600 \times 100 = 7.25\%$.

**Answer: 7.25 percent loss.**

## Q2. Clocks

At what time between 4 and 5 o’clock are the hour and minute hands of a clock at right angles for the first time?

**Worked solution.**

Minute hand speed $= 6^\circ$ per minute. Hour hand speed $= 0.5^\circ$ per minute.

At 4:00 the hour hand is at $4 \times 30 = 120^\circ$.

Right angle means difference $90^\circ$.

Let $m$ minutes after 4:00.

Position minute hand $= 6m$.

Position hour hand $= 120 + 0.5m$.

$|6m - (120 + 0.5m)| = 90$.

Case A: $6m - 120 - 0.5m = 90 \Rightarrow 5.5m = 210 \Rightarrow m = 210/5.5 = 420/11 = 38\ \frac{2}{11}$ minutes. That is the *later* right angle.

Case B: $120 + 0.5m - 6m = 90 \Rightarrow 120 - 90 = 5.5m \Rightarrow 30 = 5.5m \Rightarrow m = 30/5.5 = 60/11 = 5\ \frac{5}{11}$ minutes.

First time between 4 and 5 is $5\ \frac{5}{11}$ minutes past 4.

**Answer: 4 : $5\ \frac{5}{11}$.**

## Q3. Ratio and partnership

Three collectors A, B and C farm a district revenue contract. A puts in Rs 4 lakh for 12 months, B puts in Rs 6 lakh for 8 months, C puts in Rs 8 lakh for 6 months. Profit is Rs 1.9 lakh. C takes 10 percent of profit as managing fee before sharing. How much does B receive?

**Worked solution.**

Capital-month units:

A: $4 \times 12 = 48$.

B: $6 \times 8 = 48$.

C: $8 \times 6 = 48$.

Ratio $1:1:1$ of the remainder.

Managing fee to C: $0.10 \times 1.9 = 0.19$ lakh.

Remainder $= 1.71$ lakh. Each partner’s share of remainder $= 0.57$ lakh.

B receives only that share $= $ Rs 57,000.

(C would get $0.57 + 0.19 = 0.76$ lakh; not asked.)

**Answer: Rs 57,000.**

## Q4. Number series

Find the wrong number: 5, 27, 61, 107, 165, 233, 317.

**Worked solution.**

Differences: $22, 34, 46, 58, 68, 84$.

Expected differences if adding consecutive even increments of $+12$: $22, 34, 46, 58, 70, 82$.

The fifth difference is 68 instead of 70, so the term after 165 is the break. Check from 107: $107+58=165$ is fine; $165+70=235$, not 233. Then $235+82=317$. So **233 is wrong**; it should be 235.

**Answer: 233.**

## Q5. Statement — assumption

Statement: “The Governor-General should refuse to recognise adopted heirs in dependent states so that British districts can be consolidated.”

Which assumption is implicit?

(1) Dependent states exist only by the will of the paramount power.

(2) Adopted heirs are always incompetent administrators.

(3) Consolidation of districts always reduces the chance of revolt.

**Worked solution.**

The policy argument treats recognition of heirs as a gift the Company may withhold in order to take land. That is coherent only if (1) is granted: the dependent state’s continuity is not a right against the Company.

(2) is not required; lapse as Dalhousie used it did not rest on a competence test of the child.

(3) is a hoped-for consequence some officials believed; the statement does not need it to make grammatical sense, and 1857 later falsified the hope. CSAT implicit-assumption items want the **necessary** belief, not every possible hope.

**Answer: only (1).**

## Q6. Time and work

A telegraph line can be laid by 24 workers in 18 days working 8 hours a day. After 6 days of such work, 8 workers leave and the day is cut to 6 hours. How many more days are needed to finish?

**Worked solution.**

Total worker-hours $= 24 \times 18 \times 8 = 3456$.

Work done in 6 days $= 24 \times 6 \times 8 = 1152$.

Remaining $= 2304$ worker-hours.

Workers left $= 16$. Hours a day $= 6$. Worker-hours a day now $= 96$.

Days more $= 2304 / 96 = 24$.

**Answer: 24 days.**

# BLOCK G — Active recall and model cores (25 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in speech. Then check the core, not the whole essay.

**R1.** What is the single commercial difference between the Charter Acts of 1813 and 1833?

*Core:* 1813 ends the Company monopoly of the India trade (China tea reserved); 1833 ends the Company’s commercial business altogether and leaves it a political trustee.

**R2.** Who is the first Governor-General *of India* in the 1833 statutory sense?

*Core:* William Bentinck. (Warren Hastings was first GG of Bengal under 1773.)

**R3.** Name three operative parts of the 1853 Charter Act.

*Core:* Charter not comfortably renewed for 20 years; legislative council enlarged; open competition for the covenanted civil service.

**R4.** What did the 1829 sati law criminalise, and what Indian public voice is classically tied to it?

*Core:* Burning or burying a Hindu widow alive, plus abetment, first in Bengal Presidency; Rammohan Roy’s campaign.

**R5.** Why did the Company not fight Ranjit Singh while he lived?

*Core:* The 1809 Satluj line held; he expanded west-north; his army was a real peer; Bentinck preferred a stable frontier after expensive wars.

**R6.** Separate 1846 and 1849 in one sentence each.

*Core:* 1846 Treaty of Lahore clipped Punjab, took the Jalandhar Doab, and sold Kashmir to Gulab Singh. 1849 annexed the Lahore kingdom after Gujrat.

**R7.** Which famous Dalhousie annexation is **not** Doctrine of Lapse?

*Core:* Awadh, 1856, on alleged misgovernment.

**R8.** List six lapse or lapse-logic names from the Dalhousie years.

*Core:* Satara, Jaitpur, Sambalpur, Baghat, Jhansi, Nagpur (Udaipur small state sometimes listed; do not say Mewar).

**R9.** Why do Bombay–Thane 1853 and the telegraph belong in a political history answer, not only a science answer?

*Core:* They cut the time for troops, grain and orders to move; they are instruments of a single fiscal-military state.

**R10.** Ethical core of lapse in one line.

*Core:* The paramount power treated dynastic adoption as a privilege it could cancel when it wanted the land.
