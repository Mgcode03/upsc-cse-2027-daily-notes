# BLOCK A — Causes: one cartridge, many files (55 minutes)

Do not write “the greased cartridge caused 1857.” Write a **stack**. The cartridge is the spark on a magazine that was already packed.

## 1. Military file — the Bengal Army was a political community

The Company’s three presidency armies were not identical.

- **Bengal Army** recruited heavily from high-caste peasants of Awadh, the eastern United Provinces belt, and Bihar — Brahmins, Rajputs, Bhumihars — men whose village honour travelled with them into the line.
- Service rules had been tightening: General Service Enlistment pressure (the 1856 Act for new Bengal recruits) meant a man might be shipped across the sea. Crossing the *kala pani* was, for many, a caste injury, not a travel inconvenience.
- Batta (field allowance) had been cut after earlier wars. Postings in newly annexed Punjab and Burma felt like extra labour for less honour.
- The ratio of Indian sepoys to British soldiers had grown dangerously thin after Crimean and other drains. A handful of white officers sat on a huge Indian rank and file.
- Promotion for Indians stopped early. A subedar could be older than his British ensign and still salute him.

**Teaching sentence:** the Bengal sepoy was a taxpayer’s son with a contract; when the contract looked like a caste insult, the regiment became a political crowd.

## 2. Political file — annexation without courtesy

Carry yesterday forward as *cause*, not as decoration.

- **Doctrine of Lapse** had taken Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur and smaller states. Princes who had played by subsidiary rules discovered that adoption was no longer a safe political tool.
- **Awadh, February 1856**, annexed for “misgovernment.” This is the single heaviest political charge in the Gangetic rising. Awadh was the recruiting heart of the Bengal Army. Sepoys heard that their home talukas were being recast, that the Nawab’s court at Lucknow was gone, that old land titles were under survey.
- Nana Sahib at Kanpur, adopted son of the last Peshwa Baji Rao II, was denied the pension the Peshwa had enjoyed. A pension quarrel became a legitimacy quarrel.
- The Mughal emperor at Delhi was a pensioner in the Red Fort. Dalhousie’s generation treated the Fort as a courtesy lodging, not a court. Bahadur Shah Zafar still sat at the symbolic centre of north-Indian Muslim and old Mughal memory.

**Teaching sentence:** 1857 was not only soldiers angry at grease; it was a coalition of **disinherited courts and disinherited villages** using those soldiers.

## 3. Economic and agrarian file

- Land revenue in the North-Western Provinces and Awadh had been pushed toward a recorded, collectable demand. In Awadh, talukdars who had been intermediaries lost confidence that their estates would survive a new settlement.
- Artisans in old court towns (Lucknow, Delhi, some qasba markets) lost court demand when annexation shut a nawabi payroll.
- Peasants paid in a monetising countryside while prices and rents moved against them in bad seasons. You need the *direction* of grievance, not a fake all-India statistic.

## 4. Social-religious file

- Sati ban, widow-remarriage law (1856, associated with Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar’s campaign and the legislative generation after Bentinck), and rumours about conversion sat in the same rumour-market as the cartridge.
- Missionary presence after 1813 was legal. That fact was twisted, in barracks talk, into “the Company will make us Christian after it makes us bite cow and pig.”
- The **Enfield rifle cartridge** (1856–57 issue): to load, the end of the paper cartridge had to be bitten. Grease was widely believed to be cow and pig fat. Whether every batch was so greased is less important than this: **the army gave its men a ritual reason to refuse an order.** Refusal then became mutiny; mutiny, once Delhi was seized, became a war of succession over north India.

**Teaching sentence:** religion here is not a separate “fanatic” box; it is the language in which a broken service contract and a broken princely map were *spoken*.

## 5. Immediate chain — Meerut to Delhi

- **29 March 1857**, Barrackpore: Mangal Pandey of the 34th Native Infantry wounds officers; he is hanged; the regiment is disbanded.
- **May 1857**, Meerut: sepoys of the 3rd Light Cavalry refuse cartridges; imprisoned; on **10 May** comrades break the jail, kill officers, and ride the night road to Delhi.
- **11–12 May**, Delhi: the magazine and the city fall; Bahadur Shah is proclaimed — reluctantly, then inescapably — a symbol of a restored Hindustan.
- After Delhi, the rising is a **set of regional wars**, not one general staff.

# BLOCK B — Centres, leaders, and the shape of the war (50 minutes)

Learn **six cores** and what each core actually was. Do not memorise every skirmish.

## Delhi — the symbol

Bahadur Shah Zafar is old, poor, and not a field commander. His court becomes the address on rebel proclamations. Real fighting around Delhi is done by sepoys, city elements, and incoming contingents. The British siege army gathers on the Ridge. **Delhi is retaken in September 1857** after bitter street fighting. The princes are executed; the Emperor is tried and exiled to Rangoon (1858). For Prelims: Delhi + Zafar + September 1857 recapture.

## Kanpur — Nana Sahib and Tantia Tope

Nana Sahib (Dhondu Pant) uses the pension grievance and the Peshwa name. Tantia Tope is the able field organiser. The garrison at the entrenchment, the massacre narrative that British memory turned into a crusade, and the later recapture by Havelock and others — learn the political fact: **Kanpur is where a Maratha pensioner tries to reopen a Peshwa claim on a Ganga town.** Tantia Tope continues guerrilla war into 1858–59 and is captured and hanged in 1859.

## Lucknow and Awadh — Begum Hazrat Mahal

This is the widest *popular* theatre. Begum Hazrat Mahal, wife of the deposed Nawab Wajid Ali Shah (who himself was in Calcutta exile), stands for the Awadh court. Talukdars and peasants mix with sepoys. The Residency siege and the two reliefs are British military story; your GS story is: **Awadh fights because annexation hit both the court and the recruiting villages.**

## Jhansi and the Bundelkhand–Gwalior corridor — Rani Lakshmibai

Lakshmibai resists because lapse refused her adopted son. She defends Jhansi (1858), then moves toward Kalpi and Gwalior. Death in the field near Gwalior (June 1858) is the exam peg. Pair her with Tantia Tope for the western-central campaign. Teaching point: she is not a “token woman warrior”; she is a **dynastic claimant using the only army she can raise.**

## Bareilly and Rohilkhand — Khan Bahadur Khan

A Rohilla-Afghan notable leads a regional government. This reminds you that 1857 in the west-UP belt had a strong Muslim-ashraf and Afghan-memory strand, not only a high-caste Hindu sepoy strand.

## Bihar — Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur

An ageing talukdar of Shahabad fights a mobile campaign down the Ganga–Son country. He shows that the revolt had a **Bihar landlord-sepoy** wing, not only a Delhi-Lucknow wing. He dies in 1858 of wounds after a last action near his estate.

## What did *not* rise in the same way

Say this cleanly in Mains or you over-claim.

- Punjab’s newly conquered Sikh and Muslim peasantry, and the Sikh chiefs, largely sided with the Company or stayed out — the 1849 settlement and hostility to a restored Delhi-Mughal frame mattered.
- Bombay and Madras armies did not explode like Bengal.
- Hyderabad, most of Rajputana, Travancore, Nepal’s court, and the Punjab irregulars supplied the Raj with men, money, or roads.
- Bengal proper (the old Permanent Settlement districts east of the main sepoy belt) and the new western presidency cities did not become rebel capitals.
- South India was not a blank of loyalty; it was a different military and land-revenue world that did not share the Bengal Army’s grievance stack.

**Teaching sentence:** 1857 was a **north-Indian and central-Indian war of dispossessed elites plus Bengal sepoys**, not a coordinated national liberation army.

# BLOCK C — Why the revolt failed, and what 1858 built instead (45 minutes)

## Failure — six structural reasons

1. **No single command.** Delhi, Lucknow, Kanpur, Jhansi, Bareilly ran on local legitimacy. Tantia Tope could ride between camps; he could not issue an all-India order.
2. **No modern supply or treasury.** Rebels captured towns; they did not capture a reliable mint-and-magazine system equal to Calcutta, Bombay, and the sea.
3. **The sea and the wire stayed with the Company.** Steam troopships from Britain, Madras, and the Cape, plus the telegraph Dalhousie had laid, moved British decision faster than rebel cavalry.
4. **Indian allies of the Company.** Patiala, Jind, Gwalior’s Sindhia (after a shock at Gwalior fort), Hyderabad’s minister, Nepal’s aid, Sikh units — the Raj was already a **coalition empire**. The revolt never broke that coalition.
5. **Limited social programme.** Proclamations restored emperors, nawabs, and peshwa-memory. They did not offer a new land deal that could peel the entire peasantry away from fear of chaos. Some peasants fought; many paid whoever held the tahsil that week.
6. **British reconquest was systematic.** After Delhi (Sept 1857) and the relief of Lucknow, columns treated Awadh and the Doab as a pacification map: recapture, punish, resettle, reward loyal talukdars. By mid-1858 the field war was dying; mopping up lasted into 1859.

**Teaching sentence:** 1857 failed as a state-making project because it was **many restorations at once**, while the Company still owned the ocean, the wire, and half of India’s princes.

## Government of India Act, 1858 — the legal death of the Company

Parliament ended the fiction that a trading corporation ruled a subcontinent.

**Operative points you must own:**

- The territories and revenues of the Company transferred to the **Crown**.
- The Court of Directors and the Board of Control disappeared as the dual machine of 1784.
- A **Secretary of State for India**, sitting in the British Cabinet, received the old Board’s political authority. He was advised by a **Council of India** (fifteen members in the original design; later tweaked — do not over-drill the number unless a question states it).
- The Governor-General received the additional title **Viceroy** when acting as the Crown’s representative. Canning, already Governor-General through the revolt, became the first Viceroy in this new statutory sense.
- The Indian Civil Service continued, now openly a Crown service. Military forces of the Company were transferred; the Bengal Army was rebuilt on a different recruiting theory (more Punjab, Nepal, “martial races” as a later official language).

**Teaching sentence:** 1858 moved India from a **company-state under parliamentary leash** to a **Crown colony with a Cabinet minister and a Viceroy.**

## Queen’s Proclamation, 1 November 1858 (Allahabad reading)

Read it as a **political amnesty-plus-promise**, not as poetry.

- No further territorial ambition announced as policy — “we shall respect the rights of the native princes.”
- Adoption: the lapse logic is publicly softened; princes may hope that adopted heirs will be recognised.
- Equal treatment for subjects of every race and creed in admission to office — a repeat of the 1833 promise, still slowly honoured.
- Non-interference in religious belief as a stated rule.
- Mercy for rebels not guilty of murder, paired with a warning to those who had killed.

**Mains use:** the Proclamation is the Raj’s attempt to **buy back the princes** after a year of seeing what a princely-sepoy coalition could do. It is policy, not kindness.

## Immediate administrative aftershocks (park detail, keep direction)

- Army reorganised: fewer high-caste Awadh men in the old proportion; artillery kept more tightly under British control; mixed deployments so that no one ethnicity dominates a station the way Bengal infantry had.
- Crown’s relationship with princely states becomes the long **paramountcy** system that lasts until 1947.
- White civilian racism hardens in some stations even as official prose speaks of equality. Both facts can live in one paragraph.

# BLOCK D — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (40 minutes)

## Geography you must be able to point at

**The revolt corridor.** Draw, even on scrap paper:

- Yamuna–Ganga Doab: Meerut → Delhi → Aligarh belt → Kanpur → Allahabad.
- Awadh basin: Lucknow, Faizabad-Ayodhya country, the Ghaghara plains.
- Rohilkhand: Bareilly, the Ramganga belt.
- Bundelkhand plateau: Jhansi, Kalpi, then west to Gwalior on the Chambal–Sind fringe.
- Shahabad-Bhojpur: Kunwar Singh’s riverine Bihar, Son joining Ganga.

**Why this corridor.** This is the older Mughal-Awadh military labour market plus the Grand Trunk and river roads. The Bengal Army’s villages sat here. Punjab to the west had just been conquered and rewarded; Bengal delta to the east had a different tenure and a thinner sepoy density.

**Why the Ridge at Delhi matters.** Delhi’s northern Ridge is the last easy high ground before the walled city. A siege army that holds the Ridge holds the supply road from Punjab. Geography decided the symbol-war.

**Allahabad as the 1858 stage.** The Proclamation is read at Allahabad because the town sits at the Ganga–Yamuna confluence — old sacred geography plus the Company’s eastern fortress on the river road. Power likes a confluence.

## Economy: who paid for the war, who was paid after it

1. **War finance.** Suppressing 1857 was expensive. The bill was loaded onto Indian revenues. Later tax politics (income tax experiments, licence taxes) grow from this fiscal scar. You need the principle: **a colonial war of reconquest is paid by the colony.**
2. **Talukdari settlement in Awadh.** After the fighting, the Raj re-seated many talukdars as a conservative rural alliance. The peasant who had joined a rebel band did not design the peace; the landlord who could guarantee order often did.
3. **Recruiting economy.** Pay, pension, and land grants shifted toward Punjab, Nepal, and selected “loyal” belts. Awadh did not vanish from the army, but it lost its near-monopoly in the Bengal line. Military employment is an **economic geography** question, not only a martial-races myth question.
4. **Trade pulse.** During the fighting, Ganga traffic and Doab grain moved under escort or not at all. After 1858 the rail-and-wire skeleton Dalhousie began becomes, even more clearly, a **pacification infrastructure**.

## Ethics — four tensions a GS-IV mind should name

1. **Obedience versus conscience in uniform.** The sepoy who refuses an order because it destroys his ritual standing is making an ethics claim. The officer who treats that refusal as treason is making a command claim. A modern civil servant still meets lawful-order problems when a rule humiliates a community. The 1857 lesson is not “always mutiny”; it is **do not design a rule that a whole cadre must break in order to remain themselves.**
2. **Collective punishment.** British reconquest used village-burning, summary hanging, and racial revenge in places. Rebel sides committed mass killings of Europeans at some stations. Ethics writing should condemn **killing of non-combatants** without turning either side into a cartoon. Proportionality and distinction (combatant vs civilian) are the tools.
3. **Loyalty as a purchased virtue.** Princes who stayed “loyal” were often calculating survival. A public servant’s loyalty is to the Constitution today, not to a patron who might reward a quiet file. 1858’s reward-and-pardon machine is a case study in **incentives replacing legitimacy.**
4. **Promises after violence.** The Proclamation’s equality clause is an official moral commitment. Honouring it slowly, while running a racial civil service in practice, is the ethics of **stated value versus lived rule** — a GS-IV classic.

## Current-affairs-explained (tied to this static file)

Whenever a modern Indian debate mentions “First War of Independence” versus “sepoy mutiny,” you already have the adult answer: **it was a mutiny in origin and a regional political war in course.** Use the precise phrase the question allows; then show the stack of causes.

Whenever a contemporary farm or recruitment protest is compared loosely to 1857, refuse the lazy analogy. 1857 combined **dynastic restoration, caste-service honour, and annexation shock** inside a foreign company-state. A democratic republic with elected governments is a different legal universe. Draw the parallel only at the level of *how rumours travel in an army or a market*, not at the level of flags.

Whenever official India marks 1857 commemorations, the usable GS point is institutional: the revolt is why India was ruled **in the Crown’s name** for the next ninety years, and why princely paramountcy was rewritten as a loyalty contract.

# BLOCK E — CSAT practice with worked solutions (40 minutes)

Attempt first, then read the working. No calculator theatre: write the arithmetic.

## Q1. Simple and compound interest

A sum of ₹8,000 is lent at 10% per annum. Find the difference between the compound interest and the simple interest on this sum for 2 years.

**Working.**
Simple interest for 2 years: \( SI = \frac{8000 \times 10 \times 2}{100} = 1600 \).
Compound interest for 2 years at 10%:
Amount \( = 8000 \times (1.10)^2 = 8000 \times 1.21 = 9680 \).
\( CI = 9680 - 8000 = 1680 \).
Difference \( CI - SI = 1680 - 1600 = 80 \).
Shortcut for 2 years: difference \( = P \left(\frac{r}{100}\right)^2 = 8000 \times 0.01 = 80 \).

**Answer: ₹80.**

## Q2. Compound interest, three years, one rate

On the same ₹8,000 at 10% per annum compounded annually, what is the compound interest for 3 years?

**Working.**
Amount \( = 8000 \times (1.10)^3 = 8000 \times 1.331 = 10648 \).
\( CI = 10648 - 8000 = 2648 \).

**Answer: ₹2,648.**

## Q3. Direction sense

A sepoy leaves a magazine and walks 4 km north, then 3 km east, then 4 km south, then 5 km west. How far is he from the magazine, and in which direction?

**Working.**
North 4 and south 4 cancel.
East 3, west 5 → net 2 km west.
He is 2 km west of the start.

**Answer: 2 km west.**

## Q4. Statement and assumption

**Statement:** “The government should restore confiscated estates only to landlords who can keep the district quiet.”
**Assumptions:**
I. Some landlords are able to keep a district quiet.
II. Restoring estates is an effective tool for buying rural order.
III. All peasants prefer their old landlords.

Which assumptions are implicit?

**Working.**
The policy recommendation uses two working beliefs: that a subset of landlords can deliver quiet (I), and that estate restoration is a lever for order (II). III is not required; the speaker may be indifferent to peasant preference.
**Answer: I and II only.**

## Q5. Percentage and restoration

A district had 250 talukas. After the revolt, 40% were confiscated. Of the confiscated talukas, 70% were later restored to “loyal” claimants. How many talukas remained confiscated?

**Working.**
Confiscated \( = 0.40 \times 250 = 100 \).
Restored \( = 0.70 \times 100 = 70 \).
Still confiscated \( = 100 - 70 = 30 \).

**Answer: 30.**

## Q6. Time and distance

A column covers 90 km in 6 hours including a 30-minute halt. What is its moving speed in km/h?

**Working.**
Halt = 0.5 h. Moving time = 5.5 h.
Speed \( = 90 / 5.5 = 900 / 55 = 180 / 11 = 16.\overline{36} \) km/h, or \( 16 \frac{4}{11} \) km/h.

**Answer: \( \dfrac{180}{11} \) km/h.**

## Q7. Coding-decoding

In a cipher used for a field message, DELHI is written as EFMJI. How is KANPUR written?

**Working.**
Each letter shifts +1: D→E, E→F, L→M, H→I, I→J.
K→L, A→B, N→O, P→Q, U→V, R→S.
**Answer: LBOQVS.**

## Q8. Number series

2, 3, 8, 27, 112, ?

**Working.**
\( 2 \times 1 + 1 = 3 \)
\( 3 \times 2 + 2 = 8 \)
\( 8 \times 3 + 3 = 27 \)
\( 27 \times 4 + 4 = 112 \)
\( 112 \times 5 + 5 = 565 \)

**Answer: 565.**

# BLOCK F — Active recall + model cores (25 minutes)

Close the file. Answer in speech. Then match the core.

**1. Why is “greased cartridge” an incomplete cause?**
*Core:* It is the spark. The magazine was the Bengal Army’s caste-service contract, Awadh annexation, lapse and pension refusals, revenue shock, and conversion rumours.

**2. Name six revolt cores and one leader each.**
*Core:* Delhi–Bahadur Shah Zafar; Kanpur–Nana Sahib / Tantia Tope; Lucknow–Begum Hazrat Mahal; Jhansi–Lakshmibai; Bareilly–Khan Bahadur Khan; Bihar–Kunwar Singh.

**3. What did the 1858 Act actually do?**
*Core:* Company territories and revenues to the Crown; Secretary of State with a Council of India; Governor-General as Viceroy; Company dual directorate abolished.

**4. Why did Punjab not become a rebel capital?**
*Core:* Fresh 1849 conquest, Sikh and Punjabi units used against Delhi, little love for a restored Mughal frame, rewards already flowing to local chiefs.

**5. Awadh annexation versus Doctrine of Lapse — one-line difference?**
*Core:* Awadh was taken from a living dynasty for alleged misgovernment in 1856; lapse took states that lacked a recognised natural heir.

**6. Two reasons the revolt could not become an all-India state.**
*Core:* No unified command or treasury; sea, telegraph, and a large princely-Sikh-southern coalition remained with the Company.

**7. What political job did the Queen’s Proclamation do?**
*Core:* Reassure princes on territory and adoption, promise religious non-interference and office without race bar, and split rebels into pardonable versus murder-guilty.

**8. Ethics core from reconquest.**
*Core:* Even a lawful state may not treat civilians as a free-fire zone; even a just grievance does not licence massacre of non-combatants.
