# BLOCK A — Why reform became a public project (50 minutes)

## 1. The problem was not “India had no thought”

Pre-colonial India already argued about ritual, caste, widowhood, idol worship and scriptural authority. What changed in the nineteenth century was the **public stage**.

- Print (Bengali, Urdu, Marathi, English, later Gujarati, Tamil, Punjabi) made a pamphlet travel farther than a preacher.
- Presidency towns created a new audience: clerks, lawyers, teachers, merchants who met the colonial state every week and needed a language that was both Indian and “respectable” in a courtroom.
- Christian missions after the Charter of 1813 argued in print that Hindu and Muslim practice was idolatrous or backward. Reformers answered in the same print market. **Response is not the same as copy.** Some borrowed Unitarian and Protestant tools (reason, one God, scripture over custom). Some used those tools to defend a purified tradition.
- The legal state had already touched sati (1829), widow remarriage (1856), and would later touch age of consent (1891). Communities learned that if they did not write their own rule-book, a statute might.

**Teaching sentence:** nineteenth-century reform is **community self-legislation under colonial eyes**, using press, school and sabha.

## 2. Two families of method — keep this fork for every Mains paragraph

**Reform from within scripture.** Claim: the oldest or the truest text already forbids the abuse; custom is the villain. Rammohan on the Upanishads, Dayananda on a Vedic core, Sayyid Ahmad on a reasoned Islam — different books, same move.

**Reform as new institution.** Claim: text is not enough; build a school, a widow’s home, a printing press, a college. Vidyasagar’s school work, Syed’s MAO College, Ramabai’s homes, Phule’s schools for girls and lower castes.

Most serious figures did both. Prelims loves the *label*; Mains needs the *method*.

## 3. What “social” and “religious” meant in this century

- **Religious** questions: one God or many forms, idol, priest, conversion, who may read scripture, tomb and shrine.
- **Social** questions: sati, widow remarriage, child marriage, female education, caste dining and occupation, age of consent, inheritance.
- They were not separate files. A widow’s right to live was argued as a *dharma* question and as a *civil* question in the same essay.

**Teaching sentence:** if you split “social reform” from “religious reform” in an answer, you will miss why the same man ran a prayer meeting and a girls’ school.

# BLOCK B — Hindu and allied streams, taught as problems (55 minutes)

## 1. Rammohan Roy and the Brahmo line (Bengal)

**Problem he named:** sati, priestly monopoly of Sanskrit learning, polytheism as practised in popular ritual, and the absence of a public Indian voice that could speak to Company judges in their own rational idiom.

**Method:**
- Argument from Vedanta and from reason. He treated the Upanishads as a monotheist core and used that core against sati and against what he called idolatry.
- Institutions: Atmiya Sabha (1815) as an early discussion circle; Brahmo Sabha / Brahmo Samaj (1828) as a congregation without image worship.
- Print and petition: Persian and Bengali journalism, English tracts, and the campaign that prepared the ground for the 1829 sati regulation under Bentinck.

**Split you must know.** After Rammohan’s death (1833), Debendranath Tagore gave the Samaj a more devout, less Christian-facing tone (Adi Brahmo Samaj). Keshab Chandra Sen pushed social radicalism, inter-caste marriage, and a more theistic, almost missionary style; his own family marriage of a young daughter (the 1878 Cooch Behar marriage) broke his claim to consistency and split the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj away. For Prelims: Rammohan → Debendranath → Keshab → Sadharan split. For Mains: **the Brahmo story is the story of how far a monotheist elite can go before its own marriage politics explode.**

**Limit:** the Brahmo public was urban, literate, and small. It changed law and middle-class conscience more than village ritual.

## 2. Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar — law and classroom, not a samaj brand

**Problem:** the widow who could not remarry; the girl who could not enter a school; the Sanskrit college that taught grammar but not a usable ethic of compassion.

**Method:** he used *Parashara* and other texts to argue that widow remarriage was not anti-shastra; he drafted and pushed the Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act, 1856; he opened and inspected girls’ schools; he simplified Bengali prose so that argument could leave the pandit’s circle.

**Teaching sentence:** Vidyasagar is the model of a **textual conservative who produces a radical statute** — he does not invent a new god; he invents a new civil right with old citations.

## 3. Prarthana Samaj (Bombay, 1867)

Atmaram Pandurang, later energy from M.G. Ranade and R.G. Bhandarkar. Theist prayer, opposition to caste rigidity, widow remarriage, female education — Brahmo cousin in a Marathi-Gujarati commercial city. Ranade’s importance is the hinge: the same man sits in social conference and in early political association. **Western India reform is courtroom-and-college reform**, not only temple reform.

## 4. Arya Samaj — Dayananda Saraswati (founded 1875, Bombay; strong in Punjab and United Provinces)

**Problem he named:** Puranic Hinduism as decline; child marriage; idol; priestcraft; Christian and Muslim conversion in the Punjab belt; a community that could not defend itself in print.

**Method:**
- “Go back to the Vedas” as a slogan of purification. Dayananda’s *Satyarth Prakash* is a fighting book: it attacks rival Hindu sects as well as other religions.
- Shuddhi (reconversion / purification) as an organisational answer to missionary and boundary anxiety.
- Schools and later Dayanand Anglo-Vedic (DAV) institutions: Vedic pride plus English employability. That mix is why Arya work spread in Punjab’s town economy.

**Limit and tension:** Arya language could stiffen communal lines even while it attacked caste extremes and promoted widow remarriage and female education inside its own frame. Write both halves.

**Prelims pegs:** 1875; Dayananda; Vedas as sole infallible core in Samaj theology; shuddhi; DAV.

## 5. Ramakrishna and Vivekananda — experience, then organisation

Ramakrishna Paramahamsa (Dakshineswar) taught that many paths reach the same goal; his authority was mystical practice, not a legislative sabha.

Vivekananda (Narendranath Datta) translated that into:
- a **universal Vedanta** spoken at Chicago (Parliament of Religions, 1893) that gave colonised Hindus a dignified international voice;
- the **Ramakrishna Mission** (1897) as organised service — school, relief, medical work — so that sannyasa included social work;
- an argument that strength, education and service were spiritual duties, aimed especially at a youth that had watched 1857 fail as armed restoration.

**Teaching sentence:** Vivekananda turns *bhakti-advaita* into **organised patriotism without yet writing a party manifesto**.

**Limit:** the Mission’s social work is real; it does not dissolve caste in the village. Do not over-claim.

## 6. Theosophy and the southern public (needed as a link, not as a cult study)

The Theosophical Society (Blavatsky and Olcott, 1875 New York; Adyar becomes the Indian centre) and Annie Besant later connected western seekers to a dignified reading of Hindu and Buddhist tradition. Besant’s work on education and Home Rule belongs more to the next political chapter; today you only need: **Adyar is a geography of global Hindu self-respect**, sitting on the Chennai coast.

## 7. Lower-caste and non-Brahmin assertion — do not park this as a footnote

**Jyotirao Phule (and Savitribai Phule), Maharashtra.** Satyashodhak Samaj (1873). Problem: Brahmin monopoly of knowledge and ritual. Method: schools for girls and for those excluded from the sacred thread world; a reading of history that made the peasant and the ati-shudra the original injured party; writing in Marathi that a farmer could hear. Savitribai’s classroom is as important as Jyotirao’s tract.

**Teaching sentence:** Phule treats caste as a **power system**, not as a ritual error that a Vedic reboot will fix.

**Pandita Ramabai.** Sanskrit-educated, travels through conversion and widowhood debates, founds homes for widows (Mukti Mission line at Kedgaon). She forces the Hindu reform public to face a woman who would not stay inside its script.

**Other regional sparks you should be able to place:**
- Sri Narayana Guru in Kerala (later crystallising as a full movement around temple and dignity — “one caste, one religion, one God”) grows from the same century’s question even if its peak organisation is later.
- Vaikunda Swami and early southern anti-caste devotion sit in the same long file.
- For today, hold Phule + Ramabai as the **exam-ready pair** that stops an answer from becoming a Calcutta-and-Lahore story only.

## 8. Sikh public — Singh Sabha

After annexation and after 1857, Punjab’s Sikh elite faced Christian missions and Arya polemic. Singh Sabha (Amritsar 1873, Lahore 1879) used print, schools and a cleaned public ritual to define a modern Sikh identity. Khalsa College, Amritsar (1890s) is the institutional cousin of MAO College and DAV: **community survival through college.**

# BLOCK C — Muslim streams, and the shared print battlefield (45 minutes)

Do not write “Muslims were late to reform.” Write **two Muslim methods that disagreed with each other**.

## 1. Sayyid Ahmad Khan and Aligarh

**Problem he named:** after 1857, North-Indian Muslim ashraf faced official distrust, shrinking court patronage, and a new job market that rewarded English law and science. Isolation would mean permanent exclusion from power.

**Method:**
- Reasoned theology that could live with modern science (his commentary project and the idea that nature is God’s work, so science is not kufr).
- **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College, Aligarh, 1875** (later Aligarh Muslim University). English plus Islamic instruction; loyalty to the Crown as a political tactic of recovery.
- United Indian Patriotic Association and later the Muhammadan Educational Conference: organisation before mass politics.
- He opposed the early Congress as a premature majoritarian machine that would leave Muslims as a permanent minority in an elected arithmetic. That is a **political diagnosis**, not a moral cartoon. You will meet Congress itself in a later daily file; today own why Aligarh feared it.

**Limit:** Aligarh spoke first for North-Indian Muslim gentry and service classes. It did not organise the Bengal peasant or the Mapilla fisher. Loyalty as tactic became, in later memory, a charge of separatism. Keep cause and later use distinct.

## 2. Deoband — Darul Uloom, 1866

**Problem they named:** collapse of Muslim political power; fear that custom, shrine practice and colonial schooling would dissolve a usable sharia-trained ulema.

**Method:** a seminary that trains scholars in Hanafi jurisprudence, hadith, and a reformed Sunni practice; funds from ordinary Muslims rather than a princely purse; a network of fatwa and pupils across north India. Deoband is **ulema-building**, not college-for-ICS.

**Contrast in one line:** Aligarh wants Muslims inside the colonial professional market; Deoband wants Muslims to keep an independent law-and-piety institution if that market swallows them.

## 3. Other Muslim registers (short, accurate)

- **Ahl-i-Hadith** and various Ahl-i-Quran strands: scripture over later school tradition — a Muslim cousin of “back to the Vedas.”
- **Barelvi** defence of shrine, saint and customary devotion: not “anti-reform” so much as **another reform of authenticity**.
- **Ahmadiyya** (Qadian, later split) begins in this late-century climate of prophecy, print and colonial law — know it exists; do not drown today’s file in its internal theology.
- **Nawab Abdul Latif** and Syed Ameer Ali in Bengal and in legal scholarship show that Muslim modernism had a Calcutta-London wing, not only an Aligarh wing.

**Teaching sentence:** Muslim nineteenth-century debate is a **triangle of college, madrasa and shrine**, not a single “Aligarh movement.”

# BLOCK D — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (40 minutes)

## Geography of reform — a city map you can draw

Reform is not evenly sprinkled on the subcontinent. It clusters where **port + press + college + law court** sit together.

1. **Calcutta–Hooghly corridor.** Sati debate, Brahmo congregations, Vidyasagar’s schools, Ramakrishna at Dakshineswar on the east bank, Vivekananda’s city audience. The river is a media system: boats carry tracts as well as jute.
2. **Bombay–Pune axis.** Prarthana Samaj, Phule’s Pune schools, Ranade’s conferences, Parsi reform (Dadabhai’s generation already arguing social and political questions together), Gujarati and Marathi print.
3. **Lahore–Amritsar–Jalandhar belt.** Arya Samaj, Singh Sabha, mission schools, a frontier society whose land titles and army jobs make identity urgent.
4. **Aligarh–Deoband–Delhi triangle.** Two Muslim answers within a few hours of each other on the western UP plain: college on the Grand Trunk side, seminary in a qasba.
5. **Madras–Adyar–Kerala coast.** Theosophy at Adyar; later non-Brahmin and Sri Narayana publics on the western and south-western coast where temple entry and occupation caste are everyday geography.

**Why the interior plateau is quieter in this file.** Princely Hyderabad, Mysore and many Rajput courts ran their own limited school reforms; they did not host the same density of voluntary sabhas until later. Absence in your notes is not absence in history; it is a **density difference**.

## Economy: who paid for a samaj?

1. **New surplus classes.** Opium-and-indigo commission, legal practice, government clerkship, Bombay cotton, Calcutta trade — these paid for printing presses and scholarships. Reform is cheap compared with an army; it is expensive compared with a village shrine.
2. **Job market as theology.** English education is not only culture; it is a ticket to the lower rungs of the colonial state. DAV, MAO, mission schools and government schools compete for the same boy. That is why “Anglo-Vedic” and “Anglo-Oriental” are economic brand-names.
3. **Women’s labour and widowhood as fiscal facts.** A widow without remarriage or property is a household surplus problem as well as a dharma problem. Vidyasagar and Ramabai understood the kitchen-account side.
4. **Peasant and artisan** rarely funded the first sabhas. Phule’s originality is that he tried to write *for* them while the Brahmo press still wrote *about* them.

## Ethics — four tensions for GS-IV

1. **Authenticity versus harm.** If a custom is old and a girl dies of it, which value wins? Rammohan and Vidyasagar answer: harm plus a better reading of the text. An administrator today still meets “this is our custom” in child marriage, witch accusation, and exclusion from wells. The 1829–1856 method is: **name the harm, show a legal alternative, do not pretend the custom had no meaning.**
2. **Who speaks for a community?** Keshab marrying his under-age daughter while preaching social purity is a case of **performative ethics collapsing**. Public reformers owe consistency that private householders can dodge. That is why character questions attach to reform leaders and to civil servants who lecture villages.
3. **Loyalty, dissent and minority fear.** Sayyid’s Crown loyalty is ethically ambiguous: it can be prudence after a bloodbath, or it can become a habit of distrust toward fellow subjects. A civil servant in a diverse district must hear minority arithmetic without turning it into permanent veto, and hear majority reform without turning it into humiliation.
4. **Service as ego.** Vivekananda’s mission model — serve the poor as worship — is a clean ethic when the server disappears into the work. It sours when the ashram becomes a brand. GS-IV can use this as **seva versus display.**

## Current-affairs-explained (tied to this static file)

When a modern statute on child marriage, temple entry, or uniform civil debate is in the news, do not quote a founder as if he were a party whip. Use the **method**: text-against-custom, school-against-ignorance, statute-against-harm. Then add the missing nineteenth-century limit: most reformers still assumed the colonial state as the court of appeal.

When conversions, reconversions, or “ghar wapsi” language appear, you already have shuddhi and missionary rivalry as a Punjab-century file. The usable point is institutional: **identity organisations grow where jobs, print and boundary anxiety coincide**, not because a slogan is new.

When Aligarh or Deoband appear in a contemporary campus or seminary controversy, return to the 1866–1875 fork: professional modernism versus ulema autonomy. That fork still organises argument; it does not dictate your moral conclusion.

When a girls’ school is attacked or celebrated, Savitribai and Ramabai are the right memory — not as icons to paste, but as proof that **female literacy was already a political act in the 1850s–1880s.**

# BLOCK E — CSAT practice with worked solutions (40 minutes)

Attempt first, then read the working.

## Q1. Percentages — successive change

A town’s literacy rate rises 20% in one decade and then falls 20% in the next. If it began at 45%, what is it at the end of two decades?

**Working.**
After +20%: \( 45 \times 1.20 = 54 \).
After −20%: \( 54 \times 0.80 = 43.2 \).
Successive +a% and −a% is not a return to start. Net factor \( = (1+x)(1-x) = 1-x^2 \). Here \( x = 0.20 \), so \( 45 \times (1-0.04) = 45 \times 0.96 = 43.2 \).

**Answer: 43.2%.**

## Q2. Pipes and cisterns

Pipe A fills a tank in 12 hours. Pipe B fills it in 18 hours. Pipe C empties it in 36 hours. If all three are opened together when the tank is empty, how long to fill the tank?

**Working.**
A’s 1-hour work \( = \frac{1}{12} \).
B’s 1-hour work \( = \frac{1}{18} \).
C’s 1-hour work \( = -\frac{1}{36} \).
Together: \( \frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{18} - \frac{1}{36} \).
LCM of 12, 18, 36 is 36.
\( \frac{3}{36} + \frac{2}{36} - \frac{1}{36} = \frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9} \).
Time \( = 9 \) hours.

**Answer: 9 hours.**

## Q3. Ratio and ages

At founding of a college, the ratio of teachers to students is 1 : 15. After 5 years, teachers increase by 10 and students double, and the ratio becomes 1 : 20. How many students were there at founding?

**Working.**
Let teachers at start \( = x \), students \( = 15x \).
After 5 years: teachers \( = x+10 \), students \( = 30x \).
\( \frac{x+10}{30x} = \frac{1}{20} \).
\( 20(x+10) = 30x \)
\( 20x + 200 = 30x \)
\( 200 = 10x \)
\( x = 20 \).
Students at founding \( = 15 \times 20 = 300 \).

**Answer: 300 students.**

## Q4. Direction sense

A reformer walks 4 km north, then 3 km east, then 4 km south, then 3 km west. How far is he from the start, and in which direction?

**Working.**
North 4 and south 4 cancel. East 3 and west 3 cancel. Net displacement is 0.

**Answer: at the starting point (0 km).**

## Q5. Syllogism

Statements:
1. All Brahmo tracts are printed books.
2. Some printed books are expensive.
Conclusions:
I. Some Brahmo tracts are expensive.
II. All expensive books are Brahmo tracts.

**Working.**
“All A are B” and “some B are C” does not force “some A are C.” The expensive books may all lie in the part of printed books that are not Brahmo tracts. II is a full conversion and is false.

**Answer: neither I nor II follows.**

## Q6. Statement–assumption

Statement: “Open a girls’ school in every qasba if you want widows to have a livelihood.”
Assumptions:
I. Literacy can be converted into some earning.
II. Every qasba currently has no girls’ school.

**Working.**
I is necessary — otherwise the policy does not connect school to livelihood.
II is not necessary — “open a school in every qasba” can mean universal coverage, including places that already have one (you might still add another) or it can be a slogan. The statement does not logically require that the present number is zero.

**Answer: only I is implicit.**

## Q7. Time and work

Vidyasagar can catalogue a library in 10 days. A clerk can do it in 15 days. They work together for 3 days, then the clerk leaves. How many more days does Vidyasagar need?

**Working.**
Together 1 day \( = \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{15} = \frac{3+2}{30} = \frac{5}{30} = \frac{1}{6} \).
In 3 days they finish \( \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} \).
Left \( = \frac{1}{2} \). Vidyasagar’s rate \( \frac{1}{10} \), so days \( = 5 \).

**Answer: 5 more days.**

## Q8. Simple data comparison

Publication A sells 1,200 copies at 8 annas; publication B sells 900 copies at 12 annas. Which earned more, and by how many annas? (16 annas = 1 rupee, but the question asks in annas.)

**Working.**
A: \( 1200 \times 8 = 9600 \) annas.
B: \( 900 \times 12 = 10800 \) annas.
Difference \( = 1200 \) annas in B’s favour.

**Answer: B by 1,200 annas (75 rupees).**

# BLOCK F — Active recall and model cores (25 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in speech. Then match the core.

**R1.** Why is “greased cartridge caused 1857, then reform began” a bad timeline sentence?
**Core.** Reform publics (sati 1829, Brahmo 1828, widow act 1856) already existed before the revolt. 1858 changes the *political roof*; it does not invent social argument. After 1858 the argument thickens because print, college and minority fear all grow.

**R2.** State the Brahmo split in one breath.
**Core.** Rammohan founds a monotheist sabha; Debendranath makes it more Sanskritic-devout (Adi); Keshab radicalises ritual and social practice then loses authority over the Cooch Behar child marriage; Sadharan Brahmo Samaj walks out.

**R3.** Dayananda versus Phule — same century, different enemy.
**Core.** Dayananda’s enemy is Puranic decline and conversion; his medicine is Vedic authority plus shuddhi plus schools. Phule’s enemy is caste power; his medicine is schools for the excluded and a history that does not crown the Vedic priest.

**R4.** Aligarh versus Deoband in one contrast.
**Core.** Aligarh (1875 college) trains Muslims for the colonial professional world and treats Crown loyalty as recovery. Deoband (1866 seminary) trains ulema so that sharia learning survives without that world’s permission.

**R5.** Name four cities and one institution each.
**Core.** Calcutta — Brahmo / Dakshineswar-Mission; Pune — Satyashodhak schools; Aligarh — MAO College; Deoband — Darul Uloom; Amritsar — Singh Sabha / Khalsa College; Bombay — Prarthana Samaj; Adyar — Theosophical centre.

**R6.** What economic class paid for early sabhas?
**Core.** Port-and-court surplus: lawyers, traders, clerks, some landed gentry — not, in the first wave, the dryland small peasant. Phule is the explicit attempt to change the audience.

**R7.** Give one GS-IV sentence on Keshab’s crisis.
**Core.** A reformer who asks a society to raise the age of marriage cannot spend his own household as an exception without destroying the public reason he claims to serve.

**R8.** How will you use Vivekananda in a Mains intro without turning it into a poster?
**Core.** 1893 gives colonised Hinduism an international prose; 1897 turns sannyasa into a service organisation. That is institution-building, not a slogan.
