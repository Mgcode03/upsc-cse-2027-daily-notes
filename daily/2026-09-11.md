# BLOCK A — Why a national politics became possible (50 minutes)

## 1. 1858 did not create nationalism. It created a single addressee

After the Government of India Act 1858, one Crown government in London and one Viceroy in Calcutta (later Delhi) sat at the top of the whole subcontinent. A petition that once went to a Presidency Governor could now be framed as a petition of “India.” That is an administrative fact before it is a feeling.

Other enabling facts you already have from earlier days:

- Railways and the telegraph made a Bombay lawyer and a Calcutta editor live in the same news-week.
- English law, the High Courts and the new universities (1857 Calcutta, Bombay, Madras) produced a thin but talkative professional class.
- The press that served socio-religious argument could serve budget speeches and famine reports.
- Census, maps and all-India statutes taught people that “India” was one file in Whitehall.

**Teaching sentence:** early nationalism is **the professional class learning to speak to one empire in one language** — English first, then the vernacular press.

## 2. Nationalism here does not yet mean “quit India”

From 1870 to about 1905 the public demand is almost never immediate independence. The demand is:

- Indians inside the covenanted civil service and on legislative councils;
- lower military spending charged to Indian revenues;
- less drain of money to London;
- more representative institutions on the British constitutional model;
- famine relief and irrigation that treat the peasant as a subject with a claim, not only a revenue source.

Call this **constitutional nationalism**. It is easy to mock later. In its own decade it was radical because it denied that Indians were permanently unfit for high office.

## 3. The Ilbert Bill shock (1883) — why a legal quarrel became a political school

Ilbert, Law Member, proposed that Indian covenanted civilians as district judges or sessions judges could try European British subjects in the mofussil. White non-officials in Calcutta erupted. The bill was watered down: a European accused could claim a jury with a European majority.

**What the shock taught Indian professionals:**
- Race, not competence, was the real ceiling.
- Organised agitation works — they had just watched Europeans use meetings, press and boycott-talk to bully a Viceroy (Ripon).
- Loyalty plus education would not automatically open the covenanted service.

**Teaching sentence:** Ilbert is the moment when **the moderate learns that the empire answers organisation, not only merit.**

## 4. Associations before the Congress — do not skip this step

Congress did not fall from the sky in 1885. It inherited clubs that already knew how to pass a resolution.

**Bengal**
- Landholders’ Society (1838) — zamindar interest, early, narrow.
- British Indian Association (1851) — elite, petitioning, often landlord-heavy.
- Indian League (1875, Sisir Kumar Ghosh) and Indian Association (1876, Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose) — the important upgrade: middle-class, all-India ambition, student and professional audience, agitation on civil-service age and local self-government.

**Bombay and Poona**
- Bombay Association (1852).
- Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870) — Ranade’s world: connecting Deccan peasant distress to a public memorial.
- Bombay Presidency Association (1885, just before Congress: Pherozeshah Mehta, Badruddin Tyabji, K.T. Telang).

**Madras**
- Madras Native Association (earlier, 1850s).
- Madras Mahajana Sabha (1884).

**Other pegs**
- East India Association, London (Dadabhai Naoroji, 1866) — the first durable lobby that treated India as a question *inside Britain*.
- India League and later Indian societies in London for students.

**Teaching sentence:** 1885 is a **federation of existing city associations**, not the birthday of the first Indian political thought.

# BLOCK B — The Indian National Congress, 1885–1905, taught as a method (55 minutes)

## 1. The first session — facts you must not scramble

- **December 1885, Bombay, Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College.**
- **President: Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee** (Calcutta barrister).
- About 70-odd delegates, overwhelmingly lawyers, journalists, landowners, merchants from the three Presidencies plus a few others.
- A.O. Hume, a retired ICS officer, was the organiser-secretary who wrote letters, fixed the date, and believed a safety-valve annual conference would warn the Raj of “danger from below.” Hume is real. Hume-as-sole-author is a cartoon.

**Safety-valve debate, settled for exam use:**
- Official and Hume-side memory: give educated Indians a constitutional hall so they do not go the 1857 way.
- Nationalist memory: Indians were already associating; Hume was a useful broker who could book halls and soothe Viceroys.
- Your usable line: **both can be true at once.** An imperial broker and an Indian professional class used each other. After 1905 the class outgrew the broker story.

## 2. What the early Congress actually did each December

A four-day ritual:

1. Welcome and presidential address (a long essay on the year).
2. Resolutions: simultaneous ICS examination in India and Britain; expansion of legislative councils; separation of judiciary and executive in the districts; reduction of military expenditure; inquiry into poverty; opposition to the annexation style of frontier wars charged to Indian revenues; later, opposition to the Age of Consent backlash and to reactionary press laws when they appeared.
3. Election of next year’s President (rotating by region and community when possible — a political courtesy, not a mass primary).
4. Disperse. No standing mass membership as we now imagine a party. Work between sessions was newspapers, provincial associations, and London lobbying.

**Presidents you should be able to place in the Moderate years (sample, not a dump):** Bonnerjee 1885 and 1892; Dadabhai Naoroji 1886, 1893, 1906 (the 1906 session is already the Swadeshi edge); Badruddin Tyabji 1887 (first Muslim President — used as a signal that Congress claimed to be national, not Hindu); George Yule 1888 (first English President); Pherozeshah Mehta; Ananda Mohan Bose; Gopal Krishna Gokhale 1905.

**Teaching sentence:** early Congress is an **annual national budget-and-rights conference**, not a revolutionary directorate.

## 3. The Moderate method — three tools

Name them the way a GS answer can reuse them:

**Petition.** Memorials to the Viceroy, the Secretary of State, and Parliament. The faith underneath: British public opinion and British liberalism can be shamed into consistency.

**Prayer.** Not temple prayer — the political sense: loyal language, God-save-the-Queen openings, explicit rejection of violence. This was strategy as well as belief. After 1857, the professional class had no army and did not want one.

**Protest.** Meetings, press campaigns, evidence before commissions (Welby Commission on Indian expenditure is the classic Moderate stage). Protest still meant *speech*, not boycott of British goods — boycott becomes the next chapter after 1905.

Together these are mocked as the **3 P’s**. Mockery is allowed only after you can explain why, in 1885–1900, speech-to-Westminster was the only all-India tool that did not look like another sepoy war.

## 4. What Moderates won, and what they could not win

**Wins that are easy to forget:**
- They made poverty, drain, and Indianisation of services *official questions*. Officials had to answer in Blue Books.
- Indian Councils Act 1892: councils enlarged; limited discussion of budget; nomination still the rule, but the idea of “representation of interests” crept in. Not democracy. A crack.
- A trained all-India leadership that later Extremists and Gandhi would inherit or fight — Gokhale teaching Gandhi the value of facts is a real hinge.
- Naoroji in the British House of Commons (Finsbury, 1892) as a Liberal MP: India speaking from a Westminster bench.

**Limits you must write in the same paragraph:**
- Social base: urban, English-knowing, property-touching. Peasants appear as *evidence* of poverty, not as members with a vote inside Congress.
- Faith in British justice slowed the jump to mass disobedience.
- Annual session politics could not stop a Viceroy who did not care (Curzon will arrive as the wall they hit).
- Muslim ashraf politics (Aligarh line from yesterday) already doubted that a Hindu-majority professional Congress could protect minority weight. Early Congress tried presidents like Tyabji; it did not yet design communal arithmetic. That fight is later. Do not dump 1947 onto 1887.

## 5. Curzon as the end of the Moderate weather (preview, not tomorrow’s full file)

George Nathaniel Curzon (Viceroy 1899–1905) centralised, glorified the Delhi durbar of 1903, cut the Calcutta Corporation’s elected element, imposed universities reform that Indian graduates read as control, and then partitioned Bengal in 1905. Moderates had used reason on a government that had decided reason was inefficient. **1905 is the date when petition meets a locked door.** Tomorrow-and-after files will teach Swadeshi. Today you only need the hinge.

# BLOCK C — The economic critique: drain, deindustrialisation, railways as politics (50 minutes)

This is the intellectual heart of Moderate nationalism. Prelims asks names. Mains asks the *mechanism*.

## 1. Dadabhai Naoroji and the drain of wealth

**Claim in one sentence:** a portion of India’s surplus leaves the country every year *without an equivalent commercial return*, so India can grow trade and still grow poorer.

**What counted as drain in this argument:**
- Home Charges: interest on public debt raised for wars and railways, pensions and furlough pay of British officials paid in sterling, military charges, store purchases in Britain.
- Profits and savings remitted by European private capital and by officials.
- Unrequited exports — goods leave, the silver or bills that should come back are booked against those sterling obligations.

Naoroji’s *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901 gathers the long campaign) and his earlier papers made a moral claim as well as an arithmetic one: a rule that drains is not the liberal rule Britain advertised.

**R.C. Dutt** (*The Economic History of India*) added the land-revenue and famine file: high rigid demand in a monsoon farm economy plus grain export could produce starvation beside surplus.

**G.V. Joshi, Dinshaw Wacha, Gokhale** supplied budget speeches and railway-account criticism — the Congress “economic school.”

**Teaching sentence:** drain theory is **national accounting used as a rights claim** — if money leaves as tribute, the subject may demand representation and cuts in military waste.

## 2. How to write drain without becoming a slogan

Fair official replies you should know so your answer looks adult:

- Some Home Charges paid for a navy and a market that Indian merchants also used.
- Railways and canals were real capital, even if overpriced and guaranteed to British investors.
- India also received bullion in some periods; the current-account story is not one red line every year.

Fair nationalist replies:
- Guarantee clauses socialised railway risk on Indian taxes and privatised profit in London.
- Military adventures in Africa, Tibet or the frontier were not a service the peasant ordered.
- Even if some capital stayed, the *decision-centre* of investment was not Indian.

Exam posture: **explain the mechanism, name Home Charges, then add one limit.** That beats both hymn and sneer.

## 3. Deindustrialisation — the craft question

**Claim:** cheap Lancashire cloth, tariff design, and the death of court patronage hollowed out hand-spinning and many urban crafts; India became an exporter of raw cotton and an importer of yarn and piece-goods.

**What a careful beginner should hold:**
- Bengal and parts of the Gangetic corridor show clear distress in traditional weaving towns across the nineteenth century.
- Some regions adapted (new weaving with mill yarn, jute, later Indian mills in Bombay and Ahmedabad).
- “Deindustrialisation” is not “every artisan vanished.” It is **a shift in the skill-and-surplus map** toward raw-export and, late in the century, toward a small Indian mill bourgeoisie that will fund Swadeshi.

**Prelims peg:** first successful Indian cotton mill — Bombay, 1854 (C.N. Davar’s era); jute mills on the Hooghly under British capital; Tata steel is *next* century (1907 Jamshedpur plan) — do not drag it into 1885.

## 4. Famine as political evidence

Great famines of the later nineteenth century (1876–78 South and Deccan; 1896–97; 1899–1900) sat inside Moderate speeches as proof that revenue and grain trade rules were wrong. The Famine Codes improved procedure; they did not convince Congress that the political economy was just. **Famine is why drain theory felt true in a district, not only in a London pamphlet.**

# BLOCK D — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (40 minutes)

## Geography — the presidency triangle and the session map

Draw this from memory:

1. **Bombay** — 1885 first session; merchant-Parsi-Gujarati-Maharashtrian professional mix; mill capital later; sea face to London. Mehta’s city.
2. **Calcutta** — capital of the Raj until 1911; densest press; Surendranath’s Indian Association; the city that will explode in 1905 because partition cuts its hinterland.
3. **Madras** — Mahajana Sabha; a southern professional public that is inside Congress but never as numerically loud as the two western-eastern ports in this phase.
4. **Poona** — inland intellectual capital of the Deccan; Sarvajanik Sabha; later the Moderate–Extremist tension will have a Poona face (Gokhale / Tilak — Tilak belongs to the next file; today only mark the city).
5. **London** — Naoroji, the East India Association, parliamentary questions. Early nationalism is an **Indian Ocean and North Atlantic** story, not only a subcontinental one.
6. **Allahabad, Nagpur, Lahore, Amraoti** appear as session towns when Congress tries to look all-India. Sessions follow rail junctions.

**Physical geography that mattered to the speeches:** famine belts of the Deccan trap and the northern dry years; cotton black soil of western India that tied Bombay mills and Lancashire to the same plant; jute delta of Bengal that made Calcutta rich and its hinterland politically explosive.

## Economy — who sat in the hall, who paid for the pamphlet

- Lawyers could leave a December week without losing a harvest. That is why the social type is the vakil.
- Bombay mill money and Calcutta commerce paid presses. They did not yet pay for a mass peasant wing.
- Land revenue remained the Raj’s backbone; Congress criticism of assessments was also self-interest for some landlord delegates. Write the stain.
- Home Charges and railway guarantees are the link between a peasant’s tax and a London dividend. That link is the Moderate master-key.

## Ethics — GS-IV from this week’s file

1. **Loyalty as tactic versus loyalty as identity.** Early Congress opened with allegiance and closed with a demand. A civil servant today still lives this split: you serve the Constitution and you may criticise a government. The ethical test is whether loyalty hides careerism.
2. **Representation without the represented.** Speaking *for* the famine dead from a college hall is a moral risk. Gokhale’s answer was homework — numbers, commissions, village tours. The ethic is **epistemic humility**: do not orate facts you have not checked.
3. **Racial humiliation and self-restraint.** Ilbert taught anger. Moderates chose procedure. That can be dignity or timidity. In a district riot or a communal insult, the officer’s parallel is: **use law first, do not confuse patience with denial of the injury.**
4. **Minority presence as courtesy or as right.** Electing Tyabji is a symbol. Symbols matter; they are not a substitute for institutional protection. Public organisations still confuse the two.

## Current-affairs-explained (tied to this static file)

When a modern debate asks whether India’s colonial-era railways were “gifts” or “extraction,” return to the guarantee-and-Home-Charges mechanism. The tracks exist. The financing was political. Both sentences can stand.

When civil-service recruitment, lateral entry, or exam-age controversies appear, you already own the simultaneous-examination demand of the 1880s: **who sets the gate, and in which city the gate is held, decides who enters the state.**

When official statistics on poverty are accused of politics, remember Naoroji: the fight was never only the number; it was **who has standing to define poverty in public.**

When a party session is called “only spectacle,” compare 1885: spectacle plus a published resolution was how a people without a parliament rehearsed being a public.

# BLOCK E — CSAT practice with worked solutions (40 minutes)

Attempt first, then read the working.

## Q1. Time and work

A clerk can draft a memorial in 12 days. A junior can draft it in 18 days. They work together for 4 days, then the clerk falls ill. How many more days does the junior need?

**Working.**
Clerk’s 1-day work \( = 1/12 \). Junior \( = 1/18 \).
Together in 1 day \( = 1/12 + 1/18 = (3+2)/36 = 5/36 \).
In 4 days: \( 20/36 = 5/9 \).
Left: \( 1 - 5/9 = 4/9 \).
Junior alone: days \( = (4/9) \div (1/18) = (4/9)\times 18 = 8 \).

**Answer: 8 days.**

## Q2. Percentages — drain-style successive leak

A district surplus is Rs 10,000. 15% leaves as Home Charges. Of what remains, 10% leaves as private remittance. What remains in the district?

**Working.**
After 15%: \( 10000 \times 0.85 = 8500 \).
After 10% of remainder: \( 8500 \times 0.90 = 7650 \).
Net remaining factor \( = 0.85 \times 0.90 = 0.765 \), so \( 10000 \times 0.765 = 7650 \).
Do not add 15+10=25% on the original; the second cut is on the reduced base.

**Answer: Rs 7,650.**

## Q3. Ratio

ICS recruits from two centres are in the ratio 5 : 2 (Britain : India) in a year of 84 recruits. How many were recruited in India? If next year Indian recruits rise by 50% and British stay same, new ratio?

**Working.**
Parts \( 5+2=7 \). One part \( = 84/7 = 12 \).
India this year: \( 2 \times 12 = 24 \). Britain: 60.
Next year India: \( 24 \times 1.5 = 36 \). Britain 60.
Ratio Britain : India \( = 60:36 = 5:3 \).

**Answer: 24 Indian recruits this year; new ratio 5 : 3.**

## Q4. Simple interest (budget line)

A guaranteed railway loan of Rs 8 lakh at 5% simple interest is charged to Indian revenues. What is the 4-year interest cost?

**Working.**
\( SI = P r t / 100 = 800000 \times 5 \times 4 / 100 = 160000 \).

**Answer: Rs 1,60,000.**

## Q5. Direction sense

A session delegate walks 3 km north, 4 km east, 3 km south, then 2 km west. How far is he from the start, and in which direction?

**Working.**
North 3 and south 3 cancel. Net east \( 4-2 = 2 \) km.
He is 2 km east of start.

**Answer: 2 km east.**

## Q6. Syllogism

Statements: All delegates are professionals. Some professionals are editors. Conclusions: (I) Some delegates are editors. (II) All editors are delegates.

**Working.**
“All D are P” and “Some P are E” does not force the “some P” to sit inside D. I does not follow. II reverses and universalises — does not follow.

**Answer: neither I nor II.**

## Q7. Statement–assumption

Statement: “Hold the ICS examination simultaneously in India so that poverty does not block talent.”
Assumptions: (I) Travel to London costs money. (II) Talent exists in India.

**Working.**
Both are necessary. If London were free and easy, simultaneous exams would not be argued as a poverty measure. If no Indian talent existed, the policy would be pointless. Standard two-assumption accept.

**Answer: both I and II.**

## Q8. Average

Five provincial associations send 12, 18, 20, 10 and 15 delegates. Mean? If a sixth association sends 21, new mean?

**Working.**
Sum \( = 75 \). Mean \( = 75/5 = 15 \).
New sum \( = 96 \). Mean \( = 96/6 = 16 \).

**Answer: 15; then 16.**

# BLOCK F — Active recall with model cores (30 minutes)

Close the notes. Speak an answer. Then match the core — not the wording, the load-bearing facts.

**1. Why could a national politics appear after 1858 if 1857 had failed?**  
Core: one Crown addressee; rail-telegraph-press-university; a professional class that petitioned instead of mutinying; race ceilings (Ilbert) that taught organisation.

**2. Name three pre-Congress bodies and their cities.**  
Core: Indian Association 1876 Calcutta; Poona Sarvajanik Sabha 1870; Madras Mahajana Sabha 1884 (Bombay Presidency Association 1885 and East India Association London 1866 as bonus).

**3. First Congress session — place, president, Hume’s role without the cartoon.**  
Core: Bombay, December 1885, W.C. Bonnerjee; Hume retired ICS broker-organiser; safety-valve and Indian initiative can coexist.

**4. What did Moderates actually demand?**  
Core: simultaneous ICS, larger councils, less military charge, inquiry into poverty/drain, separation of district executive and judiciary — not immediate independence.

**5. Define drain in a mechanism sentence.**  
Core: surplus leaving as Home Charges, pensions, stores and remitted profits without equivalent return, visible as unrequited exports.

**6. Why is 1892 a Moderate “win” and not a parliament?**  
Core: Indian Councils Act 1892 enlarged discussion, including budget talk, but nomination and official majorities remained; a crack, not power.

**7. Ilbert Bill in two lines.**  
Core: Indian judges to try Europeans in the mofussil; white agitation diluted it to a European-majority jury option; taught Indians that race beat merit.

**8. Ethical trap of early Congress.**  
Core: speaking for famine peasants from an English hall without organising them; loyalty language that can become habit; minority presence as courtesy rather than design.

**9. Map: why Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, London.**  
Core: ports + courts + colleges + steam link to Westminster; nationalism’s first geography is the presidency triangle plus London.

**10. What ends the Moderate weather?**  
Core: Curzon’s centralisation and the 1905 partition of Bengal turn unanswered petition into Swadeshi — next file.
