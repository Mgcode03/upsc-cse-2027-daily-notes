# BLOCK A — India inside the First World War (55 minutes)

## 1. How India entered a war it did not declare

On 4 August 1914 Britain declared war on Germany. India was not a dominion with its own cabinet. It was an empire possession. The Viceroy (Charles Hardinge until 1916, then Chelmsford) announced that India was at war because the King-Emperor was at war. No Indian legislature voted the decision. No Indian electorate existed that could have voted it.

**Teaching sentence:** colonial India fought a world war by **viceregal proclamation**, not by national consent.

That fact is the ethical floor of every later demand for responsible government. A people who can be marched to Basra without a vote will later ask why they cannot vote on a salt tax.

## 2. What India actually supplied — hold the scale, not a fake exact census

Learn the *kinds* of contribution. Numbers in textbooks vary by whether they count combatants, labour corps, or all who left Indian ports. For Prelims, remember the **order of magnitude and the theatres**, not a single sacred headcount.

- **Men.** More than a million Indians served overseas in one capacity or another across the war years — combat units, followers, labour. The largest recruiting ground was Punjab, then the United Provinces, the Bombay Presidency, and Nepal (Gurkha regiments under British Indian organisation). The “martial races” doctrine — a Raj sorting of communities thought loyal and hardy — decided who was asked first.
- **Theatres.** Western Front (France and Flanders, 1914–15 especially); East Africa against German forces; Gallipoli and the wider Ottoman war; above all **Mesopotamia** (modern Iraq) — the campaign that ate Indian divisions. Smaller commitments in Egypt, Palestine, Aden, and the North-West Frontier.
- **Money and material.** India paid a large war gift to Britain, raised war loans, ran munitions and clothing production, and saw railways and shipping commandeered. Food and raw cotton left while domestic prices rose.
- **Princes.** Many princely states offered troops, money, and hospital ships. The Raj treated this as proof that “native India” was loyal. Nationalists later treated it as proof that loyalty had not been paid in power.

**Mesopotamia, 1915–16, Kut.** British-Indian forces advanced up the Tigris toward Baghdad, were stopped, and a large force under Townshend was besieged at Kut al-Amara and forced to surrender in April 1916. Disease, heat, and supply failure killed more men than glory. Indian sepoys and followers paid a disproportionate price. After Kut, London reorganised the campaign; Baghdad fell in 1917. For your map: **Bombay and Karachi → Persian Gulf → Basra → Tigris → Kut → Baghdad**.

## 3. Why many Indian leaders first supported the war

This surprises beginners who expect 1914 to look like 1942. It did not.

- **Moderates** around the Congress still believed that loyal help in Britain’s danger would purchase the next constitutional step — the step 1909 had refused.
- **Dominion language** was in the air: white colonies were fighting as partners; Indian liberals asked to be treated as a future partner, not a barracks.
- **The League** in these years was not a secessionist party. Many League notables also pledged loyalty, hoping weightage and separate electorates would be protected and enlarged.
- **Gandhi**, back from South Africa in 1915, argued in this phase that Indians who claimed citizenship of the empire should share its danger. He recruited for the war. Do not drag 1930 Gandhi backward onto 1918 Gandhi.

**Tilak**, released in 1914 after Mandalay imprisonment, did not sabotage recruitment. He used the war *window* to demand Home Rule, not to sink troopships.

**Teaching sentence:** 1914 loyalty was a **bargain offered**, not a conversion. When the bargain was not paid, the same men changed method.

## 4. What the war did inside India — the home front you must be able to write

- **Recruitment pressure.** In Punjab especially, officials and landlords were scored on how many men they produced. Voluntary enlistment shaded into village pressure. That memory feeds later Punjab politics, including the bitterness after 1919 — preview only.
- **Defence of India Act, 1915.** Wartime statute: detention without ordinary trial, special tribunals, gag on suspects. Revolutionaries and Ghadar suspects were its first harvest. The habit of emergency law outlives the emergency. Rowlatt (next files) is this habit trying to stay after the guns stop.
- **Prices.** Wheat, cloth, kerosene, and rail freights moved against the poor. Urban workers and fixed-salary clerks felt 1917–18 as a squeeze. Famine risk rose where monsoon and export met.
- **Influenza, 1918.** The pandemic killed on a scale that dwarfed many battles. It is part of the war’s Indian death-roll even when no German shell is involved.
- **Industrial side-effect.** Some Indian mills and workshops grew because British industry was busy and shipping was scarce. That is not “decolonisation of capital.” It is wartime shortage creating a gap Indian owners could fill — a gap they would fight to keep after 1918 with tariff politics.

## 5. Political weather by 1916–17

By the third year the early loyalty speech sounded thin.

- Casualties and Kut were public.
- Prices were public.
- No dominion gift had arrived.
- Irish Home Rule and the language of national self-determination (later Wilson, 1918) gave Indian writers a vocabulary London could not monopolise.
- Congress and League, both hungry for the next Act, found a temporary common script at Lucknow.

Hold that turn. The next three blocks are the *Indian* answers to the same war.

# BLOCK B — Ghadar: the diaspora tries to light the Punjab (45 minutes)

## 1. Who the Ghadarites were

**Ghadar** means revolt — the 1857 word reused on the Pacific coast.

Punjabi peasants and soldiers had gone to **Canada and the Pacific United States** as labourers, farm workers, and mill hands in the decade before the war. They met colour bars, the continuous-journey rule that blocked Indian immigration to Canada, and the humiliation of the **Komagata Maru** (1914): a Japanese ship hired by Gurdit Singh carrying mostly Sikh passengers was refused landing at Vancouver and forced back; violence followed at Budge Budge near Calcutta when the returned men were handled as a security problem. That voyage is the emotional preface to wartime Ghadar. Learn the chain: **Punjab village → Pacific labour market → racial exclusion → ship turned away → revolutionary paper**.

**The Ghadar Party / Ghadar movement** organised in San Francisco and nearby Punjabi settlements. The weekly *Ghadar* (Urdu and Gurmukhi, later other languages) preached that the British Empire was weakest when it was at war in Europe, and that Punjab soldiers should turn their rifles. Leaders and organisers you should be able to place: **Lala Har Dayal** (intellectual spark, later leaving the US under pressure), **Sohan Singh Bhakna**, **Kartar Singh Sarabha**, and the wider Pacific network. Do not turn the list into a phone book. Own the *type*: peasant-soldiers abroad, not Calcutta lawyers.

## 2. The 1915 plan and why it failed

When war began, Ghadar called men home. Ships carried returnees toward Calcutta, Colombo, and Punjab. The plan was roughly: contact units, raise villages, seize magazines, declare a republic in the north.

It failed for reasons you should write as a short paragraph, not as fate:

- British intelligence, including informants inside the network, met the ships.
- The Defence of India Act made rounding-up legal and fast.
- Most serving sepoys did not mutiny on cue. Barrack loyalty, fear, and incomplete cells mattered more than poetry.
- The **Lahore Conspiracy Cases** (1915 onward) hanged or transported a generation of returnees. Kartar Singh Sarabha was executed young; his name is the Ghadar martyr Prelims likes.
- The **Hindu–German conspiracy** thread — German money and arms hoped for by some Ghadar and Bengal revolutionaries — produced more files than successful landings. The *Annie Larsen* / *Maverick* arms story is a footnote, not the spine.

**Rash Behari Bose**, already in your 1912 Delhi-bomb memory, tried to coordinate a February 1915 rising inside India. When it collapsed he escaped toward Japan. That exit matters for a later file on East Asian revolutionary shelter. Do not dump the Indian National Army into 1915.

## 3. What Ghadar changed even in failure

- It internationalised Indian revolt: Vancouver and San Francisco became political addresses.
- It tied **racial immigration law** to **anti-colonial war** — a link still useful when you write about diaspora and citizenship.
- It terrified the Punjab administration into harsher village policing, which then became part of the 1919 explosion.
- It showed the limit of the first revolutionary method: conspiracy without a mass organisation at home, timed to a war the barracks would not desert.

**Teaching sentence:** Ghadar is **1857 attempted with steamships and a weekly paper**, broken by intelligence and by the sepoy who did not come out of the lines.

# BLOCK C — Home Rule Leagues and the Lucknow reunion (55 minutes)

## 1. Why Home Rule, and why 1916

“Home Rule” is the Irish phrase: self-government inside the empire, not immediate severance. Tilak and Annie Besant judged that wartime Britain could not preach freedom in Belgium and refuse a timetable in India.

This is **not** the 1920 mass satyagraha. It is organised public demand, branches, newspapers, and membership — legal until the Raj starts internments.

## 2. Two leagues, two geographies, one demand

**Tilak’s Home Rule League (April 1916).** Base in Maharashtra and the Karnataka country, with strength in the Central Provinces and Berar as well. Method: well-run branches, Marathi and English argument, work among the politically awake who still remembered Extremism. Tilak’s line: Home Rule is the birthright; use every constitutional opening; do not wait for Moderate permission.

**Besant’s All-India Home Rule League (September 1916).** Base in Madras Presidency and a wider all-India net through Theosophical contacts, *New India* and *Commonweal*, and lecture tours. Besant was Irish-English, a public speaker who could embarrass London in London’s own language. That is a political fact, not trivia.

**Teaching sentence:** Tilak owned the **Deccan membership machine**; Besant owned the **English-language megaphone and the southern network**. Together they made Home Rule a household phrase in 1917.

They cooperated more than they fused. Do not write “one league.” Write two organisations, one slogan.

## 3. What the movement actually did

- Opened branches in towns that had never hosted a standing political body after Surat.
- Sold pamphlets that translated dominion status into bazaar language.
- Forced Moderates to stop treating self-government as a distant prize.
- Pulled women into public meetings in a way the 1907 Extremist stage had not, especially around Besant.
- Drew a Raj reply: restrictions on Tilak’s movement in some districts; **internment of Besant and her associates in June 1917**. Internment advertised the cause. Release followed pressure and the Montagu turn.

Congress, still carrying the Surat scar, moved toward reunion. Extremists were back in the Congress fold by the Lucknow year. Home Rule was the bridge.

## 4. Lucknow Pact, December 1916 — learn the bargain, not the myth

Two meetings in the same city and season: Congress and League. The **Lucknow Pact** is the joint scheme they accepted on how a future Indian legislature should be built.

**What Congress accepted that it had long resisted:**
- Separate electorates for Muslims would continue in a future reformed council system.
- **Weightage:** Muslims would get a higher share of seats than their population share in provinces where they were a minority (United Provinces, Madras, Bombay, etc.).
- In Bengal and Punjab, where Muslims were a large part of the population, the share was set by negotiation so that neither side walked out — a capped majority logic, not raw census logic.
- Imperial and provincial proportions were written as a package so that League leaders could sell the deal as protection plus voice.

**What League accepted:**
- A common nationalist demand for a large elected majority in councils and for Indians in the executive — the language of moving beyond 1909.
- Joint pressure on the Raj instead of a purely loyal-minority strategy.

**Who signed the moment in your memory:** Congress presidency in that season is associated with the reunion atmosphere after years of split; League under **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**’s still-Congress-friendly phase treated the pact as a lawyer’s settlement between two political firms. Jinnah at Lucknow is a nationalist constitutionalist. Do not paste 1940 Jinnah onto 1916 Jinnah.

**Teaching sentence:** Lucknow is **Congress buying Muslim League partnership with the 1909 device it once called a wound**, and League buying a joint attack on the Raj with that same device written down as contract.

## 5. Why Lucknow mattered, and why it was fragile

It mattered because:
- The Raj could no longer say “the two organisations do not speak together.”
- Separate electorates gained *nationalist consent*, not only viceregal invention. That consent haunts later constitution-making.
- Home Rule plus pact plus war sacrifice created the pressure that produced Montagu’s words in 1917.

It was fragile because:
- The pact was elite arithmetic. It did not ask the peasant in a mixed village how he wished to vote.
- Weightage in one province is another province’s grievance waiting to start.
- A pact about **seats** is not a pact about **sovereignty**. When mass religion enters street politics after 1920, seat-ratios will not hold the peace by themselves.

# BLOCK D — Montagu’s promise, geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (45 minutes)

## 1. The August Declaration, 20 August 1917

**Edwin Montagu**, Secretary of State for India, told the House of Commons that British policy was the gradual development of **self-governing institutions** with a view to the **progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire**.

Unpack the phrase. Every adjective is a political brake.

- **Gradual / progressive** — no date. London keeps the clock.
- **Responsible government** — executive answerable to an elected house, the Westminster jewel. This is the word 1909 refused to use.
- **In India** — not only in white dominions.
- **Integral part of the British Empire** — dominion path, not exit path.

**Teaching sentence:** 1917 is the first time the Raj **officially names responsible government as the destination** while keeping the keys to the timetable.

Montagu then toured India. The **Montagu–Chelmsford Report** (1918) and the **Government of India Act 1919** (dyarchy in provinces) are the next file’s statute. Today you only need the declaration as wartime political payment.

Why pay now? Irish trouble, war weariness, Home Rule noise, Lucknow unity, American talk of self-determination, and the need to keep Indian recruitment and finance flowing. A sentence in Parliament is cheaper than a dominion cabinet.

## 2. Geography you must be able to draw tonight

1. **Punjab plains — recruitment heartland.** Doab of the Ravi-Beas-Sutlej, canal colonies, railway to Karachi and Bombay. Ghadar returnees and war battalions use the same stations.
2. **Karachi and Bombay** — troop ports toward the Gulf.
3. **Basra–Kut–Baghdad** on the Shatt al-Arab and Tigris. Desert and river logistics, not European mud.
4. **France and Flanders** — Indian expeditionary force in industrial war, winter 1914–15.
5. **San Francisco – Vancouver – Singapore – Calcutta** — Ghadar’s sea square.
6. **Budge Budge** on the Hooghly below Calcutta — Komagata Maru landing-point of the tragedy.
7. **Pune–Nagpur–Berar** — Tilak Home Rule belt.
8. **Madras city and presidency towns** — Besant belt.
9. **Lucknow** on the Gomati in the Awadh plains — pact city, between Delhi’s new capital and the eastern Ganga corridor.
10. **Mandalay** — Tilak’s pre-war prison, so you remember why 1914 release starts his last great campaign.

**Physical fact that explains recruitment:** canal-colony Punjab produced marketed wheat *and* surplus sons. The same agrarian density that fed Bombay also fed the army lists.

## 3. Economy — war as a price machine

- Recruitment is a labour export. A village loses plough-hands and gains a money-order — until the money-order stops.
- War loans drain liquid savings of the comfortable into imperial paper.
- Inflation is a hidden tax. The poor pay it in cloth and grain; the mill owner may gain from shortage.
- Jute and cotton demand swings with military need and shipping risk. Eastern Bengal’s peasant is still on the world price, as in your Swadeshi file, but now the shock is war freight, not only a boycott.
- Princely gifts are public finance by theatre: loyalty displayed as cheque and cavalry.
- Post-war, India will claim a tariff personality (fiscal autonomy convention begins to be argued after 1919). Plant that seed; do not write the whole 1920s economy today.

## 4. Ethics — GS-IV from this file

1. **Consent to war.** Sending another people’s sons without their representative yes is the colonial original sin of 1914. A modern state that conscripts or recruits without a real public should hear this file.
2. **Loyalty as invoice.** Moderates issued an invoice: blood now, status later. A government that accepts the blood and postpones the status teaches the next generation not to invoice but to strike.
3. **Separate electorates with nationalist consent.** It is one moral event when a ruler imposes identity rolls. It is another when a national movement *contracts* them to buy unity. Lucknow is the second event. You may still defend it as the possible politics of 1916; you must not pretend it was costless.
4. **Diaspora and dignity.** Komagata Maru is about the right to land and the right to be seen as a subject equal to a white subject. Racial immigration rules are constitutional facts, not only social slights.
5. **Means in wartime conspiracy.** Ghadar aimed at the army’s oath. Even if one judges the empire illegitimate, a plan that needs mass murder inside mixed cantonments has a moral remainder. Write the remainder. Do not cancel it with the word freedom, and do not cancel freedom with the word mutiny.

## 5. Current-affairs-explained (tied to this static file)

When a government thanks soldiers while delaying a political reform, run the **1914–17 test**: is gratitude a substitute for a timetable?

When two parties share power by freezing community quotas, run the **Lucknow test**: did they write a peace between organisations, or a peace between citizens?

When a diaspora protest meets a closed port, run the **Komagata test**: which law is being used — health, security, or race wearing those masks?

When an official speech promises “progressive realisation” of a right, run the **Montagu test**: who holds the clock, and can the public see the hands?

# BLOCK E — CSAT practice with worked solutions (40 minutes)

Do these without a calculator. Write every step.

## Q1. Time and work

A wartime clothing shed has three cutting lines. A alone finishes a batch in 12 days, B in 18 days, C in 36 days. A works all days; B joins from day 4; C joins only for the last 3 days. If they exactly finish the batch when C stops, how many days did A work?

**Worked solution**

Rates: A = 1/12, B = 1/18, C = 1/36 per day.

Let A work *n* days. Then B works *(n − 3)* days (joins from day 4, so misses 3 days). C works 3 days.

```
 n/12 + (n − 3)/18 + 3/36 = 1
```

LCM of 12, 18, 36 is 36.

```
 3n/36 + 2(n − 3)/36 + 3/36 = 1
 3n + 2n − 6 + 3 = 36
 5n − 3 = 36
 5n = 39
 n = 39/5 = 7.8 days
```

**Answer: 7.8 days (or 7 days and 4/5 of a day).**

Sanity: A and then B together after day 3 are much faster than 1/12; finishing under 8 days is plausible.

## Q2. Percentages and inflation

A clerk’s salary is Rs 50 a month in 1916. Grain that cost Rs 4 takes Rs 5 in 1918 (25% dearer). Cloth that took 20% of his 1916 salary now takes 30% of the *same* salary. If grain was 40% of the 1916 salary, what percent of the 1918 salary (still Rs 50) do grain and cloth together consume?

**Worked solution**

1916 grain = 40% of 50 = Rs 20. Wait: the question says the *listed* grain price moves from 4 to 5, and grain was 40% of salary in 1916. Use the 40% share for 1916 quantity, then apply the 25% price rise to that spend.

1916 grain spend = 0.40 × 50 = Rs 20.  
1918 grain spend = 20 × 1.25 = Rs 25.

1916 cloth spend = 0.20 × 50 = Rs 10.  
1918 cloth spend = 0.30 × 50 = Rs 15.

Together in 1918 = 25 + 15 = Rs 40 out of 50 = **80%**.

**Answer: 80%.**

## Q3. Ratio and the pact arithmetic

A council has 100 elected seats. Community M is 30% of population. A pact gives M 40 seats (weightage). The remaining 60 seats are shared by others in the ratio 5 : 3 between groups H and R. How many seats does H get, and what is H’s seat share if H is 55% of population?

**Worked solution**

H : R = 5 : 3 of 60 → 8 parts = 60 → 1 part = 7.5.  
H = 5 × 7.5 = **37.5 seats**. (If seats must be whole, the paper would say so; here the ratio is exact.)

H seat share = 37.5%. Population share = 55%.  
Under-representation gap = 55 − 37.5 = **17.5 percentage points**.

**Answer: H gets 37.5 seats; seat share 37.5% against 55% population.**

This is the Lucknow shape: one community’s weightage is another community’s compressed remainder.

## Q4. Boats (as transport to Basra)

A supply launch goes downstream 30 km in 2 hours and returns the same 30 km in 3 hours. Find the speed of the current and the speed of the launch in still water.

**Worked solution**

Downstream speed = 30/2 = 15 km/h.  
Upstream speed = 30/3 = 10 km/h.

Still water *b* and current *c*:

```
 b + c = 15
 b − c = 10
```

Add: 2b = 25 → b = 12.5 km/h.  
Subtract: 2c = 5 → c = 2.5 km/h.

**Answer: still water 12.5 km/h; current 2.5 km/h.**

## Q5. Coding

If HOME is written as 8-15-13-5 and RULE as 18-21-12-5, how is PACT written in the same cipher?

**Worked solution**

The numbers are ordinary alphabet positions: H=8, O=15, M=13, E=5; R=18, U=21, L=12, E=5.

P=16, A=1, C=3, T=20.

**Answer: 16-1-3-20.**

## Q6. Syllogism

Statements:
1. All wartime ordinances are emergency laws.
2. Some emergency laws are permanent in effect.
3. No permanent-in-effect law is harmless.

Conclusions:
I. Some wartime ordinances are permanent in effect.
II. Some emergency laws are not harmless.
III. All wartime ordinances are harmless.

**Worked solution**

From 1: wartime ordinances ⊆ emergency laws.  
From 2: a part of emergency laws is permanent-in-effect. That part need not overlap the wartime-ordinance subset. So I does not follow.

From 2 and 3: the permanent-in-effect emergency laws sit inside “not harmless.” So **some emergency laws are not harmless**. II follows.

III contradicts the possibility that some ordinances could later be classed permanent, and in any case “all harmless” is unsupported. III does not follow.

**Answer: only II follows.**

## Q7. Mixture

A depot mixes two grains. Type P costs Rs 3 a seer, type Q Rs 5 a seer. How many seers of Q must be mixed with 20 seers of P so that the mix values at Rs 3.80 a seer?

**Worked solution**

Alligation:  
P at 3, Q at 5, mean 3.8.  
Gap Q to mean = 1.2; gap mean to P = 0.8.  
Ratio P : Q = 1.2 : 0.8 = 3 : 2.

If P = 20 seers = 3 parts, 1 part = 20/3.  
Q = 2 parts = 40/3 seers.

**Answer: 40/3 seers of Q.**

## Q8. Statement and assumption

Statement: “Because Indian soldiers served on three continents, India must be granted responsible government at once.”

Which assumption is necessary?

A. No colony that did not send soldiers deserves self-government.  
B. Service in imperial war creates a just claim to a share of imperial political power.  
C. Responsible government is possible only after a world war.  
D. Indian soldiers served voluntarily in every case.

**Worked solution**

The leap is from military service to political status. That leap requires B. A is a different universal; C invents a timing rule not used; D is about recruitment ethics, not about the claim’s logic.

**Answer: B.**

# BLOCK F — Active recall with model cores (25 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in two or three sentences. Then match the core.

**R1.** Why did Indian Moderates support the war in 1914?

*Core:* They treated men and money as a bill the empire would have to pay in constitutional coin — dominion-style responsible government — because 1909 had given voice without power.

**R2.** What did the Defence of India Act 1915 do that ordinary criminal law did not?

*Core:* It let the wartime state detain and try suspects outside the slow, public, rights-heavy process, so Ghadar and revolutionary cases could be cleared at emergency speed.

**R3.** Why did Ghadar fail in 1915?

*Core:* Ships were watched, the Act made arrests easy, barracks did not rise on schedule, and conspiracy trials removed the returnee leadership before a rural mass could form.

**R4.** How did Tilak’s League differ from Besant’s?

*Core:* Tilak built a Deccan-centred membership machine after his 1914 release; Besant built a Madras-and-all-India lecture-and-press net. Same demand, two organisations.

**R5.** What did Congress concede at Lucknow?

*Core:* Continuation of separate electorates plus weightage for Muslims in minority provinces, in return for a joint League-Congress scheme demanding elected majorities and Indian executives.

**R6.** What is the trap inside Montagu’s August 1917 sentence?

*Core:* It names responsible government as policy but binds it with “gradual,” “progressive,” and “integral part of the Empire,” so London still owns the clock.

**R7.** Why is Mesopotamia the Indian war theatre you must be able to map?

*Core:* It was the great consumer of British-Indian units, ended in the Kut surrender of 1916, and ran along the Basra–Tigris line supplied from western Indian ports.

**R8.** How does Komagata Maru connect social exclusion to revolt?

*Core:* A ship of Indian migrants refused at Vancouver and mishandled at Budge Budge taught Pacific Punjabis that imperial subjecthood was racially ranked, which Ghadar then turned into a wartime call to rise.
