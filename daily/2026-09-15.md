# BLOCK A — Rowlatt, satyagraha, and the Punjab explosion (55 minutes)

## 1. What the Rowlatt Act actually was

The war ended in November 1918. The Defence of India Act 1915 was a wartime statute. The Raj did not want the habit to die.

A committee under **Sydney Rowlatt** recommended that special powers — detention without ordinary trial, in-camera proceedings, restrictions on suspects — should continue in peacetime against “anarchists” and revolutionaries. Two bills followed. One became law in March 1919 as the **Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act**, universally called the **Rowlatt Act**.

**Teaching sentence:** Rowlatt is **war emergency law trying to live after the armistice**, aimed at conspiracy cases, felt by the public as a general insult to the 1917 promise.

Hold the political insult, not a clause-by-clause criminal-procedure lecture:

- Arrest and detention without the ordinary safeguards of a public trial.
- Special courts and restricted legal defence in designated cases.
- The elected Indian members of the Imperial Legislative Council opposed the bill; it passed because official and nominated weight still ruled that house. That vote is why Rowlatt became a *legitimacy* crisis, not only a lawyers’ brief.

**Montagu had promised responsible government.** Rowlatt said: first we keep the lock.

## 2. Gandhi’s first national satyagraha

Mohandas Gandhi had, since 1915, run local campaigns: Champaran (indigo, 1917), Ahmedabad mill strike (1918), Kheda revenue satyagraha (1918). Those taught him peasants, mill-hands, and Gujarat patrons. Rowlatt was the first issue he tried to nationalise.

**Satyagraha** here means public refusal to obey an unjust law, accepting penalty, without hatred as the programme. It is not yet the full 1930 salt march. In 1919 it was hartal, fasting, meetings, and a pledge not to accept Rowlatt.

**6 April 1919** was observed as a hartal day in many towns. The method jumped faster than Gandhi’s control. In Delhi, Bombay, Ahmedabad and above all **Punjab**, protest mixed with rumour, railway disruption, and attacks on Europeans after arrests. Gandhi called off the Rowlatt satyagraha when violence spread — a preview of Chauri Chaura’s logic, two years early.

**Teaching sentence:** 1919 is Gandhi discovering that **a continent can stand up on the same morning**, and that he cannot yet guarantee how it will sit down.

## 3. Amritsar, 10–13 April 1919

Punjab was the recruitment heartland of yesterday’s file. It was also a province of canal colonies, returning soldiers, wartime price pain, and a lieutenant-governorship under **Michael O’Dwyer** that treated political meeting as sedition with a rural accent.

On 10 April in Amritsar, popular leaders **Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew** and **Dr Satyapal** were deported. Crowds moving toward official buildings were fired on. In the hours that followed, Europeans were killed and banks and symbols of the Raj attacked. An English missionary, Marcella Sherwood, was assaulted on a street. The city was then placed under the army. **Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer** took operational charge of Amritsar.

**13 April 1919 — Baisakhi.** A large crowd gathered in **Jallianwala Bagh**, a walled garden enclosure with few narrow exits, near the Golden Temple precinct. Some had come for the festival, some for a political meeting that had been banned under a new proclamation many in the crowd had not heard or could not read. Dyer marched a contingent of soldiers to the main entrance, sealed the exit behind them, and ordered fire into the densely packed ground without a usable warning that the crowd could disperse.

Fire continued for minutes. Official British figures later admitted hundreds dead; Indian estimates ran far higher. The exact census is still fought. For Prelims and Mains you need the **structure of the crime**, not a sacred headcount: enclosed ground, blocked exits, continuous fire, no medical arrangement, and a commander who later said he meant to produce a *moral effect* on the Punjab.

**Crawling order.** In the lane where Sherwood had been attacked, Dyer ordered that Indians passing must crawl. Public flogging and salaaming orders accompanied martial law across several Punjab districts. This is why Jallianwala is never only the garden. It is a **province treated as a conquered tract ten months after the world war those same districts had supplied**.

## 4. Hunter Committee and the national split in British conscience

The Government appointed the **Disorders Inquiry Committee** (Hunter Committee, 1919–20). It had British and Indian members. The majority criticised Dyer’s continuing fire and the crawling order but treated the massacre as an error of judgement in a dangerous week. Indian members wrote a sharper dissent. Dyer was removed from command and not re-employed in India; he was not criminally tried. A British public subscription and sections of the House of Lords treated him as the man who “saved India.”

**Rabindranath Tagore** resigned his knighthood. Gandhi returned his own war-era decorations. Congress ran its own inquiry. These gestures matter because they convert a Punjab atrocity into an **all-India moral fact**.

**Teaching sentence:** Hunter is the Raj **scolding a general without surrendering the doctrine that Indian crowds may be taught by bullets**.

The Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford dyarchy) arrives in the same political weather. Do not mix the statute into today’s massacre narrative beyond this: a constitution promising gradual responsibility was gazetted over a province still tasting martial law. That contrast is a Mains paragraph by itself.

# BLOCK B — Khilafat: why a Turkish office became an Indian mass issue (40 minutes)

## 1. The office and the fear

The **Ottoman Sultan** was, for many Sunni Muslims, the **Khalifa** — the symbolic head of a worldwide community of believers. The office was as much prestige and law-school memory as territorial command. The First World War ended with the Ottoman Empire defeated and facing partition under Allied plans (the cluster of arrangements around Sèvres). Indian Muslims who had fought *for* Britain watched Britain help dismantle the last great Muslim empire.

**Teaching sentence:** Khilafat in India is **not a demand to annex Anatolia to Delhi**. It is a demand that the Raj, which had used Muslim soldiers, not humiliate the remaining symbol of a Muslim world order.

## 2. Who organised it

The **Khilafat Committee** (1919 onward) brought together a mix you must be able to sort:

- **Ali brothers — Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali** — orators and journalists who had been interned during the war.
- **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** — already arguing that Indian freedom and Islamic dignity were one struggle, not two queues.
- **Hasrat Mohani** and a wider ulema and town-notable network.
- Financiers and professionals in Bombay and North Indian towns who could pay for tours and papers.

Gandhi joined the Khilafat demand as a just grievance of a community inside the nation he wanted to build. He also saw organisation: mosque networks, merchants, and a cause that could bring Muslims into a joint programme with Congress after Lucknow’s elite pact.

**Do not write “Gandhi invented Khilafat.”** Write: Gandhi **nationalised** a Muslim religious-political grievance by tying it to swaraj and non-cooperation.

## 3. Why the alliance was powerful and why it was perishable

Powerful because:

- It gave Non-Cooperation a mass beyond the old Congress-educated belt.
- It made Hindu–Muslim unity a *programme*, not a banquet toast.
- It connected Indian politics to a world event beginners can map: Istanbul, Anatolia, Allied partition talk.

Perishable because:

- The object was in **Turkey**, not in the Indian statute book. When Mustafa Kemal’s nationalist Turkey itself abolished the Caliphate in **1924**, the Indian demand lost its foreign addressee.
- A religious symbol plus street mobilisation can also feed riot when the joint leadership cracks — the mid-1920s communal slide is the next files’ warning, not today’s chapter-end boast.
- Congress wanted swaraj; Khilafat leaders wanted the Khalifa protected. Same hartal, two clocks.

**Treaty of Sèvres (1920)** as the insult document; **Treaty of Lausanne (1923)** and the Turkish Republic as the historical solvent. You need those names as the international frame, not as a diplomatic monograph.

# BLOCK C — Non-Cooperation, 1920–22: the programme, the spread, the halt (55 minutes)

## 1. How Congress adopted the method

In 1920 Gandhi proposed that India withdraw cooperation from a government that had delivered Rowlatt, Amritsar, and a harsh Turkish peace. The **Calcutta special session (September 1920)** accepted non-cooperation in principle. The **Nagpur session (December 1920)** recast Congress: the goal became **swaraj**, the method non-cooperation, the organisation a hierarchy of committees down toward the village, and membership opened with a small fee so that the body could claim to be more than a December club of lawyers.

**Teaching sentence:** Nagpur 1920 turns Congress from a **festival of petitions** into a **machine that can ask a student to leave a government college**.

Tilak died in August 1920. The Home Rule generation’s senior voice was gone; Gandhi’s programme filled the year.

## 2. The programme you must list in order

Non-cooperation was staged, not a single riot of refusals:

1. **Surrender of titles and honours** granted by the Raj.
2. **Boycott of legislatures** created under the 1919 Act — a direct snub to dyarchy’s new councils.
3. **Boycott of law courts** — lawyers to give up practice; parties to try arbitration.
4. **Boycott of government schools and colleges** — national schools as the replacement ideal.
5. **Boycott of foreign cloth** — swadeshi revived as a household act; **khadi** as the uniform of the movement.
6. Later talk of **refusal of taxes** and mass civil disobedience — promised as a last stage, never fully nationalised before the halt.
7. **Hijrat** in some Khilafat circles — migration from a land deemed unholy under infidel rule — a side current, not the Congress centre. Some Muslims left toward Afghanistan; the episode shows religious intensity inside the coalition.

Constructive work ran beside refusal: spinning, national education, temperance, and the attempt to settle Hindu–Muslim local fights. Gandhi treated the spinning wheel as discipline, not folklore.

## 3. What actually happened on the ground

- Titles were returned by a visible minority; the gesture was propaganda gold.
- Council boycott left the 1919 legislatures to those willing to work dyarchy — a split that will produce Swarajists in a later file.
- Student walkouts and lawyer suspensions were real in pockets (Gujarat, Bengal, Punjab towns, Andhra, Bihar) and patchy elsewhere.
- Foreign-cloth bonfires and khadi sales were the most *nationally legible* act. Bombay mills and Japanese yarn complicated the economics; the politics was still the pyre in the street.
- Peasants in Awadh, Bihar, and parts of Andhra and Gujarat read non-cooperation as a chance to push landlords and forest or revenue officials. The movement’s centre tried to keep peasant anger inside non-violence; it did not always succeed.
- The **Akali** reform current in Sikh shrines ran in the same years in Punjab — parallel, sometimes allied in spirit, not identical in command. Note it; do not collapse it into Congress.

**Prince of Wales visit, 1921.** Boycott of the tour produced clashes, especially in Bombay. The Raj advertised loyalty pageants; the movement advertised empty roads.

## 4. Chauri Chaura, 5 February 1922

In Chauri Chaura, a village-town in the Gorakhpur district of the United Provinces, a procession connected with the movement clashed with police. Protestors attacked the police station. The building was set on fire. Policemen died inside.

Gandhi suspended mass civil disobedience. The **Bardoli resolution** of the Congress Working Committee confirmed the halt. Many younger workers felt betrayed: the government had not fallen, peasants were rising, and the leader had stopped the train because of one station.

**Teaching sentence:** Chauri Chaura is Gandhi choosing **the moral consistency of the method over the military momentum of the moment**.

He was arrested in March 1922 and sentenced in the famous “great trial” at Ahmedabad to six years (released earlier, in 1924, after surgery). The first non-cooperation wave ended as an organisation still standing, a method now known to the illiterate, and a promise of swaraj “within a year” unfulfilled.

## 5. Why the movement mattered even without seizing the Viceregal Lodge

- It nationalised politics beyond presidency cities.
- It made **khadi, hartal, and title-return** readable symbols in a multilingual country.
- It trained a second generation of workers who will run 1930 and 1942.
- It proved Hindu–Muslim joint action was possible at scale — and, by collapsing after 1922–24, proved it was not self-executing.
- It left the 1919 councils working without the main nationalist party, which is why the next debate is council-entry versus council-boycott.

# BLOCK D — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (45 minutes)

## 1. Geography you must be able to draw tonight

1. **Amritsar** — on the upper Bari Doab, rail junction, pilgrim city of the Harmandir Sahib, a few hours from Lahore. Jallianwala Bagh is an enclosed urban plot, not an open maidans of Delhi.
2. **Lahore** — provincial capital of British Punjab; O’Dwyer’s seat; martial-law paperwork.
3. **Canal-colony Punjab** — Lyallpur, Montgomery and neighbouring tracts: wheat, irrigation cuts, soldier settlements. Recruitment yesterday, gag today.
4. **Delhi** — 30 March / early April 1919 firing and hartal; new imperial city watching the old recruiting province explode.
5. **Ahmedabad–Kheda–Bardoli belt** — Gandhi’s Gujarati laboratory; Bardoli later names the halt, and still later a 1928 satyagraha you will meet in another file.
6. **Bombay city** — Khilafat money, mill labour, Prince of Wales clashes, cloth import docks.
7. **Calcutta and Nagpur** — 1920 session cities that rewrote Congress.
8. **Chauri Chaura** — Gorakhpur district, eastern UP, on the fertile Ghaghara-Rapti countryside where peasant density meets small-town police lines.
9. **Anatolia and the Straits** — the foreign map that explains Indian mosque meetings in 1920.
10. **Port towns of hijrat rumour** — Sindh and NW routes toward Afghanistan; a thin but exam-useful arrow.

**Physical fact that explains April 1919:** Amritsar is a tight pre-modern city core. A walled bagh with narrow mouths is a trap once rifles stand at the mouth. Geography here is architecture plus crowd.

## 2. Economy — prices, cloth, and the peasant reading of swaraj

- 1918–21: war-end inflation plus monsoon and influenza aftershocks. Urban real wages hurt; rural net-buyers of grain hurt.
- Rowlatt protest is cheaper to join than a tax revolt: a hartal costs a day’s shop, not a year’s holding.
- Foreign-cloth boycott hits Bombay’s import trade and Lancashire, and also complicates Indian mills that used imported yarn or feared khaki-political disruption.
- Khadi is a **labour-intensive inferior good** in strict market terms and a **superior political good**. Write both sentences in Mains.
- Peasant movements hitch to Non-Cooperation because “non-cooperation” sounds like non-payment to whoever is immediately above you — landlord, taluqdar, forest guard. That translation is the movement’s energy and Gandhi’s fear.
- Titles and law-practices are middle-class capital. Surrendering them is an economic sacrifice that advertises sincerity to the peasant who cannot surrender a knighthood he never had.

## 3. Ethics — GS-IV from this file

1. **Emergency that outlives emergency.** Rowlatt is the template: a state keeps wartime teeth because conspiracy is always imaginable. A republic that extends extraordinary law should have to answer the 1919 question: who consented, for how long, with what review?
2. **Command responsibility.** Dyer’s “moral effect” is the ethic of terror as pedagogy. Hunter’s mildness is institutional self-protection. Tagore’s returned knighthood is the citizen’s refusal to let honour wash blood.
3. **Means and mass.** Gandhi’s halt at Chauri Chaura asserts that a free India built by a burned police station is the wrong country. Critics assert that leadership which cannot bear the crowd’s anger is not leading a revolution. You must be able to steelman both. The syllabus wants the tension, not a saint card.
4. **Coalition honesty.** Using Khilafat to widen the nation is statesmanship if the partner’s grief is real. It is manipulation if the partner is discarded when Turkey changes constitution. 1924 will test the file you start today.
5. **Obedience and unjust law.** Satyagraha assumes the law is public and the penalty is accepted. Martial-law crawling orders assume the body of the subject is a blackboard. Contrast those two uses of the street.

## 4. Current-affairs-explained — how 1919–22 still organises the present

You are not dumping last week’s headlines. You are naming living structures.

- **Extraordinary criminal law.** India still debates statutes that allow detention and special procedure against designated threats. Rowlatt is the ancestor argument: security versus the ordinary court. When a contemporary bill is called “Rowlatt-like,” translate: *peacetime special power, thin legislative consent, fear of revolutionary violence*. Then compare actual text; do not slogan.
- **Crowd, enclosure, fire.** Inquiry commissions after crowd deaths still split along Hunter lines: was force necessary to restore order, or was it exemplary punishment? Jallianwala is the national memory that makes “minimum force” a constitutional expectation, not a courtesy.
- **Boycott as policy.** Swadeshi cloth 1921, later tariff debates, later “vocal for local” language — same family of tools: the household as customs frontier. Ask always who pays (consumer, mill, spinner) and who speaks.
- **Religious world-events inside domestic coalitions.** Khilafat is the classic case of an overseas sacred office driving Indian street politics. Modern analogies (foreign wars, shrines, diaspora campaigns) should be handled with the 1924 lesson: when the overseas object moves, the domestic coalition must have a second common good or it tears.
- **Party organisation.** Nagpur 1920 is why Indian parties still dream of booth-level committees. Mass membership plus a moral programme is the Gandhi template; cadre-plus-election is the later competitor.

# BLOCK E — CSAT practice with worked solutions (40 minutes)

Do these without a calculator. Write every step.

**Q1.** A hartal committee can print 3,600 pamphlets in 8 days if 12 volunteers work 6 hours a day. How many days will 16 volunteers take to print 4,800 pamphlets if they work 5 hours a day, assuming constant per-volunteer productivity?

**Worked solution.**  
First find pamphlet-hours already implied.  
12 volunteers × 8 days × 6 hours = 576 volunteer-hours produce 3,600 pamphlets.  
Output per volunteer-hour = 3600 / 576 = 6.25 pamphlets.  
Need 4,800 pamphlets → 4800 / 6.25 = 768 volunteer-hours.  
16 volunteers × 5 hours/day = 80 volunteer-hours per day.  
Days = 768 / 80 = **9.6 days**.  
If the paper demands a whole-day exam convention, state 9.6; do not illegally round to 10 unless the question says “complete days.”

**Q2.** In a town, 45% of protesters are students. 40% of the remainder are lawyers. The rest are traders. If traders number 990, how many protesters are students?

**Worked solution.**  
Let total = T.  
Students = 0.45T.  
Remainder = 0.55T.  
Lawyers = 0.40 × 0.55T = 0.22T.  
Traders = T − 0.45T − 0.22T = 0.33T.  
0.33T = 990 → T = 990 / 0.33 = 3,000.  
Students = 0.45 × 3000 = **1,350**.

**Q3.** A train from Lahore to Amritsar covers 50 km at 25 km/h, stops 12 minutes, then returns the 50 km at 30 km/h. What is the average speed for the whole journey including the halt?

**Worked solution.**  
Out time = 50/25 = 2 hours.  
Return time = 50/30 = 5/3 hours.  
Halt = 12/60 = 0.2 hours.  
Total time = 2 + 1.666… + 0.2 = 3.866… = 58/15 hours.  
Total distance = 100 km.  
Average speed = 100 ÷ (58/15) = 100 × 15 / 58 = 1500/58 = **750/29 km/h** ≈ 25.86 km/h.  
Do not average 25 and 30. Halt kills the mean.

**Q4.** Statements:  
All satyagrahis are disciplined.  
Some volunteers are satyagrahis.  
Conclusions: (I) Some volunteers are disciplined. (II) All volunteers are disciplined.  
Which follows?

**Worked solution.**  
Some volunteers sit inside the satyagrahi circle; that whole circle sits inside disciplined. So those overlapping volunteers are disciplined. **I follows.**  
II claims the entire volunteer circle is inside disciplined. The “some” does not force that. **II does not follow.**  
Answer: **only I**.

**Q5.** A shopkeeper marks foreign cloth 40% above cost and then gives 20% off in a swadeshi week. Find the profit or loss per cent.

**Worked solution.**  
Let cost = 100.  
Marked = 140.  
Selling = 80% of 140 = 112.  
Profit = 12 on 100 = **12% profit**.  
Memory: successive +40% and −20% is not +20%. Multipliers 1.4 × 0.8 = 1.12.

# BLOCK F — Active recall and model cores (25 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in one or two sentences. Then check the core.

**1. Why did Rowlatt outrage people who were not revolutionaries?**  
*Core:* Because a peacetime statute kept wartime detention habits after Montagu had named responsible government, and because Indian elected members had already refused the bill.

**2. What made Jallianwala Bagh lethally specific as a site?**  
*Core:* A walled enclosure with narrow exits, a festival-plus-meeting crowd, and troops firing from the blocked mouth so dispersal was not a real option.

**3. What did Dyer say he intended, and why is that sentence the ethical key?**  
*Core:* A “moral effect” on the Punjab — punishment as public teaching, not a last-resort clearing of a lane.

**4. What did the Hunter majority do, and what did it not do?**  
*Core:* It criticised duration of fire and humiliation orders; it did not treat the act as a crime needing an Indian court’s verdict on Dyer.

**5. Why was Khilafat an Indian political fact rather than only a Turkish one?**  
*Core:* Indian Muslims treated the Ottoman Caliph as a symbolic head whose humiliation by Britain, after Indian wartime service, was a breach of imperial moral credit.

**6. What did Nagpur 1920 change inside Congress?**  
*Core:* Goal as swaraj, method as non-cooperation, a deeper committee structure, and cheaper membership so the body could claim mass reach.

**7. Name the main boycott items of Non-Cooperation in the order a beginner should write them.**  
*Core:* Titles; councils; courts; government schools; foreign cloth; with tax refusal held as a later stage.

**8. Why did Gandhi stop after Chauri Chaura?**  
*Core:* Because a mass programme he had pledged to keep non-violent had killed policemen in a burned station, and he refused to treat that as an acceptable cost of speed.

**9. Why was the 1920 Hindu–Muslim alliance structurally fragile?**  
*Core:* One clock kept swaraj in India; the other kept a Caliph in Turkey. When Turkey itself ended the office, the shared street lost its foreign hinge.

**10. How does canal-colony Punjab connect yesterday’s war file to today’s massacre file?**  
*Core:* The same irrigated tracts that gave the empire wheat and battalions became the province O’Dwyer and Dyer chose to terrorise into silence.
