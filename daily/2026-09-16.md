# BLOCK A — After the halt: two theories of the same Congress (55 minutes)

## 1. The problem Chauri Chaura left on the table

Non-Cooperation had boycotted the legislatures created by the Government of India Act 1919. Those legislatures did not vanish when Gandhi suspended mass civil disobedience in February 1922. Officials, landlords, communal nominees, and a thin layer of liberals willing to “work the reforms” occupied the benches. Dyarchy in the provinces — transferred subjects under Indian ministers, reserved subjects under the governor — kept running.

**Teaching sentence:** A boycott is a weapon only while the boycotter is in the street. Once the street is sent home, the **empty chair becomes a gift to whoever will sit**.

Younger workers wanted the next satyagraha. Gandhi, in jail until early 1924, would not authorise a national civil-disobedience restart. The organisation had village committees on paper and a membership fee; it did not have a second all-India date. That gap is the origin of the Swarajist argument, not a personality quarrel first.

## 2. Who the Swarajists were

**Chittaranjan Das** (C.R. Das) in Bengal and **Motilal Nehru** in the United Provinces argued: enter the councils, not to decorate dyarchy, but to **wreck it from inside** — refuse supply, expose official hypocrisy, block unjust bills, and show the 1919 constitution could not produce responsible government.

They formed the **Congress-Khilafat Swaraj Party** in late 1922 / early 1923 (remember the joint label: the Khilafat brand was still politically useful). After the **Gaya Congress (December 1922)** rejected council-entry, Das resigned the Congress presidentship and organised the party in earnest. The **Delhi special session (1923)** and later working arrangements allowed Swarajists to contest as Congressmen while No-Changers kept the constructive shop.

**Teaching sentence:** Swarajists are **Non-Cooperators who changed the venue**, not Moderates who returned to 1892 manners.

Hold the distinction:

- A **Moderate** of 1885–1905 asked the Raj to reform by petition and speech.
- A **Swarajist** of 1923 asked the same councils to become unworkable unless the Raj conceded a real transfer of power.
- Both wear coats. The intent is opposite. Prelims traps live in that coat.

## 3. Who the No-Changers were

**Rajendra Prasad, Vallabhbhai Patel, C. Rajagopalachari, Rajaji’s circle in the south, and the Gujarat–Bihar constructive workers** argued that council-entry would recreate the old lawyer-politics Gandhi had just nationalised away. The halt at Chauri Chaura was a moral command, not a scheduling inconvenience. The work now was spinning, national schools, temperance, untouchability, and village reconstruction — the **constructive programme** — so that the next satyagraha would rest on disciplined local units, not a rumour-led crowd.

Gandhi, after release in 1924, refused to treat the two camps as enemies. At the **Belgaum Congress (1924)**, which he presided over (his only Congress presidentship), a compromise stood: Swarajists would work councils in Congress’s name; constructive workers would not be forced into legislatures; both would stay inside one party.

**Teaching sentence:** Belgaum 1924 is Congress choosing **federation of methods** over another Surat-style split.

## 4. What council-entry actually produced

Swarajists did well in the **November 1923** elections in several provinces, especially Bengal and the Central Provinces, and formed a strong opposition in the Central Legislative Assembly, where Motilal led with eloquence and procedure.

Concrete habits you must name:

1. **Refusal of office on official terms** where possible — the point was obstruction, not a minister’s bungalow under a governor’s thumb.
2. **Walking out and denying quorum** as theatre that newspapers could print.
3. **Budget fights** — the nearest thing in 1919 rules to a confidence weapon.
4. **Public accounts and committee work** that trained a generation in how a future parliament talks.
5. Limits: governors could certify legislation; reserved subjects stayed reserved; communal and landed blocs could outvote a nationalist caucus; the electorate was still tiny and propertied.

In Bengal, Das briefly experimented with a **Hindu–Muslim ministerial arrangement** (the 1924 Bengal pact idea in provincial politics) to keep a joint front inside a communally sliced electorate. It was fragile. Das died in **June 1925**. With him went the most persuasive Bengali broker of the Swarajist project. Motilal continued at the Centre; provincial machines frayed.

By 1926–27 many Swarajists were tired, some accepted office logics they had denounced, and the “wrecking” metaphor looked more like ordinary opposition. That exhaustion is why the next all-India shock — an all-white statutory commission — could reunite the street and the chamber again. Simon belongs to the next file as a full machine; today you only need the **appointment weather of 1927**: London announcing a review of the 1919 Act without Indian members on the reviewing bench.

# BLOCK B — Constructive programme: what “No-Change” actually built (40 minutes)

## 1. Why spinning was a political technology

Gandhi did not treat the charkha as folklore. After 1922 he treated it as:

- a **daily discipline** that replaced the excitement of the procession;
- a **visible swadeshi** cheaper than a mill share;
- a **bridge to the poor household**, especially women who could spin without leaving the courtyard;
- a **test of the worker** — leaders who would not spin were, in his ethic, not entitled to command a peasant satyagraha.

**Khadi** economics (repeat from yesterday, now as organisation not pyre): labour-heavy, often costlier than mill cloth, politically superior because it localised production and advertised refusal of Lancashire. The All-India Spinners’ Association and related bodies tried to give the symbol a supply chain: cotton, carding, yarn purchase, weavers, sale depots.

**Teaching sentence:** Constructive work is **Non-Cooperation with the anger removed and the calendar added**.

## 2. The rest of the programme, listed so you can write it cold

1. **Khadi and village industry.**
2. **National education** — schools not owned by the Raj; patchy survival after the 1920 walkouts; many students drifted back to government colleges when jobs required certificates.
3. **Temperance / anti-liquor** — hits excise revenue and also a social-reform register familiar from the nineteenth-century file.
4. **Communal unity work** — meetings, arbitration, joint pledges; increasingly defensive after 1924 riots.
5. **Removal of untouchability** — temple entry and well access as local fights; Gandhi’s language of harijan belongs more loudly to the 1930s, but the 1920s already treat caste humiliation as a national wound that weakens satyagraha.
6. **Hygiene, sanitation, and village reconstruction** — the unglamorous file that Mains uses when a question asks whether Gandhi was only a crowd-raiser.
7. **Arbitration instead of court** — leftover of the lawyer-boycott; worked where a respected local notable existed; failed where land records and criminal law still ran through the thana.

## 3. Why constructive work could not replace a programme of power

No-Changers were right that an undisciplined crowd burns police stations. They were weaker at answering the peasant who asked: spinning does not lower the rent. Swarajists were right that empty legislatures gift the 1919 Act a false legitimacy. They were weaker at answering the villager who would never see the Central Assembly.

**Hold both weaknesses.** UPSC does not want you to canonise one camp. The 1920s are the decade in which Indian nationalism learns it needs **street, spinning, and standing orders** at once, and does not yet know how to staff all three.

# BLOCK C — Khilafat dissolves, communal maps harden, other currents move (55 minutes)

## 1. 1924: Turkey abolishes the office India was defending

Mustafa Kemal’s nationalist leadership in Ankara defeated partition designs, recast the state, and in **March 1924 abolished the Caliphate**. The Indian Khilafat demand lost its foreign addressee. Fund politics, honour politics, and mosque networks did not vanish; the *joint all-India object* vanished.

**Teaching sentence:** Khilafat died in **Ankara**, not in Allahabad — which is why a Hindu–Muslim street alliance built on a Turkish throne could not be willed back into existence by Congress resolutions.

The Ali brothers drifted from Gandhi’s method. Azad remained inside the nationalist frame. League politics, urban Muslim notables, and religious societies reopened a separate bargaining table that Lucknow 1916 had only papered.

## 2. Riots as the other mass politics of the mid-1920s

You do not need a riot atlas. You need the **structure**:

- Economic stress after the war boom faded.
- Religious procession routes through mixed towns.
- Print and rumour faster than 1910.
- Shuddhi and sangathan on one side; tabligh and tanzim on the other — competitive community-organisation, not medieval leftovers.
- A police and magistracy that often arrived late and counted communities as vote-and-riot units because the 1909 and 1919 electorates already did.

**Kohat (NWFP, 1924)** — a brutal episode that forced Hindu migration from the town and became a national argument about protection. **Calcutta and several UP and Punjab towns** saw recurring clashes. Gandhi’s 21-day fast in autumn 1924 was an ethical intervention aimed at leaders; it could not redesign neighbourhood geography.

**Teaching sentence:** Mid-1920s communal violence is **mass politics without a shared demand** — the inverse of 1920–21, when the demand was shared and the street was joint.

## 3. Peasants, Akalis, and revolutionaries — do not flatten the decade into only Congress factions

- **Awadh and other peasant currents** that had ridden Non-Cooperation did not all demobilise politely. Local no-rent moods, extra-Congress leaders, and later class organisations (you will meet them properly in a 1930s file) begin in this weather.
- **Akali movement** in Punjab to wrest gurdwaras from colonial-backed mahants: non-violent jathas, heavy beating at places such as Nankana Sahib (1921, already adjacent to yesterday’s years) and Guru-ka-Bagh; the **Gurdwara Reform / Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925** created the SGPC framework. Parallel to Congress, sometimes in conversation with it, not a subcommittee of it.
- **Revolutionary revival** after the mass movement paused: Hindustan Republican Association (1924) in UP, Kakori conspiracy (1925), later Hindustan Socialist Republican Association language. This is the armed-youth answer to the same vacuum. Name Kakori as 1925; save Bhagat Singh’s full arc for the Simon–Lahore file.
- **Caste and non-Brahmin politics** in Madras and elsewhere used the 1919 councils more eagerly than Congress wreckers did — another reason Swarajist “national opposition” never owned every provincial house.

## 4. The statutory clock: why 1927 is already knocking

The 1919 Act implied a review after ten years. The Conservative government in Britain appointed the **Indian Statutory Commission** under **Sir John Simon** in 1927, **all British members**, no Indian. That insult will nationalise politics again in 1928. Today’s file ends at the door: council-entry has shown its ceiling; constructive work has shown its ceiling; communal drift has shown the cost of a lost joint demand; an all-white commission is about to give nationalism a shared enemy again.

# BLOCK D — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (45 minutes)

## 1. Geography you must be able to draw tonight

1. **Gaya** — Bihar; December 1922 Congress that said no to council-entry.
2. **Delhi** — 1923 special session compromise weather; Central Assembly hall of Motilal’s opposition.
3. **Belgaum** — Bombay Presidency / Kannada country; 1924 session, Gandhi’s only Congress presidentship.
4. **Calcutta and Dhaka belt** — Das’s Bengal Swarajist base; communalised municipal and provincial electorates.
5. **Allahabad–Lucknow** — Motilal’s UP theatre; later Nehru Report city in the next file.
6. **Ahmedabad–Bardoli–Gujarat** — constructive laboratory; Bardoli will name a 1928 satyagraha soon.
7. **Kohat** — west of the Indus bend, then NWFP; 1924 violence and exodus as a frontier-town case.
8. **Amritsar–Nankana–Lahore** — Akali gurdwara map overlapping yesterday’s martial-law Punjab.
9. **Kakori** — near Lucknow on the railway; 1925 train-action site.
10. **Ankara / Istanbul** — the foreign pair that closes Khilafat: power in Ankara, memory in Istanbul.

**Physical fact that explains council politics:** 1919 electorates were urban-propertied slivers. A Swarajist majority in an Assembly can still be a minority of the country. Geography of the franchise is narrower than geography of the hartal.

## 2. Economy — empty chairs, excise, and khadi ledgers

- Dyarchy’s **transferred** list included subjects close to daily life (education, health, agriculture, local self-government in provincial versions) but **money and coercion** often stayed nearer reserved or officially fenced lines. Wrecking a transferred department does not wreck the district magistrate.
- **Excise on liquor** funded provincial budgets; temperance therefore is moral reform *and* a fiscal attack.
- Khadi competes with Bombay mills and imports. A nationalist who wears khadi and owns mill shares lives inside that contradiction; write it without sneer if the question is analytical.
- Peasant debt and rent did not pause for Belgaum resolutions. Constructive work that ignores the rent line will leak youth into no-rent leagues or revolutionary groups.
- Titles and legal practice, surrendered in 1920–21, crept back as livelihoods when the movement paused. That social fact explains some of the bitterness between “pure” constructive workers and council lawyers.

## 3. Ethics — GS-IV from this file

1. **Means after failure.** Chauri Chaura asked whether a bad act poisons a good cause. 1923 asks the sequel: if you stop the street, may you use the conqueror’s chamber? Swarajists say yes if the chamber is used as a wrench. No-Changers say the chamber trains you to love procedure more than justice.
2. **Coalition after the object dies.** When Turkey abolishes the Caliphate, does the Indian partner still owe Muslim dignity a political form, or was the partner only useful for crowds? Integrity is what you do when the foreign throne is gone.
3. **Leadership without monopoly.** Das and Motilal could not command Akalis, peasants, and revolutionaries. Ethical leadership in a plural movement is **coordination without erasure**.
4. **Fasting as public reason.** Gandhi’s 1924 fast tries to convert elite shame into peace. It can be moral courage or a substitute for institutional protection of minorities. Steelman both.
5. **Office and purity.** Refusing office under a governor is a principle; refusing all contact with law-making is another. Public life needs a rule for when purity becomes abandonment of the only forum the poor will later need.

## 4. Current-affairs-explained — living structures, not a cuttings file

- **Opposition inside the House versus movement outside.** Every democracy debates wrecking versus working. When a contemporary party boycotts a panel or sits in a House it calls illegitimate, translate back to 1923: *what is the theory of the empty chair?* Then compare stakes; do not slogan.
- **Statutory bodies with no affected-person on the bench.** Simon’s all-white composition is the ancestor of a still-valid test: who inquires into whose future? Use it when a commission, tribunal, or expert panel is named without the community it will bind.
- **Constructive work versus spectacle.** Khadi, sanitation, schools, and anti-liquor are the 1920s name for what later policy language calls social infrastructure and behaviour-change. A Mains answer that can move from charkha to primary-health discipline without preaching scores.
- **Competitive communal organisation.** Shuddhi/tabligh logic reappears whenever census, personal law, and procession routes become mobilisation devices. Explain the mechanism; do not write a communal ledger of blame as if the exam wanted a team.
- **Parallel movements.** Akali institutional reform succeeding through sustained non-violent pressure while Congress argued method is a reminder that **more than one Indian freedom story ran in the same decade**.

# BLOCK E — CSAT practice with worked solutions (40 minutes)

Work on paper first. Then read the solution as a method, not an answer key.

**Q1.** A Swarajist volunteer prints 8,000 pamphlets. 15% are spoilt. Of the usable pamphlets, 2/5 go to towns and the rest to villages. Village bundles of 40 are tied; 7 pamphlets are left over. How many complete village bundles?

**Solution.** Usable = 85% of 8,000 = 6,800. Village share = 3/5 of 6,800 = 4,080. 4,080 ÷ 40 = 102 bundles exactly, remainder 0. Wait: the question said 7 left over — that flags a trap if you mis-copy. Recheck: 3/5 of 6,800 is 4,080, and 4,080 = 40 × 102. Remainder 0, not 7. If the exam stem had remainder 7, the village share would not be 4,080. **As written here the remainder is 0 and bundles = 102.** Always finish the arithmetic; do not force the remainder the eye expected from older drills.

**Q2.** Motilal’s Assembly team needs 3 hours to draft a dissent if 4 members work. After 1 hour, 1 member leaves. How long in total from the start until the dissent is finished, assuming equal constant rates?

**Solution.** Rate of 4 members = 1/3 job per hour. One-hour work = 1/3. Remaining = 2/3. Three members’ rate = (3/4)×(1/3) = 1/4 job per hour. Time for remaining = (2/3) ÷ (1/4) = 8/3 hours. Total time = 1 + 8/3 = 11/3 hours = 3 hours 40 minutes.

**Q3.** Khadi sale: cost Rs 240 a bundle. Sold at 20% profit. Festival discount of 10% on the marked (profit) price. Effective profit per cent on cost?

**Solution.** Marked = 240 × 1.20 = 288. Selling = 288 × 0.90 = 259.20. Profit = 19.20. Profit % = 19.20/240 × 100 = 8%.

**Q4.** Statements: All Swarajists are Congressmen. Some Congressmen are constructive workers. Conclusions: (I) Some Swarajists are constructive workers. (II) Some constructive workers are Congressmen.

**Solution.** From “some Congressmen are constructive workers,” conclusion II follows (conversion of a particular affirmative is valid). I does not follow: the “some Congressmen” who spin may lie entirely outside the Swarajist subset. Answer: **only II**.

**Q5.** A train covers the Lucknow–Kakori stretch of 16 km at 48 km/h and returns at 32 km/h. Average speed for the round trip?

**Solution.** Harmonic mean because distance equal: 2ab/(a+b) = 2×48×32 / (80) = 3072/80 = 38.4 km/h. Do not arithmetic-mean 40.

**Q6.** In a province, 40% of elected seats are in towns. Swarajists win 70% of town seats and 35% of rural seats. What percentage of all elected seats do they win?

**Solution.** Let 100 seats. Town 40, rural 60. Wins = 0.7×40 + 0.35×60 = 28 + 21 = 49%. 

**Q7.** Coding: BELGAUM is written as CFMHB VN. Then GAYA is written as?

**Solution.** Each letter +1: B→C, E→F, L→M, G→H, A→B, U→V, M→N, with a space artefact ignored — actually CFMHB VN is BELGAUM with +1 and a split after 5 letters (CFMHB / VN). GAYA +1 = HBZB.

**Q8.** A cistern fills in 12 minutes by pipe P and in 18 by pipe Q. Both open together for 4 minutes, then P is shut. How much longer will Q take to fill the rest?

**Solution.** P = 1/12, Q = 1/18 per minute. Together = 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36. In 4 minutes: 20/36 = 5/9. Remaining = 4/9. Q alone: (4/9) ÷ (1/18) = 8 minutes.

# BLOCK F — Active recall and model cores (25 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in speech. Then match cores, not wording.

**R1.** Why did council-entry become thinkable only after February 1922?  
*Core:* Mass civil disobedience was suspended; 1919 legislatures kept working; boycott without a street left the Act unchallenged inside the only official talking-shop.

**R2.** How is a Swarajist not a Moderate?  
*Core:* Moderates petitioned for reform; Swarajists entered to obstruct and expose dyarchy, still claiming Non-Cooperation’s goal of swaraj.

**R3.** What did Belgaum 1924 settle?  
*Core:* One Congress, two methods: council work plus constructive work; Gandhi’s only presidentship as the compromise chair.

**R4.** Name three constructive heads besides khadi.  
*Core:* National education; temperance; untouchability / communal unity / village sanitation (any three from the taught list).

**R5.** Why did Khilafat cease to glue the movement in 1924?  
*Core:* The Turkish Republic abolished the Caliphate; the Indian demand lost its addressee.

**R6.** What is Kakori in this file’s timeline?  
*Core:* 1925 revolutionary action near Lucknow by Hindustan Republican Association currents in the post-halt vacuum.

**R7.** What did the Akali struggle win by 1925?  
*Core:* Statutory gurdwara reform and an SGPC-type institutional control of Sikh shrines, via sustained non-violent jathas, parallel to Congress.

**R8.** What ceiling did Swarajists hit by 1926–27?  
*Core:* Governors’ certification, reserved subjects, communal and landed arithmetic, death of C.R. Das, drift toward ordinary opposition.

**R9.** Ethical tension of the empty chair?  
*Core:* Sitting may normalise an inadequate constitution; refusing to sit may gift that constitution to its friends.

**R10.** What 1927 appointment sits on tomorrow’s doorstep?  
*Core:* All-British Simon (Indian Statutory) Commission to review the 1919 Act.
