# BLOCK A — Why an all-white commission walked into India (50 minutes)

## 1. The statutory clock inside the 1919 Act

The Government of India Act 1919 was sold as a ten-year experiment. Dyarchy in the provinces, a Central Legislative Assembly with a limited elected majority, a Council of State, and a promise that Parliament in London would look again. A review after about a decade was therefore not a surprise. The surprise was **who** would review.

**Teaching sentence:** Indians expected to sit on the bench that judged their fitness for more power. London appointed only British members. The quarrel is about **authorship of the next constitution**, not about whether a review should exist.

The Conservative ministry under Stanley Baldwin named the **Indian Statutory Commission** in **November 1927**. Chairman: **Sir John Simon**, a Liberal lawyer-politician. Members: seven British parliamentarians in all (Commons and Lords mix). Zero Indian. Official reason offered then: the Commission was a **committee of the British Parliament**, so only parliamentarians of that Parliament could sit. Indians heard a different sentence: you may give evidence; you may not write the report.

Hold the official reason without swallowing it. A Parliament that claimed to legislate for India could have added Indian assessors with voice, or invited a joint committee. It chose purity of its own membership. That choice is the political fact.

## 2. Why the composition detonated every camp at once

- **Congress** had spent 1922–27 arguing venue. An all-white bench gave both Swarajists and No-Changers a shared slogan: **Simon Go Back**.
- **Liberals** who had worked the 1919 councils felt insulted in the one currency they lived on — being treated as constitutional adults.
- **Muslim League** factions split: some wanted to use Simon to bargain separate electorates and weightage; others joined the boycott so as not to look like the Raj’s auxiliary.
- **Justice Party** in Madras and some non-Brahmin, landlord, and princely interests saw a chance to speak over Congress’s head. Boycott was never unanimous in every province. Prelims loves that nuance.
- **Princes** watched a commission that might one day touch paramountcy and federation. Their evidence and later Round Table logic belong partly here, fully in the next constitutional file.

**Teaching sentence:** Simon unified Indian politics **negatively** — against exclusion — without first unifying it on the **positive text** of a future constitution. That gap is why 1928–29 produces both the Nehru Report and the Fourteen Points.

## 3. Arrival, hartal, and the lathi

The Commission landed at Bombay in **February 1928** and toured. Wherever it went, black flags, empty official receptions in nationalist towns, and the chant **Simon Go Back**. Hartals were not 1920-scale everywhere; they were loud enough to make the tour a running newsreel of refusal.

**Lahore, 30 October 1928.** A protest against Simon. Police assault. **Lala Lajpat Rai**, leading the demonstration, was beaten. He died on **17 November 1928**. Nationalists named the blows as the cause; officialdom spoke of other illness. Politically the death functioned as martyrdom. The revolutionary current that had been rebuilding since Kakori now had a public wound in Punjab.

**Teaching sentence:** Lajpat Rai’s death turns a constitutional boycott into a **blood argument**, which is why Bhagat Singh’s group moves from pamphlet to assassination politics in December 1928. Name **Saunders** (the police officer they held responsible) as December 1928; keep the full HSRA–Lahore Conspiracy–Assembly bomb arc for the next file so today’s constitutional machine stays sharp.

## 4. What Simon actually reported (you need the skeleton, not the volumes)

The Commission took evidence, including from groups that did not boycott. Its report (1930, two volumes) is often skipped in coaching lists; you should still own four conclusions because they feed the 1935 Act:

1. **Dyarchy had failed** as a working division of provincial government. Responsible government in the provinces should replace it.
2. **Centre should remain strong and less responsible** for now — defence, foreign affairs, and stability arguments.
3. **Communal representation** was treated as a stubborn fact to be managed, not wished away.
4. A **federation** including princely states was floated as a future architecture.

**Teaching sentence:** Simon is boycotted as a **process** and still becomes a **source-text** for later British drafting. Boycott changes politics; it does not always erase the report’s influence on the next statute.

# BLOCK B — Indians try to write the constitution themselves: Nehru Report (55 minutes)

## 1. Why a committee in 1928

Boycott creates a vacuum: if you will not talk to Simon, you must show you can draft. An **All Parties Conference** (Delhi and later Lucknow/Allahabad weather of 1928) asked a committee to produce a constitution. Chairman: **Motilal Nehru**. Members included among others **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **M.S. Aney**, **Mangal Singh**, **Ali Imam**, **Shuab Qureshi**, and **Subhas Chandra Bose** in the working orbit of the document’s politics. **Jawaharlal** was not the author; do not confuse father and son. The popular name **Nehru Report, August 1928** means Motilal’s committee.

**Teaching sentence:** The Nehru Report is the first fullish **Indian attempt at a dominion constitution** agreed across several (not all) parties — a draft to prove fitness, and a bargain to keep Muslims, Sikhs, Liberals, and Congress in one text.

## 2. What the Report actually said — learn this as a scheme

Read it as ten bolts. If you can unscrew them in order, you can write both Prelims and a 10-mark.

1. **Dominion Status** for India — same family as Canada/Australia in the late-1920s usage: internal self-government inside the Empire, not yet the word *purna swaraj* as official Congress creed. That creed comes at **Lahore, December 1929**. The Report is therefore **one step short** of the next Congress leap.
2. **Parliamentary government** at the Centre and in provinces — responsible executives, not dyarchy.
3. **A bill of rights** — freedoms of speech, conscience, and equality language that later constitutional culture will recognise as ancestors, not as Article numbers of 1950.
4. **Secular civic framing** — joint electorates as the default democratic machine.
5. **Joint electorates** with **reserved seats for Muslims** where they were a minority, rather than separate electorates as a permanent principle. This is the bolt that breaks the old Lucknow 1916 bargain.
6. **Residuary powers** with the **Centre** — a strong-centre federal sketch.
7. **Linguistic provinces** as a principle of future map-making, not a finished atlas.
8. **No separate electorate for Muslims at the Centre** in the sense the League wanted as a shield; weightage and one-third central Muslim share were the bargaining zone that failed.
9. **Sikh and other minority** protections attempted by reservation and rights language; Sikh politics in Punjab did not all sign in comfort.
10. **Princely states** to join a federation on terms — an unfinished hinge.

**Teaching sentence:** Nehru Report trades **separate electorates** for **joint electorates plus seats and rights**. That trade is its originality and its wound.

## 3. Why many Muslim leaders said no

Lucknow 1916 had promised **separate electorates** and **weightage** in Hindu-majority provinces. The Report asked Muslims to trust joint electorates in a country where they were about a quarter of the population, in return for reservations and a rights chapter.

Objections, stated fairly:

- Joint electorates plus Hindu-majority demography could elect “Muslim” faces who owed the majority more than the minority.
- Residuary powers at the Centre looked dangerous if the Centre would be Hindu-majority in a joint roll.
- One-third Muslim representation at the Centre, and reservation in Punjab and Bengal in ways that protected Muslim majorities there, were not secured to League satisfaction.
- Sind separation from Bombay, and other provincial map demands, were not locked in as League wanted.

**Jinnah**, who had once been the Lucknow broker, moved from trying to amend the Report to writing a counter-charter.

Hindu Mahasabha and some Sikh voices also attacked the Report from other flanks (too many concessions / not enough Punjab safeguards). An All Parties text that pleases every flank is a myth; the Report is a **Congress-Liberal centre of gravity**, not a unanimous subcontinent.

## 4. Calcutta Congress 1928 and the dominion-versus-independence fork

At **Calcutta (December 1928)** younger leaders, **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Bose** loud among them, wanted **complete independence** now, not dominion status as a ceiling. Gandhi brokered a pause: accept the Report for the moment; if it is not accepted by the British by **31 December 1929**, Congress will demand independence and consider a new struggle.

**Teaching sentence:** Calcutta 1928 puts an **expiry date** on dominion language. Lahore 1929 will cash that date. Simon plus Nehru Report plus that ultimatum is one chain, not three stories.

# BLOCK C — Jinnah’s Fourteen Points and the other 1928–29 streets (50 minutes)

## 1. The Fourteen Points as a constitutional checklist (March 1929)

Do not memorise them as a poem. Group them into **five baskets** so you can rebuild any list in the hall.

**Basket 1 — Federal shape**
- Federal constitution; residuary powers with the **provinces** (opposite of Nehru Report).
- Provinces to have uniform measure of autonomy.

**Basket 2 — Centre and numbers**
- Adequate Muslim representation in legislatures; commonly remembered demand of **one-third** seats in the Central legislature.
- No territorial change (Sind, Baluchistan, NWFP status, Punjab–Bengal majorities) that reduces Muslim weight without consent.

**Basket 3 — Electorates and cabinets**
- **Separate electorates** to continue.
- Muslim share in services and cabinets; no cabinet without Muslim presence of a meaningful kind.

**Basket 4 — Culture and law**
- Protection of Muslim culture, language, religion, personal law, and charitable institutions.
- Sind to be a separate province; reforms in NWFP and Baluchistan.

**Basket 5 — Amendment lock**
- No constitutional change affecting Muslims without their consent — a **veto logic**, not only a seat arithmetic.

**Teaching sentence:** Fourteen Points are a **minority-federal charter**: weak Centre, provincial residuary power, separate electorates, demographic locks in the west and east, cultural autonomy, and an amendment veto.

Compare cold:

| Bolt | Nehru Report 1928 | Fourteen Points 1929 |
|---|---|---|
| Status | Dominion constitution drafted by Indians | Conditions for Muslim acceptance of any constitution |
| Electorates | Joint, with reservations | Separate |
| Residuary power | Centre | Provinces |
| Muslim central share | Contested; not locked as League wanted | One-third demand |
| Map | Linguistic principle | Sind, NWFP, Baluchistan, majority-province shields |
| Amendment | Ordinary constitutional design | Muslim consent as lock |

If a question asks “why 1940 Pakistan Resolution is not a bolt from the blue”, you may later show a line from separate electorates (1909) → Lucknow weightage (1916) → rejection of joint-electorate Report (1928) → Points (1929) → later sovereign-state language. Today you stop at 1929 and refuse teleology as destiny; it is a **path of failed bargains**.

## 2. Bardoli 1928 — the constructive wing’s proof of life

While Simon toured and Motilal drafted, **Vallabhbhai Patel** led a **no-tax satyagraha in Bardoli taluka (Gujarat)** against a revenue hike after a revision settlement. Peasants, including women organisers, held; government climbed down in significant measure; Patel earned the title **Sardar** in this struggle’s fame.

**Teaching sentence:** Bardoli shows that **constructive-No-Change Gujarat** could still run a disciplined local satyagraha while the all-India constitutional argument raged. It is the living answer to “did Gandhi’s village programme do any power work after 1922?”

Link the geography: Surat belt, rich garden and cotton-sugarcane country, revenue records tight enough that a hike is felt as a ledger shock, not a metaphor.

## 3. Revolutionaries in the same months

- **HSRA** language replaces HRA: socialism enters the pamphlet.
- **Saunders assassination, Lahore, December 1928** — revenge frame after Lajpat Rai.
- **Central Assembly bomb, 8 April 1929** — Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt: smoke and noise to be heard, not a massacre design; leaflets; arrest as theatre.
- Hunger-strike on jail equality (political prisoner status) will define 1929 headlines; **Jatin Das** dies in September 1929 after a long fast. Remember the ethic: colonial jail classified Indian politicals below European rights.

These acts are not a side comic. They compete with Motilal’s draft for the imagination of youth. A Mains paragraph that can hold **draft constitution + no-tax taluka + bomb in the Assembly** as three answers to the same exclusion is a mature paragraph.

# BLOCK D — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (45 minutes)

## 1. Geography you must be able to draw tonight

1. **Bombay harbour** — Simon’s February 1928 landing; first black-flag theatre.
2. **Lahore** — October 1928 lathi charge; Lajpat Rai; Saunders; later 1929 Congress of purna swaraj (next file’s centrepiece, but the city is already tonight’s wound-map).
3. **Delhi–Lucknow–Allahabad** — All Parties / Motilal drafting geography; Allahabad as Nehru household and committee weather.
4. **Calcutta** — December 1928 Congress; dominion ultimatum to 31 December 1929.
5. **Bardoli** — south Gujarat, Tapi–Surat hinterland; 1928 no-tax victory.
6. **Sind** — still a Bombay Presidency division in 1928; League demand to separate it is a **map demand with communal arithmetic**, not only a linguistic one.
7. **NWFP and Baluchistan** — frontier units with thin constitutional furniture; Points demand they be brought into the provincial reform map.
8. **Punjab and Bengal** — the two giant provinces where **Muslims were numerous enough that “majority province” shields** matter; joint versus separate electorate fights are fiercest here.
9. **Madras Presidency** — Justice Party and non-Brahmin council politics as the reminder that Simon-boycott was not every public man’s choice.
10. **London** — the only place that could legally convert any Indian draft into a statute in 1928. That is why a perfect Indian report could still be ignored.

**Physical fact that explains the electorate fight:** Muslim population was not evenly spread. A joint roll in a Hindu-majority district and a joint roll in western Punjab are different machines. Constitutional geography is **uneven density**, not a single percentage applied to every acre.

## 2. Economy — revenue hike, mill cloth, and who can vote

- Bardoli is a **settlement-revision** story: colonial land revenue claimed scientific survey; peasants experienced a political price shock after war-and-twenties price swings. No-tax is fiscal disobedience with village bookkeeping.
- 1919 franchise remained **property-and-tax thin**. Joint versus separate electorate is a fight inside a small gated stadium, while Bardoli and hartal speak a wider rural language. Write that tension when a question sounds only legal.
- Khadi and mill cloth still compete. A dominion-status India would still have tariff and sterling questions; Simon’s economic chapters matter less to Prelims than the political insult, but Mains can use “who sets cotton duties” as the hidden stake of authorship.
- Public employment shares in the Fourteen Points are **livelihood politics**. Separate electorates without services and cabinets would be theatre without payroll.

## 3. Ethics — GS-IV from this file

1. **Who may inquire into whom.** An all-male, all-white, all-metropolitan bench judging a continent’s fitness is the classic **exclusion from the tribunal**. Modern public service: if a committee will bind a community, must that community sit on it? Steelman the “parliamentary privilege of the appointing House” argument too, then show why the ruled did not accept it.
2. **Drafting as non-violence.** Motilal’s committee is politics with a pen after politics with a hartal. Ethic: proving competence rather than only proving anger.
3. **Majority rule versus minority veto.** Joint electorates honour one-person-one-vote. Separate electorates and amendment locks honour the fear of permanent cultural defeat. Integrity is stating both goods without pretending they never clash.
4. **Means of the bomb and the leaflet.** Assembly bomb as “to be heard” still uses fear. Contrast Bardoli’s disciplined refusal to pay. Do not romanticise either; name the civilian risk and the colonial jail hierarchy that fed the hunger-strike.
5. **Expiry dates on compromise.** Calcutta’s 31 December 1929 clock is ethical time-management: compromise without a sunset becomes surrender.

## 4. Current-affairs-explained — living structures

- **Expert commissions with no affected nominee.** Whenever a contemporary statutory panel on a region, caste, or resource seats only outsiders, translate: *Simon problem or legitimate external audit?* Use the test, do not paste the slogan.
- **Whose draft counts.** Nehru Report is the ancestor of “we will send our own constitution, not only objections.” When states or groups publish alternative bills, ask whether the official process has a duty to answer clause-by-clause.
- **Federal residuary power.** India after 1950 put residuary power with the Union (with later debate). The 1928–29 clash is the family quarrel behind that choice. Use it in a federalism Mains answer as history of distrust, not as trivia.
- **Separate versus joint rolls.** Any live debate on delimited seats, women’s reservation rotating by draw, or minority nomination is a distant cousin. Explain mechanism: does the voter list mix communities or split them?
- **Local tax revolt with accounts.** Bardoli is the template for a lawful-looking civic refusal that still breaks an unjust assessment. Contemporary farmer or municipal tax protests should be compared on **discipline, records, and violence**, not on flag colours.

# BLOCK E — CSAT practice with worked solutions (40 minutes)

Work on paper first. Then read the solution as a method, not an answer key.

**Q1.** A boycott committee prints 12,000 badges. 8% are defective. Of the good badges, 3/5 go to cities. The rest are packed in village kits of 36. How many full village kits, and how many badges remain?

**Solution.** Good badges = 92% of 12,000 = 11,040. Village share = 2/5 × 11,040 = 4,416. 4,416 ÷ 36 = 122 kits exactly, remainder 0. Full kits = 122; left over = 0.

**Q2.** Four drafters finish the rights chapter in 10 days. After 4 days, one drafter leaves. How many more days do the remaining three need, equal constant rates?

**Solution.** Combined rate = 1/10 per day. Four-day work = 4/10 = 2/5. Remaining = 3/5. Three drafters’ rate = (3/4)×(1/10) = 3/40 per day. Time = (3/5) ÷ (3/40) = (3/5)×(40/3) = 8 days more. Total duration from start = 4 + 8 = 12 days if asked.

**Q3.** Simon tour covers 960 km in two parts. First part at 32 km/h; second at 48 km/h. Total moving time 24 hours. Find the two distances.

**Solution.** Let first distance = \(d\). Then \(d/32 + (960-d)/48 = 24\). Multiply by 96: \(3d + 2(960-d) = 2304\). \(3d + 1920 - 2d = 2304\). \(d = 384\). Second = 576 km. Check: 384/32 = 12 h; 576/48 = 12 h.

**Q4.** In a province, 55% of electors are in joint-roll experiment booths. Of those, 40% are women. Women in those booths are what percent of all electors?

**Solution.** 40% of 55% = 0.4 × 55 = 22% of all electors.

**Q5.** Statement-conclusion.

Statements: All dominion drafts are constitutions. Some constitutions ignore separate electorates.
Conclusions: (I) Some dominion drafts ignore separate electorates. (II) All constitutions are dominion drafts.

**Solution.** (I) does not follow: the “some constitutions” that ignore separate electorates may lie outside the dominion-draft circle. (II) reverses a universal and fails. Neither follows.

**Q6.** A revenue hike is 22%. After protest it is rolled back to a 10% hike on the old base. If old assessment was Rs 2,500, what does the peasant pay after rollback, and what extra was feared?

**Solution.** Feared new = 2,500 × 1.22 = 3,050; extra feared = 550. After rollback = 2,500 × 1.10 = 2,750; extra now = 250. Relief versus feared bill = 300.

**Q7.** Coding rebuilt as alternate −1/+1 from the left. SIMON → RJLPM. Code NEHRU.

**Solution.** N−1=M, E+1=F, H−1=G, R+1=S, U−1=T → **MFGST**.

**Q8.** Syllogism.

Statements: All boycotts are protests. Some protests are drafts. No draft is a lathi charge.
Conclusions: (I) Some boycotts are drafts. (II) No boycott is a lathi charge. (III) Some protests are not lathi charges.

**Solution.** (I) does not follow. (II) does not follow. (III) follows: the protests that are drafts cannot be lathi charges. Answer: only III.

**Q9.** A committee of 5 is formed from 8 lawyers and 6 peasants. How many committees have at least 2 peasants?

**Solution.** Total = C(14,5) = 2002. Zero peasant = C(8,5) = 56. One peasant = C(8,4)×C(6,1) = 70×6 = 420. At least 2 = 2002 − 56 − 420 = **1526**.

**Q10.** Clock: at what time between 3 and 4 are the hands 4 minute-marks apart?

**Solution.** Four minute-marks = 24°. |6m − (90 + 0.5m)| = 24. Cases: 5.5m = 114 → m = 228/11; and 5.5m = 66 → m = 12. Times: **3:12** and **3:228/11**.

# BLOCK F — Active recall and model cores (25 minutes)

Close the notes. Answer in speech. Then match cores.

**R1.** Why did an all-British Simon Commission offend even many who liked the 1919 Act?
**Core:** Because the review would write the next constitution, and Indians were invited only as witnesses, not authors. The “Parliament-members-only” reason did not answer the demand for shared authorship.

**R2.** Name the city, month, and political meaning of Lala Lajpat Rai’s fatal injury.
**Core:** Lahore, October 1928 protest against Simon; died November 1928; turned boycott into martyrdom and fed the Saunders assassination weather of December 1928.

**R3.** Who wrote the Nehru Report, what status did it demand, and what did it do to separate electorates?
**Core:** Motilal Nehru’s All Parties committee, 1928; dominion status with parliamentary responsible government; joint electorates with reserved seats instead of separate electorates as the organising principle.

**R4.** Give three structural contrasts between the Nehru Report and Jinnah’s Fourteen Points.
**Core:** Joint vs separate electorates; residuary power Centre vs provinces; no Muslim amendment-lock vs consent-lock / one-third central share and majority-province map shields.

**R5.** What did Calcutta Congress 1928 decide about time?
**Core:** Work with the Report for now; if Britain does not accept the dominion framework by 31 December 1929, Congress will go to complete independence.

**R6.** What was Bardoli 1928, and why does it belong in a Simon year file?
**Core:** Patel-led no-tax satyagraha against a revenue revision in a Gujarat taluka that won a climb-down; proof that constructive-province machinery could still coerce the Raj locally while the all-India fight was about a commission and a draft.

**R7.** One Simon Report idea that later statutes used.
**Core:** Dyarchy had failed; provincial responsible government plus a still-strong Centre and a possible princes-plus-provinces federation.

**R8.** Why is “Swarajist = Moderate” still a wrong sentence in 1928?
**Core:** Swarajists had wanted to wreck dyarchy from inside; in 1928 many of them boycotted Simon and Motilal drafted a replacement constitution. Moderates of 1885 asked; these men were bargaining a dominion text and an ultimatum.
