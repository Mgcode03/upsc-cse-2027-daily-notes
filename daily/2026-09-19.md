# BLOCK A — Second Round Table Conference, 1931 (50 minutes)

## 1. Why Gandhi went alone

The Pact had two process clauses: Civil Disobedience pauses; Congress sits at the next London table. Congress chose **Gandhi as its sole official delegate**. That is not vanity theatre. It is a claim: the mass movement speaks with one mouth, so the British cannot shop among “moderate Congressmen” while the street waits outside.

He did not go as a private saint. He went as the authorised bargainer of a party that had just filled the jails. Other Indians sat too — princes, Muslim League voices, Liberal and Justice Party figures, **B.R. Ambedkar** for Depressed Classes, Sikh, Indian Christian, Anglo-Indian, and European commercial seats. **Teaching sentence:** Second RTC is not “Gandhi versus Britain”. It is Gandhi as one chair in a room already carved into **community and princely chairs**.

Session window to lock: **September–December 1931** (opened 7 September 1931). Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald**’s Labour government was already sliding into the **National Government** of the slump year; British room to concede was smaller than Irwin’s Delhi mood in March.

## 2. What was on the table

Three files sat together. Do not melt them into one word “constitution”.

1. **Federation.** An all-India federation of British Indian provinces plus princely states. Princes wanted entry on terms that protected their internal autocracy. Congress wanted a federation that did not freeze the people of the states as subjects of unaccountable courts.
2. **Centre and safeguards.** Defence, foreign affairs, and a block of “special responsibilities” for the Viceroy were the British floor. Responsible government at the Centre, if spoken at all, came with so many locks that it looked like a labelled box with no key.
3. **Communal arithmetic.** Who votes for whom? Joint electorates with reserved seats versus separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Europeans, and — this is the new sharpness of 1931 — for **Depressed Classes**.

Gandhi’s public line in London: Congress represents the nation including minorities and the Depressed Classes; separate electorates fragment the body politic; joint electorates with safeguards can protect without partitioning the voter roll. Ambedkar’s public line: the Depressed Classes are not a pious appendix of Hindu society; without a **separate political weapon**, upper-caste majorities will swallow reserved goodwill after the British leave. Muslim League voices treated Congress’s “we speak for all” as a majoritarian claim. Princes treated democracy talk as a threat to treaties.

**Teaching sentence:** Second RTC failed first on **who is a political community**, and only second on the shape of the federation.

## 3. Why the conference could not close

- No agreed communal table. MacDonald warned that if Indians did not settle seats among themselves, the British government would **impose an award**.
- Gandhi would not sign a scheme that treated Depressed Classes as a separate electorate carved out of the Hindu roll. Ambedkar would not accept a scheme that left Depressed Class voters dependent on caste-Hindu goodwill at the nomination and campaign stage.
- Economic depression in Britain reduced appetite for a generous transfer. Conservative pressure inside the National Government preferred safeguards and delay.
- News from India: the Pact was already leaking. Peasant no-tax pockets, ordinance habits of local officers, and distrust after the March 1931 executions made “truce spirit” thin.

Gandhi left London without a signed constitution. **Teaching sentence:** The Second RTC’s product is not a statute. Its product is a **permission slip for MacDonald to write the communal numbers himself**.

## 4. Return and the second Civil Disobedience

Gandhi landed back to a harder Raj. Irwin had been succeeded by **Lord Willingdon**. Willingdon’s reading of the Pact was narrower: truce was a pause, not a partnership. Congress leaders were rearrested in early 1932; Gandhi himself was imprisoned in **Yerawada** (Yeravda) Central Jail, Poona. Civil Disobedience was restarted and then hammered by ordinances. The movement of 1932 is real but more scattered than 1930: no second Dandi as national camera, more district repression, more fatigue.

Hold one clean contrast for Prelims options: **1930** is the year of the emblem (salt). **1932** is the year of the **franchise fight inside jail**.

# BLOCK B — Communal Award and Poona Pact (55 minutes)

## 1. Communal Award, 16 August 1932

Ramsay MacDonald announced the **Communal Award** while Gandhi sat in Yerawada. Learn it as a **seat-and-roll design**, not as a moral essay.

Core design, stated without drama:

- **Separate electorates** continued and extended for communities already treated as separate political units: Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans.
- **Depressed Classes** were given **separate electorates** of their own, **plus** the right to vote in the general (caste-Hindu) constituencies — a **double vote** in the Award’s original logic.
- Seats were also sketched for labour, commerce, and other special interests in the provincial scheme that would later sit inside the 1935 Act.
- The Award was about **provincial** legislatures first. Do not dump Lok Sabha 1950 numbers into 1932.

**Why “double vote” matters.** A separate Depressed Class roll elects its own legislators. The same voters also help elect general-seat legislators. Ambedkar saw this as compensation for centuries of exclusion: a guaranteed voice *and* a stake in the general house. Gandhi saw a permanent wall: once a community has its own roll, Hindu society is legally told that the Depressed Classes are not of it, and the national movement is told that India is a stack of electorates, not a people.

**Teaching sentence:** The Award does not invent communal electorates (1909 already did). It **adds the Depressed Classes as a separate electorate** and thereby forces Congress and caste-Hindu leadership to answer Ambedkar in public.

## 2. Gandhi’s fast and the moral theatre of Yerawada

Gandhi announced a **fast unto death** against separate electorates for the Depressed Classes — not against reserved seats as such, and not against separate electorates for Muslims, which he had long treated as a different historical file. That distinction is examinable. Critics then and now call it inconsistent. You must be able to state his claim: dividing Hindu society at the ballot is a spiritual amputation; Muslim separate electorates were already a settled, if unhappy, political fact.

The fast began **20 September 1932** in Yerawada. The country watched a jail cot become a negotiating table. Temple-entry pledges, upper-caste guilt meetings, and hurried talks in Poona and Bombay filled the week. Ambedkar was under crushing pressure: if Gandhi died, the Depressed Classes could be blamed by a grieving national public; if Ambedkar surrendered the separate roll without a hard substitute, his people could lose the only state-backed weapon they were being offered.

**Teaching sentence:** A fast in this setting is not private piety. It is **coercive moral force** used inside a franchise dispute. GS-IV must name the coercion as well as the sincerity.

## 3. Poona Pact, 24 September 1932

The settlement is an agreement **between** Gandhi’s side (caste-Hindu / Congress negotiators) and **Ambedkar** (with Depressed Class colleagues). The British government then revised the Award to match the Pact. Lock the date: **24 September 1932**.

What changed, in operational language:

1. **Separate electorates for Depressed Classes were dropped.**
2. **Reserved seats** for Depressed Classes in provincial legislatures were **increased** above the Award’s separate-electorate numbers — the price of giving up a private roll.
3. Election to those reserved seats would be by the **joint electorate** (general voters including Depressed Class voters), but through a **two-stage method** in the original Pact design: a primary election in which only Depressed Class voters would panel a shortlist of candidates, and a second election in which the general joint electorate would choose among that panel.
4. A similar reserved-seat logic was accepted for the Central legislature.
5. A sum of money and a time horizon for educational / social uplift appeared as accompanying promises — important as intention, weak as an enforceable statute in 1932.

Numbers you should treat as **order-of-magnitude memory**, not as a substitute for the principle: the Pact raised Depressed Class reserved provincial seats substantially (commonly taught as from the Award’s about **71** separate seats to about **148** reserved seats in the provincial total then under discussion). If a Prelims option fights over 71 versus 148, the direction is what saves you: **more reserved seats, no separate roll**.

**Teaching sentence:** Poona Pact trades the **separate roll** for a **larger reserved block inside a joint roll**, with a primary-panel device meant to keep candidate choice from being wholly captured by caste-Hindu majorities.

## 4. Who gained, who paid, what remained raw

- Ambedkar signed under the shadow of a dying body and a hostile street. He later wrote of the Pact as a wound. That later judgement is part of the syllabus of honesty, not an optional footnote.
- Congress could say the nation was not legally split at the Hindu–Depressed Class line.
- Depressed Class voters still needed literacy, money, and protection to use even a joint roll. A reserved seat without land, school, and safety is a chair in a locked room.
- Muslim, Sikh, and European separate electorates **remained**. Poona Pact is not a general repeal of communal electorates. Options that say “Poona Pact ended separate electorates in India” are false.
- Temple-entry and well-access campaigns after the Pact (the “Harijan” turn in Gandhi’s language) are social-reform follow-through. They do not replace the franchise text. Learn both tracks: **legal seat design** and **social boycott**.

Karachi 1931 had spoken of rights of the poor. Poona 1932 shows that “the poor” is not one political subject. Caste is a **representation technology**, not only a ritual insult.

# BLOCK C — Third RTC, White Paper, and the road toward 1935 (35 minutes)

## 1. Third Round Table Conference

**November–December 1932.** Congress is again absent (leaders in jail; movement under ordinance). Attendance is thinner. The conference works over details that the first two had left as sketches: federal finance, state entry, special responsibilities, provincial autonomy as the deliverable the British would actually risk.

**Teaching sentence:** Third RTC is a **drafting room without the mass party**. It can refine clauses. It cannot manufacture consent.

## 2. White Paper (1933) and the next statute

The British government issued a **White Paper** in 1933 gathering conference results into a scheme: provincial autonomy; a proposed all-India federation if enough princes acceded; a Centre still armoured on defence, foreign affairs, and safeguards. A **Joint Parliamentary Committee** then chewed the White Paper. The statute that comes out is the **Government of India Act 1935** — that Act is a later file. Today you only need the conveyor belt:

**Simon → RTCs → Award/Pact → White Paper → Joint Committee → 1935 Act.**

Do not start memorising 1935’s provincial lists tonight. Own the belt.

## 3. Civil Disobedience wound down

By 1933–34 Gandhi and Congress leadership faced empty treasuries, tired districts, and a new temptation: **council entry under the coming provincial reforms**. Official withdrawal of mass Civil Disobedience is commonly dated to **1934**. Individual satyagraha flickers. Constructive work (village, khadi, Harijan funds) is offered as the moral remainder. The next political machine will be the **1937 provincial elections**. Park that date. Do not invade it today.

# BLOCK D — Geography, economy, ethics, current-affairs-explained (45 minutes)

## 1. Geography you must be able to place tonight

1. **London, St James’s / parliamentary conference rooms** — Second RTC, autumn 1931; Third RTC, winter 1932. The constitution is still being spoken in a foreign capital.
2. **Poona / Pune** — Yerawada Jail west of the old city; the fast; the Pact named for the town where the bargain is struck.
3. **Bombay** — commercial and reform public that funds and argues the week of the fast; Depressed Class and caste-Hindu deputations move through this port-city.
4. **Ahmedabad–Sabarmati** — the ashram Gandhi has left behind; constructive workers telegraph the fast into Gujarat’s older satyagraha districts.
5. **The Deccan lava plateau around Pune** — rain-shadow agriculture, caste-layered village, and a jail that sits inside a Maratha-Peshwa memory city now used as a colonial prison. Physical setting is not decoration: western India’s social structure is exactly where Ambedkar’s critique of caste as everyday power is sharpest.
6. **Depressed Class geographies in the franchise debate** — not one map colour. Maharashtra’s Mahar country, Tamil non-Brahmin and Adi-Dravida politics, Malayali social ferment, Bengal’s Namasudra tracts, north Indian Chamar and related labour belts. A single all-India “Harijan map” is a Congress convenience. For Mains, name at least two regional textures.
7. **Princely India as a hole in the voter map.** Federation talk in London is also a map of **places with no British-Indian roll at all**. Representation ethics in 1931–32 is incomplete if you only argue Hindu versus Depressed Class and forget the states’ subjects.

**Physical teaching sentence:** 1932’s decisive geography is **a Deccan jail plus a London table**. The salt coast of 1930 is no longer the camera.

## 2. Economy — seats, schools, and who can use a vote

- A reserved seat is a **political asset**. It can be used to bargain land laws, factory inspectorates, school budgets, and water in the first ward. Without those, it is symbolism.
- Depressed Class poverty in 1932 is not only income poverty. It is **exclusion from well, school, temple, and village office** — a restriction on human capital formation. Franchise design that ignores the well and the school will reproduce the same elite in a reserved nameplate.
- The slump of the early 1930s cuts cash incomes. Agrarian castes under price crash and Depressed Class labour under collapsing wages do not have identical interests. “Peasant India” in a Mains paragraph that erases this split is a weak paragraph.
- Temple-entry and tank-access campaigns have an economic edge: common water and common road lower the daily tax of humiliation and raise labour mobility.
- Separate electorates create **vote-bank incentives** for colonial and later party machines. Joint electorates with reservations create a different incentive: candidates must seek a crossover slice. Neither design abolishes caste as a labour market.

## 3. Ethics — GS-IV from this file

1. **Who may speak for a wounded group.** Gandhi claims a trustee’s voice over Hindu society including those it oppresses. Ambedkar denies the trustee. The ethical test is not warmth. It is **whether the represented can fire the representative**.
2. **Fast as persuasion versus fast as hostage-taking.** Intent can be purification; effect can be blackmail of a weaker bargainer. Write both sentences. UPSC ethics marks the candidate who can hold a paradox without dissolving it.
3. **Means inside a good cause.** Congress’s national unity aim does not automatically justify erasing a separate roll that the intended beneficiaries want. Unity is a value. So is consent of the least powerful.
4. **Consistency of communal principle.** If separate electorates are wrong because they split a people, why accept them for some communities and die to prevent them for another? A fair answer distinguishes history (1909 already done) from principle (you still owe a reason). A slick answer pretends there is no tension.
5. **Aftermath duty.** After a Pact, the stronger party owes **delivery**: schools, wells, protection from riot and boycott. Signing plus sermons is not the whole duty.

## 4. Current-affairs-explained — living structures

- **Reserved seats versus separate rolls today.** India’s Constitution after 1950 keeps **joint electorates with reserved seats** for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes — the Poona direction, not the Award’s separate-roll direction. When contemporary debates flare over sub-quotas, women-in-reservation, or delimitation, ask 1932’s question: is the design giving a **weapon** or a **label**?
- **Who sits when the text is written.** Second RTC with a single Congress delegate and a full set of communal chairs is a warning about constituent processes that look inclusive on the nameplate and stacked in the voting rule.
- **Moral protest that changes bargaining power.** Hunger strikes in jails, student fasts, and celebrity pledges still move state calendars. Judge them as Gandhi–Ambedkar week must be judged: sincerity, asymmetry of cost, and the fine print after the photograph.
- **Data and dignity.** Award and Pact are both number machines. Modern welfare targeting, caste census arguments, and creamy-layer fights are the same family: **can you count a disadvantage without freezing it into a permanent political wall?**
- **Federal absence.** Princely subjects in 1931 have a cousin in any present-day group that is discussed in a capital and cannot vote on the discussants. Representation without jurisdiction is a speech, not a right.

# BLOCK E — CSAT with worked solutions (40 minutes)

Q1. A provincial house has 200 seats. The Award gives community D 18 separate seats. The Pact replaces that with reserved seats equal to 12% of the house. How many extra seats does D gain, and what is the percentage increase on the Award number?

**Worked solution.**  
Pact seats = 12% of 200 = 24.  
Extra = 24 − 18 = 6.  
Increase on Award base = (6 / 18) × 100 = **33⅓%**.  
Answer: **6 seats; 33⅓%**.

Q2. In a reserved constituency the primary panel has 4 Depressed Class candidates. Only 45% of that community’s 8,000 voters turn out, and they split votes in the ratio 5 : 4 : 3 : 2 among the four. How many votes does the leading candidate get?

**Worked solution.**  
Turnout = 0.45 × 8000 = 3600.  
Parts = 5+4+3+2 = 14.  
Leading share = (5/14) × 3600 = **1285 5/7** votes.

Q3. A joint-roll seat has 50,000 voters, of whom 16% are Depressed Class. If 60% of Depressed Class voters and 40% of the others turn out, what share of actual votes is Depressed Class?

**Worked solution.**  
DC voters = 0.16 × 50000 = 8000; others = 42000.  
DC votes = 0.60 × 8000 = 4800.  
Other votes = 0.40 × 42000 = 16800.  
Total votes = 21600.  
Share = 4800 / 21600 = **2/9 ≈ 22.22%**.

Q4. Statements:  
A. All separate electorates are communal devices.  
B. Some reserved seats are filled by joint electorates.  
Conclusions:  
I. Some communal devices are reserved seats.  
II. No reserved seat is a separate electorate.

**Worked solution.**  
A places separate electorates inside communal devices. B says some reserved seats use joint rolls — it does not identify reserved seats with separate electorates.  
I does not follow. II does not follow.  
**Neither follows.**

Q5. A negotiator left Bombay on 1 August 1931 (day 1 of a 21-day sea passage), spent 96 days in Europe, and returned on a 21-day sea passage. On what date does he touch Bombay again?

**Worked solution.**  
Arrives London 21 August. Europe from 22 August for 96 days ends 25 November. Return from 26 November for 21 days → **16 December 1931** under this convention.

Q6. Three communities allotted seats 5 : 3 : 2 in a 150-seat block. C’s seats rise by 50% of original, taken equally from A and B. New ratio A : B : C?

**Worked solution.**  
A 75, B 45, C 30. C becomes 45 (+15). A 67.5, B 37.5, C 45 → **9 : 5 : 6**.

Q7. A=1 … Z=26. Sum of letters in POONA?

**Worked solution.**  
16+15+15+14+1 = **61**.

Q8. Fast from morning 20 September to evening 24 September 1932. How many midnights crossed?

**Worked solution.**  
**4 midnights.**

Q9. 40% joint rolls, 35% separate, rest undecided. One-fifth of undecided shift to joint. New joint percentage?

**Worked solution.**  
Undecided 25%; shift 5%; new joint **45%**.

Q10. Premises: Some Pact signatories are reformers. All reformers oppose separate rolls. Conclusions: I. Some Pact signatories oppose separate rolls. II. All Pact signatories are reformers.

**Worked solution.**  
**Only I.**

# BLOCK F — Active recall + model cores (25 minutes)

**Q1.** Why was Gandhi the sole Congress delegate at the Second RTC?  
**Core:** To prevent the British from splitting moderate constitutionalists from the mass party; one authorised mouth for the movement.

**Q2.** On what issue did the Second RTC primarily break?  
**Core:** Who is a political community — communal and Depressed Class representation — more than federation adjectives.

**Q3.** Date and author of the Communal Award?  
**Core:** 16 August 1932; Ramsay MacDonald.

**Q4.** What new thing did the Award do to Depressed Class franchise?  
**Core:** Separate electorates plus a double-vote logic.

**Q5.** Against which part of the Award did Gandhi fast?  
**Core:** Separate electorates for Depressed Classes, not reserved seats as such, not Muslim separate electorates.

**Q6.** Date and bargain of the Poona Pact?  
**Core:** 24 September 1932; drop separate DC rolls; raise reserved seats on a joint roll with a primary panel.

**Q7.** Did the Poona Pact abolish all separate electorates in India?  
**Core:** No. Muslim, Sikh, European and some other communal separate electorates remained.

**Q8.** Where was Gandhi imprisoned during the fast?  
**Core:** Yerawada Central Jail, Poona.

**Q9.** Constitutional conveyor after the Pact?  
**Core:** Third RTC → White Paper 1933 → Joint Parliamentary Committee → Government of India Act 1935.

**Q10.** One ethical tension to write without flattening.  
**Core:** Fast as sincere resistance to a legal partition of Hindu society and as coercive pressure on Ambedkar.
